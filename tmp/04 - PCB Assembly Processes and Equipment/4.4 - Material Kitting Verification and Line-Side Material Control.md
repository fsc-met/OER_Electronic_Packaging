# 4.4 - Material Kitting, Verification, and Line-Side Material Control

Section 4.3 established the controlled manufacturing job package. The next question is physical:

> **Do the actual boards, components, and process materials at the line match the job that has been released?**

This is the purpose of **material kitting and line-side material control**.

Kitting is sometimes treated as a warehouse activity, but in PCB assembly it is also a manufacturing-control activity. A line can have the correct machine program and still build the wrong product if:

- the wrong component reel is loaded;
- the correct part is loaded into the wrong feeder location;
- an unapproved substitute is used;
- the wrong PCB revision reaches the line;
- expired or incorrectly stored solder paste is issued;
- moisture-sensitive material is no longer in the required handling state;
- material from incompatible soldering systems is mixed.

The practical objective is simple:

> **Correct material, correct identity, correct condition, correct location, correct job.**

---

## 4.4.1 - A Kit Is More Than a Box of Parts

A **manufacturing kit** is the set of boards, components, process materials, and related identifiers prepared for a specific production job or production stage.

A kit may include:

- bare PCBs or panels;
- tape-and-reel components;
- trays;
- tubes/sticks;
- bulk-supplied components where applicable;
- solder paste or other joining materials;
- stencils, fixtures, or other job-specific tooling references;
- labels, travelers, or electronic records that identify the job and material status.

The exact kit structure depends on the product and factory.

The important manufacturing question is not merely:

> **Are the parts present?**

It is:

> **Are the correct approved parts present, in sufficient quantity, in acceptable condition, and correctly assigned for this job?**

---

## 4.4.2 - Internal Part Number, Manufacturer Part Number, and Physical Container

Material control becomes easier when three different identities are kept separate.

### Internal part number

The **internal part number** is the organization's own controlled identifier for the required item.

For example:

`RES-10K-0603-1PCT`

This identifier may point to one or more approved manufacturer part numbers.

### Manufacturer part number

The **manufacturer part number (MPN)** identifies the specific manufacturer's component.

Different approved MPNs may satisfy the same internal part requirement if the product documentation allows them.

An operator should not decide independently that a visually similar part is an acceptable substitute.

### Physical container identity

The actual material reaches production in a physical package such as:

- reel;
- tray;
- tube/stick;
- bag;
- box;
- jar or cartridge.

The container label may include some combination of:

- manufacturer;
- manufacturer part number;
- internal part number;
- quantity;
- lot/batch;
- date code;
- barcode or 2D code;
- moisture-sensitivity/status information;
- expiration/use-by information where applicable.

Not every supplier uses the same label format. The line-side control system therefore needs a reliable way to connect the **physical container** to the **approved job requirement**.

## 4.4.3 - Quantity Verification Is Part of Readiness

A correct component is still not useful if the job runs out of material halfway through the build.

For a component used multiple times on each assembly, the minimum placement quantity is:

$$
N_{\text{placement}} = N_{\text{build}} \times n_{\text{per assembly}}
$$

where:

- $N_{\text{placement}}$ = number of component placements required;
- $N_{\text{build}}$ = number of assemblies to be built;
- $n_{\text{per assembly}}$ = quantity of that component used on each assembly.

For example, if a board uses four identical resistors and the job quantity is 120 boards:

$$
N_{\text{placement}} = 120 \times 4 = 480
$$

At least 480 successful placements are required.

The actual quantity prepared or issued for the line may need to be greater because of:

- feeder setup;
- leader/trailer material;
- component loss;
- machine rejects;
- sampling;
- approved attrition allowance.

The allowance is **not universal**. It depends on the component packaging, equipment, process, product, and company policy.

> **Practical rule:** Separate the calculated build requirement from the approved setup/attrition allowance. Do not hide unexplained excess quantity inside the BOM quantity.

## 4.4.4 - A Simplified Kit Verification and Feeder-Setup Table

The following table shows the type of check a production team may perform before or during line setup.

| Job requirement | Physical material / setup | Verification question | Example reaction if not verified |
|---|---|---|---|
| Internal part number | reel/tray/tube label | Does this container map to the required controlled part? | Hold material; resolve identity |
| Approved MPN / alternate | supplier/manufacturer label | Is this exact part approved for this job/revision? | Do not substitute without authorization |
| Required quantity | container quantity / inventory record | Is enough usable material available for the planned build and approved allowance? | Replenish or adjust the authorized build plan |
| Lot/date-code requirement | container label / controlled record | Does the material meet any applicable lot/date-code restriction? | Hold and obtain disposition |
| Feeder assignment | feeder ID and slot/location | Is the correct material loaded into the correct feeder/location? | Stop setup and correct the assignment |
| Polarity/orientation requirement | package marking / feeder presentation | Will the machine receive the part in the required orientation? | Correct presentation/setup before placement |
| Moisture-sensitive status | material label / exposure-status record | Is the component still in an allowed handling state? | Follow the applicable moisture-control procedure |
| Solder-paste identity | paste label | Is the correct alloy/flux product and lot being used? | Hold; do not mix or substitute |
| Solder-paste status | use-by/storage/exposure record | Is the paste within the controlled use condition? | Follow product/process disposition rules |
| Bare-PCB identity | board/panel marking and traveler | Is the correct PCB part number/revision/lot at the line? | Hold the boards and resolve the mismatch |
| Bare-PCB condition | packaging and visible board condition | Is the board appropriately stored, clean, undamaged, and suitable for the planned assembly step? | Hold for evaluation |
| Return-to-stock status | partial reel/tray/tube label | Can the remaining material still be positively identified and its relevant status preserved? | Do not return as anonymous/uncontrolled stock |

This is a simplified instructional table. Actual factories may use scanners, software interlocks, paper signoffs, electronic travelers, or combinations of these methods.

## 4.4.5 - Barcode and 2D-Code Verification Reduce Manual Identity Errors

Manual label reading is useful, but automated identity checks can reduce opportunities for wrong-part loading.

A typical verification sequence may be:

1. select the authorized production job;
2. scan or enter the required material identity;
3. scan the physical reel/tray/tube;
4. scan or identify the feeder;
5. verify the assigned feeder slot/location;
6. allow loading only when the required identities agree.

Some placement systems and factory software can interlock or block setup/run authorization when the scanned part, feeder identity/location, and active job do not match.

Other factories use a more manual process.

The engineering principle is the same:

> **The physical part container should be positively matched to the job and to its intended line-side location before normal placement begins.**

A barcode does not make the information correct by itself. If the source database, label, alternate-part authorization, or feeder map is wrong, a scan can still confirm the wrong information. Automated verification reduces one class of error; it does not replace configuration control.

A useful mental model is:

> **Scan verifies identity against controlled data; it does not create the approval.**

## 4.4.6 - Feeder Assignment Is Part of Material Control

For automated placement, the component container is only part of the setup.

The line must also establish:

- feeder identity;
- feeder type/size compatibility;
- assigned feeder slot or location;
- correct component presentation;
- remaining usable quantity;
- any required polarity/orientation relationship.

A correct reel in the wrong feeder position can be as serious as a wrong reel.

Consider a simple example:

- the active program expects `R25`-family components from feeder location 12;
- the correct reel is present at the line;
- the reel is accidentally loaded into feeder location 13;
- another valid component is loaded into location 12.

Both reels may be approved parts for the product, yet the setup is still wrong because the **part-to-feeder map** does not match the active program.

Section 4.16 develops feeder hardware and supply formats. Section 4.20 develops placement setup and first-article verification. Here, the focus is only the material-identity relationship.

## 4.4.7 - Lot and Date Code Are Identification Information, Not Automatic Accept/Reject Rules

Component labels often include a **lot code**, **batch code**, **date code**, or similar production identifier.

These can help manufacturing organizations:

- distinguish material groups;
- apply customer/product restrictions;
- maintain controlled records;
- investigate a later problem;
- separate suspect material when required.

However:

> **A date code by itself is not a universal expiration limit.**

Whether an older component is usable depends on the applicable component specification, storage history, solderability/condition, customer requirements, and the organization's controlled rules.

The same caution applies to lot mixing. Some products allow material from multiple approved lots; others restrict it.

Section 4.4 therefore teaches **lot/date-code awareness and verification**, not a universal age limit.

Formal incoming-quality evaluation, solderability verification, supplier quality, containment, and material disposition belong in Chapter 5.

## 4.4.8 - Solder Paste Must Be Controlled by Product, Lot, and Condition

Solder paste is a process material, not a generic consumable.

A line-side paste check may include:

- product name/type;
- solder alloy;
- flux formulation;
- lot/batch;
- use-by or expiration information;
- required storage condition;
- time/condition after removal from controlled storage;
- container status.

Manufacturers publish product-specific storage and handling instructions. These instructions can differ among paste formulations and packaging types.

For this reason, a generic rule such as "all solder paste lasts six months" is not reliable enough for production control.

> **Use the current product data sheet and the approved manufacturing instruction for the specific paste.**

Two pastes can use the same nominal solder alloy and still have different:

- flux chemistry;
- rheology;
- storage requirement;
- stencil life;
- reflow behavior.

They should not be treated as automatically interchangeable.

Detailed solder-paste composition and behavior are developed in Section 4.8. Printing-environment and process variables are developed in Sections 4.11-4.13.

## 4.4.9 - Moisture-Sensitive Component Status Must Be Known Before Issue

A reel or tray can have the correct part number and still be unsuitable for immediate use if its moisture-handling status is not controlled.

At the kitting/line-side level, verify that the applicable moisture/reflow-sensitive status is known, such as:

- moisture-sensitivity classification/labeling where applicable;
- sealed/dry-pack condition;
- exposure-status record after opening;
- dry-storage or bake status when required by the controlled process.

This section does not teach floor-life calculations or baking procedures.

Section 4.5 develops **ESD control and moisture-sensitive device handling** in detail.

The line-side principle is simply:

> **Do not issue moisture-sensitive material into a reflow process when its required handling status is unknown.**

---

## 4.4.10 - Bare PCBs Are Also Controlled Material

The bare PCB is sometimes overlooked because it is not supplied in a component reel.

Before assembly, verify as applicable:

- PCB part number;
- revision;
- fabrication lot/batch;
- panel configuration;
- board quantity;
- packaging/storage condition;
- surface-finish appearance;
- cleanliness;
- obvious physical damage;
- moisture/storage status when controlled by the board/product requirements.

A board with the correct artwork but the wrong revision is the wrong material for the job.

Likewise, a correctly identified board may require evaluation or controlled conditioning if handling or storage has degraded its solderable surfaces or allowed moisture uptake beyond the applicable assembly requirement.

Current printed-board handling standards specifically address protection against:

- contamination;
- physical damage;
- solderability degradation;
- moisture uptake.

The exact packaging, storage, dry-out, and shelf-life requirements depend on the board construction, surface finish, assembly process, supplier information, and product requirements.

> **Do not invent a universal bare-board shelf life or bake schedule at the production line. Follow the applicable controlled requirements.**

---

## 4.4.11 - Alternate and Substitute Parts Require Authorization

A common production pressure occurs when the required material is not available.

Suppose the BOM requires one approved 10 kΩ, 1% resistor, but the stockroom has another 10 kΩ, 1% resistor in the same package.

It may look equivalent.

It may even fit the footprint.

That does not automatically make it an approved substitute.

Differences can include:

- voltage rating;
- power rating;
- temperature coefficient;
- termination finish;
- qualification level;
- package dimensions/tolerances;
- approved sourcing status;
- customer restrictions.

The substitute may be used only if it is already an approved alternate or is authorized through the applicable engineering/material-control process.

> **Production shortage is not engineering authorization.**

---

## 4.4.12 - Keep Incompatible Material Systems Separated

Some manufacturing operations support both tin-lead and lead-free products.

When such processes coexist, material identity must remain clear.

Controls may be needed for:

- solder paste;
- bar solder;
- solder wire;
- flux;
- process-specific tools or consumables;
- product labeling;
- storage locations;
- work instructions.

The required degree of physical/tooling segregation depends on the product and process requirements.

The basic error to prevent is straightforward:

> **Do not allow an operator to select a soldering material only because the container looks familiar or the alloy behaves similarly.**

Material identity, process authorization, and product requirements control the selection.

---

## 4.4.13 - Line-Side Control Continues After Setup

Material control does not end when the first board starts moving.

During production, the line may require:

- reel replenishment;
- feeder replacement;
- tray change;
- splice or reel changeover;
- replacement paste container;
- partial-kit transfer;
- material removal after a job stops or changes.

Each event creates another opportunity for a mismatch.

A good line-side practice keeps only clearly identified, authorized material associated with the active job and controls any replenishment before it is used.

When material leaves the line, the remaining container should preserve the information needed for its next decision, as applicable:

- material identity;
- quantity;
- lot/date code;
- moisture/exposure status;
- use-by/expiration status;
- job or process restrictions.

A partially used reel without reliable identity or required status information should not quietly return to general inventory.

The detailed enterprise genealogy of every lot, serial number, machine event, and inspection result belongs in Chapter 5. Section 4.4 focuses on the **minimum material identity/status information needed to prevent wrong or uncontrolled material from entering the active assembly process**.

---

## 4.4.14 - A Practical Line-Readiness Check

Before normal production begins, ask:

| Check | Question |
|---|---|
| **Job identity** | Is the correct product/revision active? |
| **PCB identity** | Are the correct PCB/panel revision and lot present? |
| **Component identity** | Do the physical reel/tray/tube labels map to the approved job requirements? |
| **Alternate control** | Are any substitutes explicitly approved? |
| **Quantity** | Is enough usable material available for the build and approved allowance? |
| **Feeder assignment** | Is each required component mapped to the correct feeder/location? |
| **Material status** | Are lot/date-code, moisture, storage, and expiration/use-by conditions acceptable where required? |
| **Process material** | Is the correct solder paste/alloy/flux product and lot available? |
| **Segregation** | Are incompatible material systems clearly separated where required? |
| **Return control** | Can unused/partial material be returned without losing identity or relevant status? |

If one answer is unknown, the correct action is to hold the affected setup/material until the information is resolved.

Section 4.5 next develops two material-handling risks that require their own controls before components enter the assembly process: **electrostatic discharge (ESD)** and **moisture sensitivity**.

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- kitting is a manufacturing-control activity, not merely a warehouse task;
- internal part number, manufacturer part number, and physical container identity are related but different;
- material verification must cover identity, quantity, condition/status, and line-side assignment;
- wrong-part prevention depends on preserving the identity chain from the approved job requirement to the reel/tray/tube and feeder location;
- lot/date-code information supports controlled decisions but does not create a universal age limit;
- solder paste must be controlled by the specific product, alloy/flux system, lot, storage condition, and use status;
- moisture-sensitive component status must be known before issue, while detailed MSL handling belongs in Section 4.5;
- bare PCBs require revision/lot and condition/storage verification just like other production materials;
- substitutes require authorization;
- tin-lead and lead-free materials must remain clearly controlled where both processes coexist;
- material control continues during replenishment and return-to-stock, not just at initial setup;
- full product genealogy and manufacturing-data analytics belong later in Chapter 5.

### Technical corrections / qualifications

1. **No universal solder-paste shelf life.**
   The primary lecture includes representative generic storage/shelf-life numbers. Current manufacturer guidance varies by paste formulation and packaging, so Section 4.4 retains the control concept but directs the reader to the specific approved product data/instruction.

2. **No universal component age/date-code rejection limit.**
   Date/lot codes are identifiers and may support restrictions or investigations; acceptability depends on controlled product/supplier requirements and condition.

3. **Bare-board handling standard updated.**
   Older reference material may point to IPC-1601. Current IPC revision information shows IPC-1601 has been superseded by IPC-1602.

4. **MSL details intentionally deferred.**
   Section 4.4 verifies that moisture status is known before issue. Section 4.5 owns MSL, floor life, dry storage, and baking awareness.

5. **Traceability boundary preserved.**
   Section 4.4 uses scanning/records for setup verification and wrong-part prevention. Full genealogy, cross-process analytics, and MES data use belong in Chapter 5.

6. **SnPb/Pb-free segregation is requirement dependent.**
   The public section teaches identity/segregation awareness without claiming one universal physical-separation scheme.
