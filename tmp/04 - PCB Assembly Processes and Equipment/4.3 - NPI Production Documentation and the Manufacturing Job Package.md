# 4.3 - NPI, Production Documentation, and the Manufacturing Job Package

Section 4.2 showed how boards, materials, information, and process evidence move through a PCB assembly factory. Before any of those machines can be set up correctly, the factory must know exactly **what product is being built, which revision is authorized, how the job should be processed, and what evidence is required before the build can continue**.

That transition is a central part of **New Product Introduction (NPI)**.

NPI is the coordinated work used to move a new or revised product from released design information into a controlled manufacturing process. The exact organization and terminology vary among companies, but the practical objective is consistent:

> **Turn the released product definition into a buildable, verifiable manufacturing job without losing configuration control.**

A manufacturing organization may call the controlled information for one build a **job package**, **work package**, **manufacturing package**, **shop packet**, or another local name. This book uses **manufacturing job package** as a general term.

The job package is not one file. It is the controlled collection of information needed to prepare, execute, verify, and record the build.

---

## 4.3.1 - From Design Release to Manufacturing Readiness

Chapter 3 ended with a controlled design release. That release establishes the intended product, but production still has work to do before the first board enters a machine.

Typical NPI/manufacturing-readiness activities include:

1. identify the released product part number and revision;
2. receive and review the applicable manufacturing data;
3. resolve missing, conflicting, or ambiguous information;
4. prepare product-specific tooling and machine programs;
5. prepare work instructions and the process route;
6. confirm material, stencil, fixture, and equipment readiness;
7. define the required inspection/test checks;
8. perform an initial build or first-article/setup verification;
9. resolve issues through controlled correction or engineering change;
10. release the manufacturing process for the next authorized stage.

These steps are not one universal corporate procedure. A prototype shop may use a compact version. A high-volume or regulated product may require a much more formal approval process.

The important point is that **design release and production readiness are related, but they are not identical milestones**.

---

## 4.3.2 - What Is in a Manufacturing Job Package?

A useful way to organize manufacturing information is by the question it answers.

| Information group | Representative items | Main question answered |
|---|---|---|
| **Product definition** | BOM, approved manufacturer information, assembly drawing, placement/centroid data, PCB/panel identification, polarity/orientation information | **What are we building?** |
| **Process definition** | stencil/paste information, work instructions, process traveler/router, machine programs/recipes, special handling notes | **How should this job be built?** |
| **Verification definition** | first-article/setup checks, inspection-plan information, test-plan awareness, required signoffs/hold points | **What must be checked before the job continues?** |
| **Configuration identity** | product part number, revision, engineering-change status, controlled document/program identifiers | **Which approved version applies?** |

Not every company uses the same documents or names. Some information may be contained in an integrated digital manufacturing-data package rather than separate files. The engineering requirement is not that every factory use the same format; it is that the information be **complete enough, internally consistent, controlled, and usable by the actual manufacturing process**.

A missing file is not the only failure mode. A job package can look complete and still be wrong if one item is stale, belongs to another revision, or cannot be interpreted correctly by the equipment or people who must use it.

## 4.3.3 - BOM, Assembly Drawing, and Placement Data Do Different Jobs

Three items are especially easy to confuse because all describe the same assembly from different viewpoints.

### Bill of Materials (BOM)

The **BOM** identifies the parts required by the assembly.

Depending on the organization's system, a BOM may include:

- internal part number;
- manufacturer part number;
- approved manufacturer/source information;
- description;
- quantity;
- reference designators;
- package or other identifying information;
- do-not-populate / not-fitted status where applicable;
- approved alternate information where controlled.

The BOM answers primarily:

> **Which items belong in this assembly?**

Detailed reel, tray, lot, date-code, barcode, and line-side verification is developed in Section 4.4.

### Assembly Drawing

The **assembly drawing** gives a human-readable view of how the product is assembled.

It may show:

- component reference designators;
- board side;
- component outlines;
- polarity or pin-1 orientation;
- connector orientation;
- mechanical hardware;
- special assembly notes;
- product/assembly part number and revision.

The assembly drawing answers primarily:

> **What should the assembled board look like, and what orientation or special assembly information must a person understand?**

It is not a substitute for machine-readable placement data.

### Component-Placement / Centroid / Pick-and-Place Data

Placement data provide machine-usable or machine-importable information describing where components belong.

Typical fields may include:

- reference designator;
- X coordinate;
- Y coordinate;
- rotation;
- top/bottom side;
- package or footprint identifier.

A representative data row might communicate:

| RefDes | X | Y | Rotation | Side | Package |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---|
| U3 | 54.20 mm | 31.80 mm | 90° | Top | QFN-32 |

This is only an instructional example. Actual file structure, coordinate origin, units, rotation convention, side convention, and column names vary among CAD/CAM systems and assembly equipment. A value such as `90°` is meaningful only after the applicable coordinate and rotation conventions are known.

The placement-data question is:

> **Where and at what orientation should the placement system put this component?**

Section 4.18 develops placement-program coordinates and component-library information in detail.

> **Practical distinction:** The BOM tells the factory **what parts** are required. The assembly drawing helps people understand **what the assembly should look like**. Placement data tell manufacturing equipment **where components are located and oriented**.

## 4.3.4 - Approved Manufacturer and Source Information

A part description such as `10 kΩ resistor` or `5 V regulator` is usually not enough to control production.

The manufacturing organization must know which actual component configurations are approved.

Depending on the company, this information may be controlled through:

- manufacturer part numbers;
- an **Approved Manufacturer List (AML)**;
- an **Approved Vendor List (AVL)**;
- purchasing/source-control records;
- approved alternates within the BOM or enterprise system.

The exact names and ownership vary. An approved-manufacturer control identifies **which manufacturer part numbers are permitted**. An approved supplier/vendor control may separately identify **which purchasing sources are permitted**. These controls are related, but company terminology and ownership vary.

Section 4.4 develops the line-side part-verification problem. The point here is simpler:

> **The job package must link the assembly requirement to the approved physical component configuration.**

An operator or buyer should not have to decide independently that two parts "look equivalent."

## 4.3.5 - Work Instructions, Travelers, and Machine Recipes Are Not the Same Thing

A manufacturing job often uses several layers of process information.

### Work Instruction

A **work instruction** tells a person how to perform or verify a defined task.

Examples include:

- how to load a particular fixture;
- where a special component must be installed;
- which orientation mark must face a specified direction;
- which handling precaution applies;
- what setup verification must be completed.

A good work instruction should be specific enough that the intended task does not depend on an operator remembering an undocumented local practice.

### Process Traveler / Router

A **traveler** or **router** is commonly used to control or record the route of the job through manufacturing. Some organizations distinguish these terms; others combine their functions.

It may identify:

- required operation sequence;
- work center or station;
- hold points;
- required signoffs;
- quantity/status;
- inspection/test steps;
- rework or deviation status where applicable.

It answers:

> **Where does this job go next, and what must be completed before it moves on?**

Paper travelers still exist, but the same function may be implemented electronically.

### Machine Program / Process Recipe

A **machine program** or **process recipe** contains or references equipment-specific information used to execute a manufacturing operation.

Examples include:

- stencil-printer product recipe;
- pick-and-place program;
- reflow-oven recipe;
- selective-solder program;
- inspection program.

A machine program is normally **derived from and linked to the released product/process requirements**, but it is not necessarily a direct design output.

For example, two factories may build the same released PCBA on different placement machines or reflow ovens. The equipment-specific program names and settings may differ even though both processes produce the same approved product.

> **Key distinction:** Product revision and machine-program revision are related through configuration control, but they do not have to use the same revision label.

Later sections show what the individual machine programs contain and how their settings affect the process.

## 4.3.6 - Revision Consistency Is a Production Requirement

Chapter 3 introduced release-package consistency from the design side. The production-side consequence is immediate:

> **Do not set up the line until the job package points to one controlled, approved product configuration.**

Consider this example:

- product: `Controller Assembly A-100`;
- released product revision: `Rev D`;
- BOM: current `Rev D`;
- assembly drawing: current `Rev D`;
- placement data: regenerated for `Rev D`;
- placement machine program: still based on the previous `Rev C` placement export.

The machine program may load successfully. The feeder setup may look normal. The first board may even look reasonable.

The job is still not ready to run.

> **Do not repair a configuration problem with an undocumented shop-floor workaround.**

The correct action is to stop the release/setup process, determine which information is stale, regenerate or update the affected controlled manufacturing output, verify the result, and document the approved configuration before production continues.

This does **not** mean every file or recipe must display the same revision letter. Manufacturing artifacts may have their own controlled identifiers. What matters is that each current artifact is traceably linked to the same approved product configuration.

### Engineering changes during NPI

NPI frequently uncovers issues that require a change.

Examples:

- a component orientation is wrong;
- a stencil aperture needs an approved change;
- a fixture interferes with a component;
- a work instruction is ambiguous;
- the intended process route cannot be executed as released.

A manufacturing engineer should distinguish between:

- an adjustment that is already allowed inside an approved process window;
- a correction to a manufacturing document/program;
- a change to the controlled product definition.

If the controlled product definition changes, the change must go through the organization's applicable engineering/configuration-control process before the revised definition is used for production. The terms **engineering change order (ECO)**, **engineering change notice (ECN)**, or similar names are common, but company usage varies.

The general rule is:

> **Change the controlled source -> regenerate or update affected manufacturing outputs -> recheck the affected setup -> document the approved status.**

## 4.3.7 - Prototype, Pilot Build, and First Article

NPI commonly uses small or staged builds before routine production. The terminology is not perfectly standardized across companies, so the engineering purpose matters more than the label.

### Prototype Build

A **prototype build** is primarily used to learn about the product and prove basic design/manufacturing feasibility.

It may involve:

- small quantity;
- manual operations;
- temporary tooling;
- non-production equipment;
- extra engineering support.

A successful prototype proves useful things, but it does not automatically prove that the intended production process is ready.

### Pilot Build

A **pilot build** generally moves closer to the intended production method.

It may be used to evaluate:

- manufacturing sequence;
- tooling;
- machine programs;
- work instructions;
- material presentation;
- inspection/test flow;
- cycle-time or capacity concerns;
- repeatability of the intended production method.

The size and formality of a pilot build depend on the product and organization.

### First Article / First-Board Verification

In PCB assembly practice, **first article** or **first-board verification** may refer to the first completed board or small set of boards from a new setup, revision, or change that receives extra verification before the broader run continues.

Checks may include:

- correct product/revision;
- correct components;
- correct polarity/orientation;
- placement correctness;
- visible workmanship;
- required inspection/test results;
- setup/program verification.

The exact first-article process is organization- and product-dependent. A factory's first-board check should not automatically be equated with a formal industry-specific First Article Inspection requirement used in regulated sectors. In this chapter, the term is used at the practical PCB-assembly level unless a more formal requirement is explicitly stated.

### Process Qualification / Approval Awareness

Some products, customers, industries, or organizations require a more formal demonstration that a process, recipe, material, machine, or change is suitable for its intended production use and can produce the required result under defined conditions.

That may involve:

- defined process conditions;
- documented trial or qualification build;
- measured output;
- inspection/test evidence;
- engineering/quality approval.

Not every prototype, setup, or process adjustment requires the same level of qualification. The required approval level comes from the applicable product, customer, regulatory, and internal manufacturing requirements.

## 4.3.8 - A Practical Job-Readiness Check

Before a new or revised PCB assembly job is allowed to run, a technician or engineer should be able to answer the following questions from controlled information.

| Readiness question | Evidence to check |
|---|---|
| **What exact product are we building?** | assembly part number and released revision |
| **Which components are approved?** | current BOM and approved manufacturer/source information |
| **What should the assembly look like?** | assembly drawing, polarity/orientation information, special notes |
| **Where should components be placed?** | current placement/centroid data and approved placement program |
| **What process route and instructions apply?** | traveler/router, work instructions, process notes |
| **What tooling/material setup applies?** | PCB/panel identification, stencil/tooling identity, paste/material instructions |
| **Which machine programs/recipes are approved?** | controlled printer, placement, reflow, selective-solder, or other applicable program identifiers |
| **What must be checked before the build continues?** | first-article/setup checks, inspection plan, test-plan awareness, hold/reaction instructions |
| **Are any changes or exceptions still open?** | engineering-change/deviation status and required approvals |

If one of these answers cannot be established, the correct response is not to guess. The job should be held at the appropriate point until the missing or conflicting information is resolved.

> **Production-readiness principle:** The factory should be able to identify **what to build, how to build it, how to verify it, and which controlled revision applies** before normal production begins.

Section 4.4 now moves from the information package to the physical material side of readiness: kitting, component verification, line-side material control, and traceability.

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- NPI is the controlled transition from released design information to a manufacturable job;
- a manufacturing job package is a collection of controlled information, not one universal file;
- the BOM, assembly drawing, and placement data describe different aspects of the same assembly;
- work instructions, travelers/routers, and machine programs/recipes serve different manufacturing functions;
- product revisions and machine-program revisions must be controlled and traceably linked, but they do not have to share the same revision label;
- prototype, pilot, first-article/setup verification, and process qualification serve different purposes and vary in formality by organization/product;
- stale or conflicting manufacturing information should stop the job rather than be resolved by undocumented shop-floor guesswork;
- production readiness requires clear answers to what will be built, how it will be built, what will be verified, and which approved configuration applies.

### Important technical clarifications added during drafting

1. **Job package is not one universal file.**
   The file set and naming depend on organization, product, equipment, and manufacturing system.

2. **Placement data are not universal in format.**
   Origin, units, side convention, and rotation convention vary. The numerical row in the public section is explicitly illustrative.

3. **Product revision is not machine-program revision.**
   Different controlled manufacturing artifacts may have their own identifiers/revisions while remaining traceably linked to the same approved product configuration.

4. **First article is context dependent.**
   The section uses factory-floor first-board/first-article language without claiming equivalence to a formal regulated-industry FAI standard.

5. **Prototype success is not production-process qualification.**
   A prototype may use temporary/manual methods and therefore cannot automatically prove production readiness.

6. **Process qualification/approval is requirement dependent.**
   The section does not imply that every setup or parameter adjustment requires the same formal qualification.
