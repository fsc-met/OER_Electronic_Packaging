# Figure 3.8.4 Suggested Content Specification

## Figure title for authoring/reference only
**Acute Copper Geometry and Historical Acid-Trap Context**

## Instructional purpose
Teach the historically important “acid trap” idea while correcting the oversimplified claim that every 90° trace bend is automatically a modern fabrication defect.

## Central teaching message
The historical acid-trap concern is mainly associated with **acute interior copper geometry** and narrow wedges, not every 90° trace bend. Modern processes reduce the historical risk, but avoiding unnecessarily sharp interior copper wedges remains good DFM practice.

## Overall layout
Use a **three-panel or three-column comparison**:

1. **Acute interior wedge / historical concern**
2. **Clean 90° bend**
3. **45° or rounded robust style**

This comparison is important and should be visually balanced.

## Required objects and labels
### Panel A
- same-net copper branch or turn that creates an **acute interior angle** or narrow wedge/pocket;
- label: **Acute interior copper angle**;
- optional note: **Historical acid-trap concern**.

### Panel B
- simple right-angle (90°) trace bend;
- label: **Clean 90° bend**;
- optional note: **Not automatically a fabrication defect**.

### Panel C
- 45° mitered bend or rounded bend;
- label: **45° or rounded routing style**;
- optional note: **Common robust practice**.

## Required technical relationships
- Panel A must clearly show an **interior acute wedge**, not merely any corner.
- Panel B must be a clean, ordinary 90° bend with adequate width.
- Panel C must be shown as an alternative style, not as the only acceptable geometry.
- The figure must preserve the historical concept while making the modern correction explicit.

## Spatial / geometry requirements
- Keep line widths consistent enough for visual comparison.
- All three examples should be shown at similar scale.
- Use enough white space to keep labels separate from the copper geometry.
- If leader lines are used, avoid crossing them unnecessarily.

## Scope boundaries
- Do not turn this into a signal-integrity corner discussion.
- Do not discuss impedance discontinuity or return-path behavior here; that belongs in Chapter 5.
- Do not claim that 45° routing is universally mandatory.
- Keep the figure focused on fabrication-oriented geometry.

## Technical-verification basis
Backed by the verified Section 3.8 teaching inventory:
- traditional instruction often warned against 90° bends because of “acid traps”;
- a more accurate modern formulation is that **acute interior angles** are the main historical DFM concern;
- modern chemistry and process control reduce the historical issue substantially;
- 45° or rounded routing remains a common robust style, but a clean 90° bend is not automatically defective.
