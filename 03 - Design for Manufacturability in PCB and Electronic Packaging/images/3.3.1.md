## Suggested Content for Figure 3.3.1

**Figure title for authoring/reference only:**  
`Different Sources of DFM Rules and Requirements`

### Instructional purpose

Give readers a compact visual model for **how different kinds of DFM values are interpreted and used in a design decision**.

The figure should help readers distinguish:

- what the product **must satisfy**;
- what the selected manufacturing process **can achieve**;
- what engineering chooses as a **preferred/routine DFM target**;
- what the CAD system is configured to **check automatically**;
- what is only **instructional/example data**.

The figure must also show that:

- standards/guidance and internal DFM rules can influence the decision chain;
- a CAD rule gets its meaning from an engineering basis rather than from the software itself;
- instructional values are useful for learning but are not automatically release rules;
- the path is a practical interpretation model, not a universal legal/organizational authority hierarchy.

This figure is the main conceptual visual in Section 3.3 and should make later numerical DFM sections easier to interpret.

### Central teaching message

> **Before using a DFM value, identify what role it plays: requirement, process capability, preferred engineering target, configured CAD rule, or instructional/example data.**

The primary design-decision logic should read:

> **What must be satisfied? → What can the selected process achieve? → What target will engineering use? → What will CAD check? → Verify the completed design**

Instructional/example data should be shown **outside the release-design chain** so readers do not mistake classroom values for universal engineering limits.

## Required technical relationships

The final figure must communicate all of the following:

1. **Governing requirements** answer what the product must satisfy.
2. **Process capability** answers what a defined selected manufacturing/verification process can achieve.
3. **Preferred DFM targets** are engineering choices that normally provide practical margin relative to relevant capability boundaries.
4. **EDA design rules** are configured implementations of engineering decisions.
5. **DRC/CAD checking does not create the authority behind the rule.**
6. **Verification** checks the completed design against the intended basis.
7. **Applicable standards/guidance** may influence requirements or design targets depending on the document and how it applies.
8. **Internal DFM rules** can formalize engineering choices and feed CAD rule sets.
9. **Instructional/example values** are bounded learning data and do not automatically become release rules.
10. Similar-looking numerical values can have very different engineering roles.
11. The diagram is a practical interpretation model, **not a universal authority ladder**.

The figure must **not** imply:

- every standard is automatically a governing requirement;
- supplier capability is a product acceptance requirement;
- preferred target is always numerically larger than capability;
- CAD/DRC determines engineering authority;
- internal DFM rules override governing product/safety requirements;
- instructional values can be copied directly into a release design without verification;
- one supplier's capability is universal industry capability.

---

## Numerical-direction caution

Do **not** show a generic numeric inequality such as:

`minimum capability < preferred target`

as the central figure logic.

Reason:

- for minimum-spacing rules, more margin often means a larger value;
- for maximum-size or maximum-tolerance limits, the numerical direction may reverse;
- the section deliberately teaches role and applicability rather than a universal mathematical hierarchy.

Use wording such as:

- `Provides practical process margin`
- `Moves routine design away from the relevant process boundary`

rather than a directional inequality.

### Main-arrow logic

Preferred arrows:

`Governing requirement`
→
`Process capability`
→
`Preferred DFM target`
→
`EDA design rule`
→
`Verification`

This is a **practical design-decision sequence**, not a claim that process capability can modify or supersede a governing requirement.

If needed, add a small note under the main path:

- `Reconcile applicable requirements and process capability before selecting the design target.`

This note can prevent misinterpretation of the simple left-to-right sequence.
