# 3.8 - Trace and Copper Geometry

Section 3.7 focused on holes, vias, and the copper margin around them. This section shifts attention to the **shape of the copper itself**.

The central DFM idea is simple:

> **Copper features must remain wide enough and well connected after normal fabrication variation.**

That one idea explains most of the checks in this section:

- the narrowest part of a trace matters;
- a trace should connect cleanly to a pad or via;
- thin copper slivers and narrow webs are fragile;
- unnecessarily sharp interior copper geometry should be avoided;
- copper near routed or scored edges needs process margin.

Detailed current capacity, impedance, return paths, and high-speed routing are treated later in Chapter 5.

---

## 3.8.1 - Fabrication Changes Nominal Copper Geometry

The copper drawn in CAD is **nominal geometry**. The finished copper on the board is not an exact mathematical copy.

Important contributors include:

- imaging resolution;
- lateral etching at copper edges;
- outer-layer plating;
- copper thickness;
- local copper distribution;
- process compensation and inspection.

The practical result is that a feature designed very close to the minimum manufacturing capability has less remaining margin for normal process variation.

A useful current engineering scale for ordinary rigid boards using about **1 oz copper** is:

| Copper feature | Useful DFM reference |
|---|---:|
| Common mainstream minimum trace width | **0.10 mm (4 mil)** |
| Comfortable planning value when density allows | **0.15 mm (6 mil)** or larger |

Useful unit relationships are:

$$
4\ \text{mil} = 0.1016\ \text{mm} \approx 0.10\ \text{mm}
$$

and

$$
6\ \text{mil} = 0.1524\ \text{mm} \approx 0.15\ \text{mm}
$$

Some modern processes can go tighter than 0.10 mm, but that should be treated as **process-dependent capability**, not as the default planning value.

Copper thickness also matters. Heavier copper generally requires wider minimum traces and spaces because more copper must be removed during etching.

A **±20%** finished trace-width tolerance is a useful current supplier example for understanding process variation.

For example, a nominal **0.10 mm** trace with a ±20% width tolerance corresponds to:

$$
0.10(1-0.20)=0.08\ \text{mm}
$$

to

$$
0.10(1+0.20)=0.12\ \text{mm}
$$

This does **not** mean every 0.10 mm trace will finish at 0.08 mm, and it does not mean the designer should manually add 20% to every trace. It is simply a reminder that a limiting feature has less process margin.

> **Minimum capability asks, “Can this feature be made?” Preferred geometry asks, “Can it be made repeatedly with useful margin?”**

---

## 3.8.2 - Check the Narrowest Local Copper Width

A trace is only as manufacturable as its **narrowest local width**.

A route may be assigned a nominal width of 0.20 mm, but if it necks down to 0.10 mm between two nearby features, the local 0.10 mm region becomes the controlling geometry.

A **neck-down** is a short region where a trace becomes narrower than the surrounding route. Neck-downs are sometimes necessary to escape a dense package or pass through a constrained area.

For example:

- a 0.20 mm trace that necks to **0.15 mm** still has useful manufacturing margin relative to the 0.10 mm reference;
- the same trace necked to **0.10 mm** is operating at the representative minimum;
- a local feature below the selected fabricator's minimum is not rescued by the fact that most of the trace is wider.

The practical review is therefore:

> **Check the minimum local width, not only the nominal route width.**

Abrupt width changes can also create small copper wedges. When space permits, a short taper or smooth transition can provide cleaner geometry.

## 3.8.3 - Make Pad and Via Connections Robust

A trace should enter a pad or via in a way that leaves a clear, intentional copper connection.

Less robust entry geometry can create:

- a thin copper neck beside the pad or via;
- a tangential or grazing connection with little junction area;
- an acute same-net copper wedge;
- a connection that becomes vulnerable if the drilled hole shifts.

A centered radial entry into a round pad or via is usually easy to manufacture and inspect.

A **teardrop** adds copper between a narrow trace and a wider pad, via, or track. It can:

- smooth the width transition;
- add local copper area;
- increase local mechanical robustness;
- reduce the chance that drill misregistration disconnects the trace from a drilled pad or via.

Teardrops are **optional**, not mandatory. In dense layouts, the added copper must still obey neighboring spacing and mask rules.

## 3.8.4 - Remove Fragile Copper Slivers and Narrow Webs

A **copper sliver** is a long, thin, or needle-like copper remnant.

A **narrow copper web** is a required copper connection or region that becomes locally thin, often because a void, cutout, clearance, or nearby feature removes most of the surrounding copper.

These shapes are different, but the DFM question is the same:

> **Does this local copper feature have enough width to survive the selected fabrication process?**

Very narrow copper can be:

- over-etched or partially removed;
- detached during processing;
- difficult to inspect consistently;
- a source of unintended conductive debris if it separates.

The design response is straightforward:

- if the narrow remnant serves **no useful function**, remove it;
- if the copper **must remain**, widen it until its local width satisfies the applicable fabrication rule.

## 3.8.5 - Avoid Unnecessarily Sharp Interior Copper Geometry

Older PCB design guidance often stated that **90° traces create acid traps**.

That statement is too broad for modern rigid-board fabrication.

The historical concern is better associated with an **acute interior copper angle** or narrow wedge. Such geometry can create a small, process-sensitive copper region during etching.

Modern imaging, spray etching, chemistry control, and process compensation have reduced the historical risk substantially.

The practical distinction is:

- an **acute interior wedge** remains poor DFM geometry because it creates a small, process-sensitive copper feature;
- a clean **90° trace bend is not automatically a fabrication defect**;
- 45° or rounded routing remains a common robust style, but it is not a universal requirement.

## 3.8.6 - Keep Copper Clear of Routed and Scored Boundaries

Section 3.5 introduced **copper-to-board-edge clearance** as a different rule from component-to-edge spacing.

The DFM reason is that routing, scoring, and profile registration have finite positional tolerance. Copper too close to a routed or scored boundary can be:

- clipped by the profile operation;
- exposed at the edge;
- left with insufficient laminate support.

Representative current supplier values illustrate the scale:

| Edge condition | Representative copper-clearance example |
|---|---:|
| Routed board edge, outer copper | about **0.25 mm** |
| Routed board edge, inner copper | about **0.40 mm** |
| V-score / V-cut region | about **0.45 mm** |

These are **representative process examples**, not universal requirements.

A routed slot or internal cutout is also a machined board boundary. Measure copper clearance to the **actual routed boundary**, not merely to a convenient CAD centerline.

Special edge features are different. Castellations, edge plating, edge connectors, and intentionally exposed copper require process-specific rules.

## 3.8.7 - Applied Trace and Copper Review

The table below applies the main ideas of this section. Final release should use the selected fabricator's actual capability together with the product's electrical requirements.

| Design observation | DFM interpretation | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| 0.15 mm trace on a routine 1 oz rigid board | Comfortable geometry in the representative rule set | Keep unless electrical requirements require another width |
| 0.10 mm trace on a routine 1 oz rigid board | At a common mainstream minimum | Use when density requires it and the selected fabricator supports it |
| 0.20 mm trace necked locally to 0.10 mm | Local neck controls manufacturing margin | Widen the neck if space permits |
| 0.10 mm trace used with heavier copper | May be below the ordinary capability for that copper thickness | Check the selected fabricator and widen if required |
| Trace grazes the edge of a drilled pad or via | Small local copper junction | Recenter the entry or use a verified teardrop if useful |
| Copper pour leaves a needle-like isolated remnant | Fragile, nonfunctional copper sliver | Remove or reshape the pour |
| Required copper web around a cutout is too narrow | Local copper feature has insufficient process margin | Increase the web or move the copper/cutout boundary |
| Acute same-net copper wedge at a branch | Process-sensitive local geometry | Reshape to remove the acute interior wedge |
| Clean 90° trace bend with adequate width | Not automatically a fabrication failure | Keep or change for routing/electrical reasons; do not reject solely as an “acid trap” |
| Outer copper 0.20 mm from a routed edge | Below the 0.25 mm representative example | Move copper inward or verify a fabricator that supports it |
| Copper 0.30 mm from a V-score line | Below the 0.45 mm representative example | Increase the keepout or use a different depanelization approach |

A practical review sequence is:

> **1. Check the narrowest local width -> 2. Inspect pad/via entries -> 3. Remove slivers and acute wedges -> 4. Check routed/scored boundaries -> 5. Verify the fabricator's copper capability.**

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- finished copper differs slightly from nominal CAD geometry, so limiting features have less process margin;
- **0.10 mm (4 mil)** is a useful common minimum reference for mainstream 1 oz rigid-board traces, while **0.15 mm (6 mil)** or larger gives more margin when density allows;
- the **narrowest local width** controls manufacturability;
- trace-to-pad and trace-to-via connections should leave clear local copper;
- teardrops can add local margin, but they are optional;
- nonfunctional copper slivers should be removed, while required narrow webs should be widened;
- the historical acid-trap concern is mainly about **acute interior copper geometry**, not every 90° bend;
- copper near routed, cut, or scored boundaries must follow the selected process capability.

Section 3.9 next examines **solder mask**, where copper geometry must be combined with mask registration capability, opening size, and minimum mask-web requirements.

### Fresh technical verification

Verification date: 2026-09-13.

#### Trace-width capability and copper thickness

Current PCBWay capability documentation publishes:

- minimum trace = **0.10 mm / 4 mil** for standard capability;
- a recommendation to use **above 0.15 mm / 6 mil** when possible for lower cost;
- heavier copper requires wider trace/space values.

Current JLCPCB capability documentation publishes:

- 1 oz:
  - 1- and 2-layer: **0.10 / 0.10 mm** minimum width/spacing;
  - multilayer: **0.09 / 0.09 mm** minimum width/spacing;
- 2 oz:
  - 2-layer: **0.16 / 0.16 mm**;
  - multilayer: **0.15 / 0.15 mm**;
- trace-width tolerance: **±20%**.

Current Eurocircuits guidance identifies **0.150 mm / 6 mil** as a typical standard track width and explains that copper thickness, imaging, etching, and copper balance influence final track width.

Disposition:

- public section retains **0.10 mm** as a useful common mainstream minimum for 1 oz rigid work;
- **0.15 mm** is used as a comfortable planning value;
- **0.09 mm** is identified only as a tighter modern capability;
- 2 oz is described with a qualified **0.15-0.20 mm** engineering scale rather than one universal number.

#### Finished trace-width variation

JLCPCB currently publishes **±20%** trace-width tolerance.

Eurocircuits also describes an acceptable 20% width/isolation tolerance under its manufacturing/acceptance guidance.

Disposition:

- the public `0.10 mm -> 0.08-0.12 mm` example is numerically correct;
- it is explicitly framed as tolerance awareness, not a rule to manually resize every trace.

#### Copper slivers and narrow webs

Eurocircuits copper-layer guidance explicitly warns about:

- small unconnected copper areas;
- narrow copper webs;
- copper slivers;

and states that the narrow feature must satisfy the applicable minimum track-width criterion.

Disposition:

- no separate universal sliver-width number is invented;
- the public section connects required narrow webs to the applicable minimum copper-feature rule.

#### Teardrops

Current KiCad PCB Editor documentation states that teardrops:

- add extra copper to smooth transitions between tracks and pads/vias/other tracks;
- increase mechanical robustness;
- reduce the risk that a misaligned drill hole disconnects a track from a drilled pad or via.

Disposition:

- teardrops are presented as useful optional geometry rather than a universal requirement;
- spacing/mask consequences remain subject to the surrounding DFM rules.

#### Acid-trap / corner nuance

The instructional material uses the traditional rule `avoid 90° corners`.

Current DFM guidance continues to flag **acute copper angles** as a manufacturing concern, while current Altium guidance notes that modern low-viscosity etchants and process control have largely mitigated the historical acid-trap problem.

Disposition:

- preserve the teaching purpose;
- correct the overstatement;
- do not classify every 90° trace bend as a fabrication defect;
- keep high-speed electrical corner behavior for Chapter 5.

#### Copper near edges and cutouts

Current Eurocircuits guidance gives representative values:

- routed board edge to outer copper: **0.25 mm**;
- routed board edge to inner copper: **0.40 mm**;
- V-score copper clearance: **0.45 mm**.

Current PCBWay capability also publishes approximately **0.25 mm** line-to-board-edge distance for standard CNC milling.

Disposition:

- retain these as representative current process examples;
- state explicitly that the selected fabricator and depanelization method control the actual value.
