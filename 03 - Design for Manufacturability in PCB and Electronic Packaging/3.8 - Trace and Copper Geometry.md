# 3.8 - Trace and Copper Geometry

Section 3.7 focused on holes, vias, and the copper margin around them. Section 3.8 keeps the DFM focus on the **copper geometry itself**: trace width, local necks, pad and via entry, narrow copper remnants, corners, and copper near routed or scored edges.

Chapter 2 introduced how copper patterns are imaged, plated where applicable, and etched. The design question here is:

> **Will every local copper feature remain manufacturable after normal imaging, etching, plating, and registration variation are considered?**

The main checks are:

- trace width and local minimum width;
- narrow necks and abrupt width changes;
- pad/via entry geometry;
- teardrops where they provide useful margin;
- copper slivers and narrow webs;
- acute interior copper geometry;
- copper near board edges, slots, and cutouts;
- copper thickness and etching capability.

This section focuses on **producibility**. Detailed current capacity, impedance, return-path behavior, and high-speed routing belong in Chapter 5.

---

## 3.8.1 - Trace Width Is a Local Manufacturing Constraint

A trace is only as manufacturable as its **narrowest local copper width**.

A route may be assigned a nominal width of 0.20 mm, but if it necks down to 0.10 mm between two features, the local 0.10 mm region is the geometry that must survive imaging and etching.

For ordinary rigid boards using about **1 oz copper**, a useful current engineering scale is:

| Copper feature | Useful DFM reference |
|---|---:|
| Common mainstream minimum trace width | **0.10 mm (4 mil)** |
| Comfortable planning value when density allows | **0.15 mm (6 mil)** or larger |
| Tighter modern multilayer capability | approximately **0.09 mm (3.5 mil)** in some processes |

Useful unit relationships are:

$$
4\ \text{mil} = 0.1016\ \text{mm} \approx 0.10\ \text{mm}
$$

and

$$
6\ \text{mil} = 0.1524\ \text{mm} \approx 0.15\ \text{mm}
$$

These are **manufacturing-scale references**, not universal design requirements.

Copper thickness matters. Published 2 oz rigid-board capabilities commonly require wider trace/space geometry than 1 oz copper; values around **0.15-0.20 mm** are a useful current engineering scale for tighter 2 oz work, depending on layer and fabricator.

> **Do not choose trace width from routing convenience alone. Use the electrical requirement together with the selected fabricator's copper-thickness and feature-size capability.**

## 3.8.2 - Narrow Necks and Abrupt Width Changes

A **neck-down** is a short region where a trace becomes narrower than the surrounding route.

Neck-downs are sometimes necessary to escape a dense package or pass between nearby features. The DFM problem is not that every width change is wrong. The problem is creating an unnecessarily small local feature with little process margin.

For example:

- a 0.20 mm trace that intentionally necks to **0.15 mm** remains above the common 0.10 mm minimum reference;
- the same trace necked to **0.10 mm** is now operating at the representative minimum;
- a local feature below the selected fabricator's minimum is not rescued by the fact that most of the trace is wider.

Abrupt transitions can also create small copper corners or wedges. When space permits, a short taper or otherwise smooth transition can provide a cleaner, more robust shape.

The practical review is:

> **Check the minimum local width, not just the nominal route width.**

Electrical current capacity and impedance effects of a neck-down are important but are treated later.

## 3.8.3 - Pad and Via Entry Geometry

A trace should enter a pad or via in a way that leaves a clear, intentional copper connection.

Poor entry geometry can create:

- a very narrow copper neck beside the pad or via;
- an acute wedge where two same-net copper shapes meet;
- a tangential or grazing connection that has little remaining copper if the drilled hole shifts;
- an abrupt width transition that is more fragile than necessary.

A centered radial entry into a round pad or via is usually easy to interpret and manufacture.

A **teardrop** adds copper between a narrow trace and a wider pad, via, or track. Its purpose is to smooth the width transition and add local copper area.

Modern PCB tools can generate teardrops automatically. They can be useful because they:

- increase local mechanical robustness;
- add copper around a trace-to-pad or trace-to-via junction;
- reduce the chance that drill misregistration disconnects a trace from a drilled pad or via.

Teardrops are **not mandatory on every PCB**. On dense fine-pitch layouts, extra copper can reduce neighboring clearance, so the teardrop itself must obey the applicable spacing and mask rules.

## 3.8.4 - Copper Slivers and Narrow Webs

A **copper sliver** is a long, thin, or needle-like copper remnant that is difficult to manufacture robustly.

Slivers can occur when:

- two polygon boundaries converge;
- a copper pour wraps tightly around a cutout or void;
- a narrow isolated copper island remains after a pour is filled;
- a plane necks down between clearances;
- a small wedge is created beside a pad, via, or trace.

Very narrow copper can be:

- over-etched;
- partially removed;
- detached during processing;
- difficult to inspect consistently;
- a source of unintended conductive debris if it separates.

A useful DFM rule is:

> **If a narrow copper web must remain, its minimum local width should meet the applicable minimum copper-feature rule for that layer and copper thickness.**

If a small isolated copper island serves no electrical, thermal, shielding, or manufacturing purpose, removing it is normally more robust than keeping a fragile remnant.

## 3.8.5 - Acute Copper Geometry and the Historical "Acid Trap"

Older PCB design guidance often warned that **right-angle traces create acid traps**.

That statement is too broad for modern rigid-board fabrication.

The historical concern is better described as an **acute interior copper angle** or narrow wedge in which process chemistry can remain locally concentrated during etching. Historically, such geometry was associated with additional local copper removal and possible trace narrowing or open circuits.

Modern imaging, spray-etching, chemistry control, and process compensation have reduced this concern substantially.

The important distinction is:

- an **acute interior angle** or narrow same-net wedge remains poor DFM geometry because it creates a small, process-sensitive copper feature;
- a clean **90° trace bend is not automatically a modern fabrication defect**;
- 45° or rounded routing remains a common robust layout style, but it should not be justified by claiming that every right-angle corner will trap etchant.

## 3.8.6 - Copper Near Board Edges, Slots, and Cutouts

Section 3.5 introduced **copper-to-board-edge clearance** as a different rule from component-to-edge spacing.

The DFM reason is that routing, scoring, and profile registration have finite positional tolerance. Copper too close to an edge can be:

- exposed after routing;
- clipped by the profile operation;
- left with insufficient laminate support;
- affected differently on outer and inner layers.

Representative current supplier values illustrate the scale:

| Edge condition | Representative copper-clearance example |
|---|---:|
| Routed board edge, outer copper | about **0.25 mm** |
| Routed board edge, inner copper | about **0.40 mm** |
| V-score / V-cut region | about **0.45 mm** |

These are **examples of current fabrication capability**, not universal design values.

A routed slot or internal cutout should be treated as a machined board edge. Measure copper clearance to the **actual routed boundary**, not simply to a convenient centerline in the CAD drawing.

Special edge features are different. Castellations, edge plating, edge connectors, and intentionally exposed copper require process-specific fabrication rules and should not be evaluated with an ordinary no-copper edge rule.

## 3.8.7 - Etching Variation, Copper Thickness, and Finished Trace Width

CAD geometry is nominal geometry. The finished copper feature is not an exact mathematical copy.

Important contributors include:

- photoresist or direct-imaging resolution;
- lateral etching at conductor edges;
- outer-layer plating;
- copper thickness;
- local copper distribution;
- process compensation and inspection.

Current supplier and fabricator guidance commonly uses approximately **±20%** as a useful finished trace-width tolerance scale.

For example, a nominal **0.10 mm** trace with a ±20% width tolerance corresponds to:

$$
0.10(1-0.20)=0.08\ \text{mm}
$$

to

$$
0.10(1+0.20)=0.12\ \text{mm}
$$

This does **not** mean every 0.10 mm trace will finish at 0.08 mm, nor does it mean the designer should manually add 20% to every trace. It means that a feature drawn at a limiting capability has less remaining margin for normal process variation.

Thicker copper generally requires wider minimum traces and spaces because more copper must be removed during etching and outer-layer plating changes the final conductor geometry.

> **Minimum capability answers "can this feature be made?" Preferred geometry asks "can it be made repeatedly with useful margin?"**

## 3.8.8 - Applied Trace and Copper Review

The table below uses the representative values in this section for engineering judgment. Final release should use the selected fabricator's actual capability and the product's electrical requirements.

| Design observation | DFM interpretation | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| 0.15 mm trace on a routine 1 oz rigid board | Comfortable mainstream geometry in the representative rule set | Keep unless electrical requirements require another width |
| 0.10 mm trace on a routine 1 oz rigid board | At a common mainstream minimum | Accept only when density requires it and the selected fabricator supports it |
| 0.20 mm trace necked locally to 0.10 mm | Local neck controls the manufacturing margin | Widen the neck if space permits |
| 0.10 mm trace used with 2 oz copper | May be below ordinary published capability for the heavier copper | Check the selected fabricator; widen the feature if required |
| Trace grazes the edge of a drilled pad/via | Small local copper neck may disappear under drill/etch variation | Recenter the entry or add a verified teardrop |
| Copper pour leaves a needle-like isolated wedge | Fragile/nonfunctional copper sliver | Remove or reshape the pour |
| Required copper web around a cutout is thinner than the layer's minimum trace width | Local copper feature is below the fabrication rule | Increase the web or move the cutout/copper boundary |
| Acute same-net copper wedge at a pad or branch | Historical acid-trap / local-etch concern | Reshape to remove the acute interior pocket |
| Clean 90° trace bend with adequate width and spacing | Not automatically a fabrication failure | Keep or change for routing/electrical reasons; do not reject solely as an "acid trap" |
| Outer copper 0.20 mm from a routed edge | Below the 0.25 mm representative example | Move copper inward or verify a fabricator that supports the geometry |
| Copper 0.30 mm from a V-score line | Below the 0.45 mm representative example | Increase the keepout or use a different depanelization method |

A practical review sequence is:

> **Check minimum local width -> inspect pad/via entries -> remove slivers and acute wedges -> check edge/cutout relationships -> verify copper thickness and fabrication capability.**

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- trace manufacturability is controlled by the **narrowest local copper width**, not only the nominal route width;
- **0.10 mm (4 mil)** is a useful common minimum trace-width reference for mainstream 1 oz rigid boards, while **0.15 mm (6 mil)** or larger provides more process margin when density allows;
- heavier copper usually requires wider minimum copper geometry;
- unnecessary neck-downs and needle-like copper features reduce manufacturing margin;
- teardrops can strengthen trace-to-pad/via transitions and add drill-misregistration margin, but they are not mandatory everywhere;
- copper slivers and narrow webs should be removed or widened unless they serve a real function;
- the historical acid-trap concern is mainly about **acute interior copper geometry**, not every 90° bend;
- copper near routed, scored, or cut edges must follow the applicable fabrication process;
- finished copper width varies from nominal CAD geometry because imaging, etching, plating, and copper thickness all matter.

### Fresh technical verification

Verification date: 2026-09-13.

#### Trace-width capability and copper thickness

Current PCBWay capability documentation publishes:

- minimum trace = **0.10 mm / 4 mil** for standard capability;
- a recommendation to use **above 0.15 mm / 6 mil** when possible for lower cost;
- heavier copper requires wider trace/space values.

Current JLCPCB capability documentation publishes:

- 1 oz:
  - 1- and 2-layer: **0.10 / 0.10 mm** minimum width/spacing;
  - multilayer: **0.09 / 0.09 mm** minimum width/spacing;
- 2 oz:
  - 2-layer: **0.16 / 0.16 mm**;
  - multilayer: **0.15 / 0.15 mm**;
- trace-width tolerance: **±20%**.

Current Eurocircuits guidance identifies **0.150 mm / 6 mil** as a typical standard track width and explains that copper thickness, imaging, etching, and copper balance influence final track width.

Disposition:

- public section retains **0.10 mm** as a useful common mainstream minimum for 1 oz rigid work;
- **0.15 mm** is used as a comfortable planning value;
- **0.09 mm** is identified only as a tighter modern capability;
- 2 oz is described with a qualified **0.15-0.20 mm** engineering scale rather than one universal number.

#### Finished trace-width variation

JLCPCB currently publishes **±20%** trace-width tolerance.

Eurocircuits also describes an acceptable 20% width/isolation tolerance under its manufacturing/acceptance guidance.

Disposition:

- the public `0.10 mm -> 0.08-0.12 mm` example is numerically correct;
- it is explicitly framed as tolerance awareness, not a rule to manually resize every trace.

#### Copper slivers and narrow webs

Eurocircuits copper-layer guidance explicitly warns about:

- small unconnected copper areas;
- narrow copper webs;
- copper slivers;

and states that the narrow feature must satisfy the applicable minimum track-width criterion.

Disposition:

- no separate universal sliver-width number is invented;
- the public section connects required narrow webs to the applicable minimum copper-feature rule.

#### Teardrops

Current KiCad PCB Editor documentation states that teardrops:

- add extra copper to smooth transitions between tracks and pads/vias/other tracks;
- increase mechanical robustness;
- reduce the risk that a misaligned drill hole disconnects a track from a drilled pad or via.

Disposition:

- teardrops are presented as useful optional geometry rather than a universal requirement;
- spacing/mask consequences remain subject to the surrounding DFM rules.

#### Acid-trap / corner nuance

The instructional material uses the traditional rule `avoid 90° corners`.

Current DFM guidance continues to flag **acute copper angles** as a manufacturing concern, while current Altium guidance notes that modern low-viscosity etchants and process control have largely mitigated the historical acid-trap problem.

Disposition:

- preserve the teaching purpose;
- correct the overstatement;
- do not classify every 90° trace bend as a fabrication defect;
- keep high-speed electrical corner behavior for Chapter 5.

#### Copper near edges and cutouts

Current Eurocircuits guidance gives representative values:

- routed board edge to outer copper: **0.25 mm**;
- routed board edge to inner copper: **0.40 mm**;
- V-score copper clearance: **0.45 mm**.

Current PCBWay capability also publishes approximately **0.25 mm** line-to-board-edge distance for standard CNC milling.

Disposition:

- retain these as representative current process examples;
- state explicitly that the selected fabricator and depanelization method control the actual value.
