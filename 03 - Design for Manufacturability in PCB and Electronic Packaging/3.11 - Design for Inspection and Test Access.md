# 3.11 - Design for Inspection and Test Access

Section 3.10 focused on markings that help people identify components and interpret an assembled board. Useful markings are not enough, however, if the important solder joints cannot be seen or the electrical nodes cannot be reached.

Inspection and test access should therefore be considered **before the PCB is released**.

A useful design logic is:

> **What must be verified + feature visibility + electrical access + physical access -> inspection and test strategy**

The two ideas are related but not identical:

- **inspection access** asks whether an assembly feature can be observed well enough to check it;
- **test access** asks whether the required electrical node or function can be reached by the selected test method.

A board can have good visual access but poor electrical test access, or the opposite. Good DFM therefore asks **how each important feature or node will actually be verified after assembly**.

---

## 3.11.1 - Optical Inspection Requires a Usable Line of Sight

Manual visual inspection and automated optical inspection (AOI) depend on optical access to the feature being checked.

Optically accessible features may include:

- component presence and orientation;
- reference and polarity relationships;
- exposed leads;
- visible solder fillets;
- bridges or obvious solder deposits;
- misplaced or lifted components.

Whether a particular defect is detectable also depends on the inspection equipment, lighting, viewing angle, algorithm, and acceptance method.

The important DFM question is not simply whether a component is visible from directly above. The question is whether the selected inspection system has a usable view of the feature that matters.

A tall connector, capacitor, shield, or other component can obstruct the view of a nearby small component or solder joint. This is commonly described as **AOI shadowing**.

Modern AOI equipment may use multiple lighting directions, angled views, or three-dimensional measurement, so one tall component does not automatically make a nearby joint uninspectable. The actual component geometry and the intended inspection system control the result.

## 3.11.2 - Hidden Solder Joints Need a Different Inspection Strategy

Optical inspection cannot see through an opaque package body.

This matters especially for **bottom-terminated and area-array packages**.

Examples include:

- ball grid array (BGA) packages, where the solder balls are beneath the package;
- quad flat no-lead (QFN) packages, where the center thermal/ground pad and much of the termination geometry are underneath the package;
- other packages whose critical solder interfaces are hidden after assembly.

For these packages, X-ray inspection may be an appropriate or necessary part of the manufacturing-quality plan.

The DFM lesson is important:

> **Needing X-ray inspection is not automatically evidence of poor DFM.**

A package may be entirely appropriate for the electrical, thermal, mechanical, or size requirements of the product even though its solder joints cannot be inspected optically.

The design responsibility is to recognize this before release and make sure the manufacturing and quality plan includes an inspection method that can evaluate the required features.

A simple DFM comparison is:

| Assembly feature | Optical access | X-ray relevance | Design implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exposed gull-wing lead and solder fillet | Usually good if not obstructed | Usually not required for basic visibility | Preserve line of sight where optical inspection is planned |
| Small SMT joint beside a tall component | May be reduced by shadowing | Depends on the feature and process plan | Reposition parts or confirm the inspection system can view the joint |
| QFN perimeter/center solder regions | Some external evidence may be visible, but important underside regions are hidden | Often useful for hidden solder features | Plan the inspection method around the actual package construction |
| BGA solder balls | Hidden after assembly | Commonly appropriate | Do not treat X-ray requirement itself as a DFM failure |

Detailed defect acceptance, X-ray interpretation, and inspection-process operation belong in Chapter 4.

## 3.11.3 - Electrical Access and Physical Probe Access Are Different

A net can exist electrically in the schematic and PCB layout while still being difficult to reach physically after assembly.

For probe-based testing, the board needs an accessible conductive target such as:

- a dedicated test point;
- an exposed pad intentionally used for test;
- an accessible connector contact;
- another approved probeable feature.

A useful test point should remain accessible after all normal components, shields, connectors, and other hardware are installed.

Poor examples include:

- a test pad underneath a large component;
- a probe target blocked by a connector body or shield;
- a test point placed so close to a neighboring component that a probe cannot approach reliably;
- a test target covered by solder mask;
- a connector intended for testing that becomes inaccessible in the assembled product or fixture.

The design question is therefore:

> **Does the required electrical node also have a practical physical access path for the selected test method?**

## 3.11.4 - ICT and Flying-Probe Testing Need Physical Access

**In-circuit test (ICT)** commonly uses a fixture with many spring-loaded probes that contact selected test points or other approved conductive targets.

ICT is primarily a structural/component-level manufacturing test rather than a complete proof of final product function. It can access many nodes quickly, but the board layout must support the required physical contacts.

**Flying-probe testing** uses programmed movable probes rather than a dedicated fixed bed-of-nails fixture. It can be attractive for prototypes, lower-volume production, or designs that change frequently because dedicated fixture investment can be reduced.

However, flying probes still require reachable conductive targets and collision-free probe motion for the selected equipment.

The important distinction is:

> **Changing from ICT to flying probe changes the probing strategy, but it does not make a physically unreachable conductive target automatically reachable.**

A simplified comparison is:

| Method | Physical contact approach | Typical DFM concern |
|---|---|---|
| ICT | Many fixed fixture probes contact the board through a dedicated fixture | Sufficient accessible targets and fixture/probe clearance |
| Flying probe | Programmed movable probes contact selected conductive targets | Reachable targets and collision-free probe approach |
| Connector-based test | Test equipment connects through an installed connector | Connector must remain accessible and expose the required signals |

Actual probe sizes, keep-outs, fixture rules, and target dimensions are equipment- and supplier-dependent. Those values should come from the selected test process rather than from one universal PCB rule.

## 3.11.5 - Boundary Scan Can Reduce Some Physical-Probe Requirements

Some digital integrated circuits support **boundary-scan** test access through standardized on-chip test logic and a test access port.

Boundary scan can help test interconnections between compatible devices without placing a physical probe on every digital node.

This can be especially valuable when:

- package pitch is fine;
- many interconnects are hidden beneath packages;
- board area for dedicated test points is limited.

Boundary scan does not eliminate all test-access planning.

The design still needs:

- compatible devices and interconnect architecture;
- access to the required boundary-scan interface;
- appropriate test data and programming support;
- another strategy for nets and devices that are not covered by boundary scan.

At the awareness level used here, the main DFM lesson is:

> **Physical test points, probe-based test, connector-based test, and boundary scan are complementary design choices rather than interchangeable names for the same test method.**

Detailed boundary-scan implementation and test-program development are outside this chapter.

## 3.11.6 - Applied Inspection and Test-Access Review

The table below illustrates the type of decision expected during a pre-release DFM review.

| Design observation | DFM interpretation | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| Fine-pitch exposed leads have a clear view from the intended optical system | Optical inspection access is intentionally supported | Preserve the viewing geometry |
| Tall connector blocks the required view of nearby small solder joints | Optical inspection may be unreliable | Reposition parts or verify another viewing/inspection method |
| BGA is used and its solder balls are hidden after assembly | Hidden-joint package, not automatically poor DFM | Include an appropriate X-ray inspection strategy |
| QFN center thermal pad is important to assembly quality | Critical solder region is hidden optically | Include an inspection method capable of evaluating the hidden region |
| Important net has an exposed, reachable test point | Electrical and physical test access are both provided | Verify the target against the selected probe/fixture rules |
| Important net exists but its only exposed pad is underneath a component | Electrical node exists but physical probe access is poor | Add intentional access or select a non-probe test strategy |
| Test point is exposed but blocked by a tall connector | Conductive target exists but probe approach is restricted | Relocate the target or confirm the selected test hardware can reach it |
| Low-volume board changes frequently | Dedicated ICT fixture may not be the preferred first choice | Consider flying probe or another appropriate test strategy |
| Compatible digital devices support boundary scan | Some interconnects may be testable without individual physical probes | Plan the boundary-scan interface and coverage deliberately |

A practical review sequence is:

> **Identify what must be verified -> determine whether the feature is visible -> select the inspection method -> identify the electrical nodes that require test -> verify physical access -> confirm the selected test strategy.**

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- inspection access and electrical test access are related but different design responsibilities;
- optical inspection depends on actual line of sight, so component height and placement can create shadowing;
- hidden solder joints in BGA, QFN, and similar packages may legitimately require X-ray inspection;
- the need for X-ray does not by itself mean that the design has poor DFM;
- a net can exist electrically without providing a practical physical probe target;
- ICT and flying-probe testing both depend on suitable physical access, although their fixture strategies differ;
- accessible connectors can provide useful test access when they expose the required signals;
- boundary scan can reduce some physical-probe requirements when compatible devices and test architecture are available.

Section 3.12 next examines **design for rework and service access**, where component spacing and mechanical access influence how safely and efficiently parts can be removed, replaced, or serviced.

### Important primary-source correction

The primary instructional slide states broadly that X-ray is slower, more expensive, not normally used by default, and that needing X-ray usually indicates poor DFM.

That wording is **not retained as a universal rule**.

Technical correction:
- BGA solder balls and important QFN underside/center-pad regions are inherently hidden from optical inspection after assembly;
- current electronics manufacturing commonly uses X-ray or automated X-ray inspection for such hidden solder joints;
- requiring X-ray can therefore be a legitimate consequence of package selection rather than a DFM failure.

The original teaching purpose is preserved by teaching:
- use optical inspection where the required feature is optically accessible;
- recognize when package/layout geometry prevents optical access;
- plan a suitable inspection method before release.
