# 3.3 - Design Rules, Manufacturing Capabilities, and Requirements

Section 3.2 showed that DFM review should happen while the design can still be changed. The next practical question is:

> **Where do the DFM rules come from?**

PCB designers see numbers in standards, manufacturer design guides, CAD rule sets, and examples. Those numbers are useful only when they are applied in the right context.

For this chapter, keep one simple idea in mind:

> **Use general design guidance as a starting point, check the intended manufacturer's rules, then enter appropriate rules into the PCB CAD system and run DRC.**

---

## 3.3.1 - Where DFM Rules Come From

MET students will most often encounter DFM rules in three places.

### General design guidance and standards

Industry design guidance and standards provide commonly used design practices, terminology, and technical guidance.

They are useful starting points, but a general guideline is not automatically the exact manufacturing limit for every PCB supplier or assembly process.

### Manufacturer / supplier DFM rules

PCB fabricators and electronics-assembly suppliers publish design rules based on their own processes and equipment.

These documents may include:

- minimum capabilities;
- preferred design values;
- special-process requirements;
- tooling or assembly limitations;
- information used during vendor DFM review.

For an actual design, the current rules from the intended fabricator or assembly supplier are important because they describe the process that will be used to build the board or assembly.

### CAD design rules and DRC

The selected values are entered into the PCB CAD system as **design rules**.

The CAD software then performs **design-rule checking (DRC)** to compare the layout with those configured rules.

A simple working sequence is:

> **Design guidance + manufacturer rules -> choose practical design rules -> enter the rules in CAD -> run DRC**

## 3.3.2 - Minimum Does Not Mean Preferred

Manufacturer documents often list both **minimum** and **preferred** values.

For a rule written as a minimum, such as minimum spacing:

| Term | Practical meaning |
|---|---|
| **Minimum** | The process can produce the feature under the stated conditions, but the design is close to the process boundary. |
| **Preferred** | A less aggressive design value that provides more manufacturing margin and is usually easier to produce consistently. |

The preferred value is often the better routine choice when board space and product requirements allow it.

This does **not** mean the minimum value is unusable. A high-density design may intentionally use a limiting capability when the selected manufacturer supports it and the product needs it.

The key distinction is:

> **Minimum capability is not the same as the preferred design value.**

Later sections use this idea when discussing component spacing, annular rings, vias, solder mask, and other PCB features.

---

## 3.3.3 - Why Manufacturer Rules Matter

Manufacturers do not all have the same equipment, materials, processes, or preferred operating ranges.

For PCB fabrication, suppliers can differ in areas such as:

- trace and spacing capability;
- drilling and registration capability;
- solder-mask capability;
- board construction and special processes.

Assembly suppliers can also differ in:

- component-placement capability;
- tooling and handling needs;
- inspection methods;
- process limitations for particular packages or board designs.

Because of these differences:

> **A rule that works for one manufacturer should not automatically be assumed to work for another.**

Before production release, check the current DFM information for the intended fabricator and assembler. Vendor DFM review reports can provide an additional process-specific check of the completed design.

---

## 3.3.4 - What DRC Actually Does

**Design-rule checking (DRC)** compares the PCB layout with the rules entered into the CAD software.

For example, the CAD system can check whether:

- traces are too close together;
- a trace is narrower than the configured rule;
- a hole or annular ring violates the configured rule;
- copper is too close to a board edge;
- solder-mask clearances violate the rule set.

DRC is valuable because it checks the layout quickly and consistently.

But:

> **Passing DRC means the entered rules were satisfied. It does not guarantee that the rules match the intended manufacturer.**

This is why manufacturer DFM rules and supplier review still matter.

Section 3.14 returns to DRC and DFM tools in more detail.

---

## 3.3.5 - How This Book Uses DFM Numbers

Later sections use representative DFM values so you can:

- understand the approximate scale of common PCB features;
- compare a layout with a supplied rule;
- practice minimum-versus-preferred decisions;
- identify likely DFM problems.

Unless a value is specifically identified as a governing requirement, treat it as **instructional or representative** rather than as a universal production limit.

For a real design:

> **Verify the actual value with the intended manufacturer and any applicable product requirements before release.**

This lets the examples remain useful for learning without suggesting that every PCB process uses exactly the same limits.

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- DFM rules come from general design guidance, manufacturer-specific rules, and the CAD rule set used for checking;
- manufacturer rules are important because processes and capabilities differ;
- minimum capability is not the same as a preferred design value;
- DRC checks the rules entered into the CAD system;
- passing DRC does not prove that the rule set matches the intended manufacturer;
- numerical values used in this book are for learning unless they are specifically identified otherwise.

Section 3.4 next applies these ideas to **component placement and orientation**.
