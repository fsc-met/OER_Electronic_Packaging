# 3.3 - Design Rules, Manufacturing Capabilities, and Requirements

Section 3.2 showed that design-rule checking, DFM/DFA review, supplier feedback, and controlled revision all occur before production release. To use those activities correctly, engineers must understand **where the rules come from**.

A value shown in a CAD rule, supplier capability table, design guide, standard, or worked example may look like the same kind of number. Its engineering meaning can be very different.

> **Before applying a DFM number, identify its source, role, and applicable process.**

This interpretation framework will be used throughout the rest of Chapter 3.

---

## 3.3.1 - Different Sources Answer Different Questions

Start by asking, “**What kind of value is this?**”

| Source or role | What it tells you | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **Governing requirement** | What the product must satisfy | release/acceptance decision |
| **Applicable standard or standards guidance** | Recognized requirement, method, or guidance relevant to the product | design/verification input |
| **Supplier/process capability** | What a defined fabrication, assembly, inspection, or test process can achieve | feasibility/process selection |
| **Preferred/routine DFM target** | A less aggressive target chosen to provide practical manufacturing margin | normal design target |
| **Internal company DFM rule** | A controlled organizational target or practice | consistent design practice |
| **EDA design rule** | What the CAD system is configured to check | automated DRC |
| **Instructional/example value** | A value supplied for learning or a bounded example | analysis/practice only |

These roles are related, but they are not interchangeable.

Supplier capability information may help establish a preferred target, and that target may then be encoded as a CAD rule. But:

> **A CAD rule is not automatically a supplier capability, and a supplier capability is not automatically a product requirement.**

## 3.3.2 - Governing Requirements: What Must Be Satisfied

A **governing requirement** is an applicable requirement that the released product or design must satisfy.

Depending on the product, it may come from:

- customer or contractual requirements;
- regulatory or safety requirements;
- controlled product specifications;
- engineering drawings and approved notes;
- company requirements;
- standards or standards provisions that apply to the product.

Multiple requirements can apply to the same feature. If they conflict, the conflict must be resolved through the organization's controlled engineering process rather than by choosing whichever value is easiest to meet.

The practical question is:

> **What must this product satisfy?**

That is different from asking what a supplier can manufacture.

---

## 3.3.3 - Process Capability: What Manufacturing Can Achieve

A **process capability** describes what a defined manufacturing or verification process can achieve under stated conditions.

For Chapter 3, capability includes more than bare-board fabrication. Examples include:

- PCB trace, spacing, drilling, and registration capability;
- solder-mask registration capability;
- component-placement capability;
- assembly-tool access;
- inspection capability;
- electrical-test/probe access;
- tooling, handling, and depanelization capability.

Capability is **conditional**, not universal. It can depend on the supplier, equipment, material/board construction, component package, process route, production conditions, and inspection/test method.

Therefore, capability data should be read together with its applicable notes and conditions.

> **Capability answers “Can this process produce or verify the feature?” It does not automatically answer “Is this the preferred routine design target?”**

---

## 3.3.4 - Capability Boundary Versus Preferred DFM Target

Chapter 2 introduced the distinction between a fabrication capability boundary and a preferred capability. Chapter 3 applies the same reasoning to the broader DFM problem.

A **capability boundary** is a limiting value of the intended process under specified conditions.

A **preferred or routine DFM target** is a design value chosen to provide more manufacturing margin and avoid unnecessary use of the process edge.

For minimum-spacing rules, a preferred target normally provides **more spacing** than the stated minimum. For a maximum-size or maximum-tolerance rule, the numerical direction may be different.

The important principle is not “larger is always better.” It is:

> **Move the routine design away from the relevant process boundary unless the product has a reason to use the tighter capability.**

Preferred targets can provide:

- more tolerance to normal variation;
- simpler manufacturing control;
- better yield potential;
- fewer special-process requirements;
- more supplier/process flexibility.

Using a limiting capability can still be a valid engineering choice when the product requires it and the process is intentionally selected and controlled.

---

## 3.3.5 - Internal DFM Rules Turn Decisions Into Repeatable Practice

Organizations often establish internal DFM rules so that common manufacturing decisions are applied consistently.

An internal rule may be based on:

- approved supplier capabilities;
- preferred process margin;
- product-family needs;
- manufacturing experience;
- quality or reliability objectives;
- applicable requirements.

An internal DFM rule should therefore have a known technical basis and scope.

A rule appropriate for one product family, supplier strategy, or manufacturing process is not automatically appropriate for every future design.

---

## 3.3.6 - EDA Rules Are Automated Implementations

PCB CAD tools perform **design-rule checking (DRC)** using rules configured in the design system.

The useful relationship is:

> **Engineering basis -> approved design target -> EDA rule -> DRC result**

The EDA rule is the software implementation of an engineering decision. The software does not create the authority behind the rule.

This creates three important cases:

| Situation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Correct engineering basis + correctly configured rule | DRC is a useful automated control |
| Correct requirement/capability + incorrectly configured rule | DRC result can be misleading |
| Correct software check + outdated engineering basis | The software can correctly check the wrong rule |

Therefore:

> **“DRC passed” means the encoded rules were satisfied. It does not prove that every applicable manufacturing or product requirement was satisfied.**

Section 3.14 develops DRC, DFM tools, supplier reports, and release review in more detail.

---

## 3.3.7 - Standards Must Be Used According to Their Role

Standards can provide important:

- requirements;
- definitions;
- test methods;
- qualification/performance criteria;
- acceptability criteria;
- design guidance.

Their role depends on the specific document, applicable revision, product, technology, and how the document applies to the product definition.

Two cautions follow:

- an “industry guideline” should not automatically be called a **standard requirement**;
- a supplier capability should not automatically be described as an industry-wide or **IPC limit**.

For real engineering work, the current applicable source should be checked directly rather than relying on a simplified value copied from a secondary reference.

---

## 3.3.8 - Instructional Values Are Bounded Learning Data

Chapter 3 will use numerical values in worked examples and practice problems.

When a deterministic answer is needed, the problem may provide a **representative instructional DFM rule set** or a fictional supplier capability table.

These values are useful because they let readers practice:

- comparing a design with a rule;
- distinguishing capability from preferred target;
- recognizing reduced process margin;
- selecting a corrective action.

Unless explicitly identified otherwise, they are not universal design limits or acceptance criteria.

The same caution applies to older lecture values, textbook examples, or generic design-guide numbers: they may provide useful engineering context, but their current applicability must be verified before release.

---

## 3.3.9 - A Five-Step Method for Interpreting a DFM Value

When you encounter a DFM number, use five steps.

### Step 1 - Identify the feature

What is being controlled?

Examples include copper spacing, component clearance, annular ring, solder-mask geometry, or test access.

### Step 2 - Identify the source

Does the value come from a governing requirement, standard, supplier capability, internal rule, CAD rule set, or instructional example?

### Step 3 - Identify the role

Is the value:

- something the product **must satisfy**;
- something the process **can achieve**;
- a **preferred engineering target**;
- an **automated check**;
- or **example/problem data**?

### Step 4 - Check applicability

Confirm the relevant:

- product;
- process/supplier;
- technology;
- conditions;
- revision.

### Step 5 - Determine the design rule to use

Reconcile the applicable information before release.

A concise engineering chain is:

> **Requirement -> process capability -> preferred target -> EDA rule -> verification**

This is not a universal authority ladder. It is a practical reminder that an EDA rule should have a defensible engineering basis.

---

## 3.3.10 - Example: Manufacturable but Outside the Preferred Target

Consider a hypothetical board feature.

Suppose:

- the selected supplier says the feature is within its offered capability;
- the organization's preferred DFM rule provides more margin;
- the CAD system is configured to that preferred rule;
- no governing product requirement forces use of the tighter geometry.

If the layout uses the tighter supplier capability:

- the supplier may still be able to manufacture it;
- the design may fail the preferred DRC rule;
- the DRC failure does not mean the feature is physically impossible;
- engineering must decide whether to revise the layout or intentionally approve the tighter process.

This distinction is central to DFM:

> **“Manufacturable” and “preferred for routine production” are not the same statement.**

The reverse is also possible: a feature can satisfy an internal preferred rule but still violate a separate governing requirement. That requirement must still be resolved.

---

## 3.3.11 - Why Process Margin Still Matters

A design can satisfy individual supplier limits while using many aggressive features close to process boundaries.

That can increase sensitivity to:

- normal process variation;
- supplier/process changes;
- inspection burden;
- special controls;
- scrap or remake risk;
- cost.

The exact effect is product- and supplier-dependent, so no universal cost or yield penalty should be assigned.

Instead, ask:

> **Which features are comfortably within the intended process, which are close to a capability boundary, and is the reduced margin justified?**

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- similar-looking DFM values can have different engineering roles;
- governing requirements define what the product must satisfy;
- manufacturing capability describes what a defined process can achieve;
- preferred DFM targets normally provide more process margin than capability boundaries;
- internal DFM rules turn engineering decisions into repeatable practice;
- EDA rules are configured implementations of engineering decisions;
- DRC can correctly check an inappropriate or outdated rule;
- standards must be used according to their actual role and applicability;
- instructional values are bounded learning data unless explicitly identified otherwise;
- “manufacturable” does not necessarily mean “preferred for routine production”;
- important design rules should have a traceable engineering basis.
