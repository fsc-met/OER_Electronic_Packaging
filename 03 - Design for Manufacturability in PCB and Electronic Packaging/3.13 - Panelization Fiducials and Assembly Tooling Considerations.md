# 3.13 - Panelization, Fiducials, and Assembly Tooling Considerations

Section 3.12 treated the PCB as an assembled product that may need rework or service. Manufacturing also requires another viewpoint: the PCB may not travel through fabrication and assembly as one loose board.

Small boards, irregular boards, or repeated products are often handled as part of a **panelized array**.

A useful design logic is:

> **Board geometry + panel/separation method + handling/alignment needs + assembly access -> panel and tooling strategy**

The designer does not always create the production panel directly. A PCB fabricator or assembly supplier may create it from the released single-board design. Even then, the board layout must leave enough margin and usable geometry for panel rails, separation features, fiducials, tooling, conveyors, and fixtures.

---

## 3.13.1 - Why Boards Are Panelized

**Panelization** places one or more PCB images into a larger manufacturing or assembly panel.

This can help:

- small boards travel through conveyors and handling equipment;
- several boards are processed together as one stable workpiece;
- fiducials and tooling holes can be placed on removable process rails;
- panel-level support improves handling stability;
- repeated boards can share common assembly handling and alignment features.

Panelization therefore affects more than fabrication material utilization.

For assembly, the panel may need to provide:

- **process rails** or handling edges;
- clear conveyor/clamping regions;
- panel-level fiducials;
- tooling or alignment holes;
- stable breakaway geometry;
- room for overhanging components;
- enough stiffness and support for the intended process.

Current assembly suppliers commonly use process rails on the order of **5 mm** when extra edge width is needed for automated handling. This is a useful engineering scale, not a universal rail-width requirement. The selected assembler's conveyor, clamp, stencil-printer, and fixture requirements control the released panel design.

A useful mental model is:

> **The individual PCB defines the product geometry; the panel adds temporary geometry needed to manufacture, align, support, transport, and later separate that product.**

## 3.13.2 - Rails and Handling Edges Need Clear Manufacturing Space

A rail or process edge may be used by:

- conveyor belts;
- board guides;
- clamps;
- stencil printers;
- pick-and-place equipment;
- fixtures or support tooling.

Components placed near or beyond the final board edge can interfere with those features.

This is especially important for:

- edge connectors;
- USB or other overhanging connectors;
- switches;
- large capacitors;
- tall components;
- parts whose body extends beyond the routed PCB outline.

Possible design responses include:

- increasing the panel border;
- adding a routed cutout in the rail;
- increasing spacing between repeated boards;
- changing panel orientation;
- relocating the component if product requirements allow.

A process rail is therefore not simply "waste material." It can be a functional part of the assembly interface.

## 3.13.3 - Depanelization Method Changes the Required Geometry

The individual PCB eventually has to be separated from the array.

Two common approaches are:

- **V-score / V-cut**, where controlled grooves are formed along intended straight separation lines;
- **tab routing**, where most of the board outline is routed and small tabs remain to hold the PCB in the panel.

A tab may be solid or may include a row of small drilled holes, often called a **mouse-bite** or perforated breakaway tab.

These approaches are not interchangeable.

### V-score awareness

V-score is well suited to compatible straight-line board boundaries and can allow boards to share a separation line with little or no gap between board profiles.

However:

- the score normally continues along a straight line;
- irregular board shapes may require routing or a mixed strategy;
- depanelization bends the board along the score;
- components and fragile features near the score can be exposed to separation stress.

### Tab-routing / mouse-bite awareness

Tab routing is useful when:

- the board outline is irregular;
- most of the perimeter needs to be routed;
- controlled connection points are preferred.

However:

- routing requires physical space for the cutter path;
- tabs concentrate separation force locally;
- mouse-bite tabs leave a small serrated edge after separation;
- component, copper, and mechanical keep-outs should consider the actual break region.

Current supplier examples commonly use roughly **1.6-2.0 mm** between routed board profiles in panelized arrays. V-score can use a **0 mm board-to-board profile gap** where the supplier's straight-line scoring geometry allows it. These values illustrate process scale only; they are not universal panel rules.

## 3.13.4 - Depanelization Keep-Outs Protect Components and Copper

Section 3.5 introduced component-to-edge spacing, and Section 3.8 treated copper-to-edge geometry. Panelization adds another question:

> **What happens at the specific location where the panel will be broken, routed, or scored?**

The chapter's representative **2.5 mm general component-to-edge planning value** and **3.0 mm or more for tall/heavy components** remain useful starting points for ordinary placement review.

They do **not** guarantee adequate depanelization margin.

Local risk can be greater when a component is:

- directly beside a V-score;
- beside a stiff breakaway tab;
- heavy, tall, brittle, or mechanically sensitive;
- connected by fragile solder joints;
- positioned so panel bending loads the component during separation.

Similarly, copper near a routed or scored edge must satisfy the actual fabricator's copper-to-edge rule.

A good pre-release review therefore distinguishes:

- the final board edge;
- the panel separation line;
- the local break/tab region;
- the selected supplier's component and copper keep-outs.

Do not use one universal "depanelization clearance" number for every panel method.

## 3.13.5 - Global and Local Fiducials Provide Optical Alignment References

A **fiducial** is a controlled optical target that assembly equipment can recognize.

Fiducials help machines determine:

- panel position;
- panel rotation;
- board position within a panel;
- local registration near precision-placement regions where required.

### Global fiducials

**Global fiducials** locate the overall panel or board.

A common current assembly practice is to use **at least three panel-level fiducials** placed far apart and arranged so position and rotation are unambiguous.

### Local fiducials

**Local fiducials** provide an additional reference near an individual PCB or precision component region.

They can be useful when:

- several boards are repeated in one panel;
- fine-pitch or high-accuracy placement benefits from local correction;
- the selected assembly equipment or supplier requires them.

Local fiducials are not automatically required beside every component.

A common current supplier example is a **1.0 mm circular exposed-copper fiducial** with a larger solder-mask/feature-clearance region around it. The exact target size, surface finish, keep-out, number, and location should follow the selected assembler's vision-system requirements.

The important DFM principle is not to memorize one fiducial drawing. It is to provide optical references that the selected assembly process can see and use reliably.

For high-accuracy placement, the most useful question is:

> **Which coordinate frame does the machine need to correct: the whole panel, the individual PCB, or a local precision-placement region?**

## 3.13.6 - Tooling Holes Are Mechanical Alignment Features

A fiducial is an **optical** reference.

A tooling or alignment hole is a **mechanical** feature.

Tooling holes can be used to:

- locate a panel in a fixture;
- control orientation;
- register tooling;
- support handling or assembly equipment.

They are commonly placed in removable panel rails when that keeps production tooling off the product PCB.

Current supplier examples commonly place non-plated tooling holes in roughly the **2-3 mm diameter scale**, but the exact diameter, position, plating condition, and hole pattern depend on the equipment and supplier.

The tooling-hole pattern should also make panel orientation unambiguous where incorrect loading is possible.

> **Fiducials and tooling holes solve different alignment problems: one is optical, the other mechanical.**

---

## 3.13.7 - Conveyor and Board-Support Access Must Be Preserved

During stencil printing, component placement, and other assembly operations, the panel may need physical support.

Design issues can occur when:

- components extend into conveyor or clamp regions;
- bottom-side components occupy areas needed for support pins;
- overhanging components collide with panel rails;
- a very thin or weak panel sags between supports;
- panel cutouts remove too much stiffness;
- tooling features are blocked by product hardware.

The detailed support fixture is normally created by the assembly supplier.

The design team should still make sure that a feasible support strategy exists.

## 3.13.8 - Panel Orientation and Repeated-Board Arrangement Should Be Intentional

Repeated boards in a panel do not always have to face the same direction.

Depending on board shape and assembly process, a supplier may:

- repeat all boards in the same orientation;
- rotate selected boards for nesting or panel stability;
- increase spacing around an overhanging component;
- add rail cutouts;
- change the break-tab pattern;
- use an asymmetric fiducial or tooling pattern to prevent 180-degree loading errors.

Rotation is not automatically a DFM problem.

The important requirement is that:

- the panel orientation is unambiguous;
- assembly data match the repeated-board arrangement;
- component overhangs and rails do not interfere;
- separation geometry remains practical;
- the panel remains stable enough for handling and assembly.

Detailed nesting algorithms and machine programming are outside this section.

## 3.13.9 - Applied Panel and Tooling Review

The values below are **representative current manufacturing examples**, not universal requirements.

| Design observation | DFM interpretation | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| Small PCB is placed in a panel with about 5 mm process rails | Consistent with a common assembly-handling scale | Verify the selected assembler's rail/conveyor requirement |
| Panel has only two global fiducials | Some equipment may work, but current assembly guidance commonly uses three well-separated references | Add/verify fiducials with the assembler |
| Panel uses 1.0 mm exposed-copper circular fiducials with clear surrounding area | Consistent with a common current fiducial scale | Verify exact mask opening, keep-out, finish, and location |
| Panel uses 2.0 mm non-plated tooling holes in the rails | Consistent with one common assembly-tooling scale | Verify hole diameter and pattern with the selected equipment/supplier |
| Irregular PCB is specified for V-score on all sides | V-score geometry may not match the irregular outline | Consider routed/tabbed separation or a mixed strategy |
| Routed panel has about 1.6-2.0 mm between board profiles | Consistent with current supplier examples for cutter access | Verify router diameter, tabs, and supplier panel rules |
| Tall or brittle component is close to a V-score or break tab | Local depanelization stress may threaten the part or solder joints | Increase local keep-out or change the break method/location |
| Bottom-side components occupy all likely support-pin regions | Assembly support may require special tooling | Relocate parts where practical or confirm a custom support strategy |
| Connector overhangs into the panel rail | Conveyor, rail, or depanelization interference may occur | Add panel cutout/spacing or revise orientation with the assembler |
| Repeated boards are rotated in the panel | Rotation itself is not a defect | Confirm orientation references and assembly data are consistent |

A practical review sequence is:

> **Identify the panel/handling strategy -> choose the separation method -> protect break regions -> provide fiducials -> provide mechanical tooling where required -> preserve conveyor/support access -> verify orientation and repeated-board arrangement -> confirm with the selected fabricator/assembler.**

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- an individual PCB may be manufactured and assembled as part of a larger panel;
- process rails provide handling space and can carry fiducials and tooling features;
- V-score and tab routing create different separation geometry and should not be treated as interchangeable;
- depanelization keep-outs should protect components, copper, and fragile mechanical features near break regions;
- at least three panel-level fiducials and a 1.0 mm circular copper target are useful current assembly-planning examples, but the selected assembler controls the actual fiducial rule;
- tooling holes provide mechanical registration and commonly fall in the roughly 2-3 mm diameter scale, but exact requirements are equipment dependent;
- conveyor edges and board-support regions must remain physically usable;
- repeated-board orientation can vary, but orientation and assembly data must remain unambiguous;
- panelization should be reviewed with the actual fabricator and assembler before release.

Section 3.14 next examines **DFM review, tools, and design release**, where encoded design checks are combined with broader engineering and supplier review before manufacturing release.
