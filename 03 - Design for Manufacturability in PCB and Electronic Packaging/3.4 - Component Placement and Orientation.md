# 3.4 - Component Placement and Orientation

Section 3.3 showed how practical DFM rules are selected and checked. The next step is to apply those rules to the physical placement of components on the PCB.

Placement is not only about making every component fit. The layout must also support:

- accurate automated placement;
- clear orientation and polarity;
- board handling and depanelization;
- inspection;
- rework;
- mechanical support for larger or heavier parts.

> **A component can be electrically connected correctly and still be placed poorly for manufacturing.**

---

## 3.4.1 - Placement Data and Placement Accuracy

Automated pick-and-place equipment uses placement information generated from the PCB design. The exact file format depends on the CAD and assembly system, but the basic information commonly includes:

- component reference designator;
- X-Y location;
- component center or centroid;
- rotation angle;
- board side;
- package/footprint identification.

A simple conceptual placement entry might look like this:

| Item | Example value |
|---|---:|
| Reference designator | `U3` |
| X position | 42.30 mm |
| Y position | 18.70 mm |
| Rotation | 90° |
| Board side | Top |

This is an instructional example, not a required placement-file format.

The assembly machine assumes that the design data are correct. If the wrong footprint, centroid, rotation, or polarity is supplied, a precise machine can still place the component incorrectly.

### How accurate is pick-and-place?

Current production equipment commonly specifies placement accuracy on the order of **tens of micrometers**. For example, current production systems include manufacturer specifications around **±35 μm** and **±25 μm** under their stated accuracy conditions, while specialized high-accuracy systems can be tighter.

For comparison:

$$
35\ \mu\text{m} = 0.035\ \text{mm}
$$

The exact capability depends on the machine, recognition method, component package, operating mode, setup, and production conditions.

The DFM lesson is not to memorize one machine specification. It is to understand that:

> **Placement accuracy is finite, so the footprint and surrounding layout must tolerate normal placement variation.**

Detailed land-pattern geometry is covered in Section 3.6.

## 3.4.2 - Make Orientation Clear and Intentional

Orientation is part of placement data and should be intentional.

Where practical:

- orient similar components consistently;
- use a consistent polarity direction for polarized parts;
- make pin 1 easy to identify on orientation-sensitive ICs;
- keep polarity marks visible after assembly;
- avoid unnecessary orientation changes that make programming, inspection, or manual verification harder.

Orientation-sensitive parts can include:

- diodes;
- LEDs;
- electrolytic capacitors;
- integrated circuits;
- polarized connectors.

Consistent orientation does **not** mean every component must point in the same direction. Routing, mechanical constraints, thermal needs, connector location, and package geometry may require different orientations.

Modern placement equipment can rotate parts automatically, so the main reasons for consistent orientation are usually **clarity, programming, inspection, polarity control, and rework**, not simply reducing machine rotation time.

> **Use orientation conventions to make the assembly easier to understand and verify, not as a rigid rule that overrides the rest of the design.**

---

## 3.4.3 - Keep Manufacturing Space Near Board Edges

A component placed very close to the PCB edge can interfere with:

- routing or scoring;
- depanelization;
- conveyor or rail handling;
- assembly fixtures;
- test fixtures;
- manual handling.

Mechanical stress during board separation can also be transferred to nearby components and solder joints.

For the representative DFM rule set used in this chapter, **about 2.5 mm (0.10 in)** from the board edge is a useful planning value for ordinary component placement. Section 3.5 will distinguish minimum, preferred, and situation-dependent clearances in more detail.

This is not a universal production requirement. Actual clearance can be smaller or larger depending on:

- panelization method;
- board-routing or V-score location;
- tooling rails;
- component height and mass;
- overhanging connectors;
- supplier process.

> **Use the edge guideline to recognize risk early; use the intended supplier's rule to release the design.**

---

## 3.4.4 - Consider Height, Heavy Parts, Inspection, and Rework

Component height changes what nearby equipment and people can reach or see.

Tall components can create:

- **AOI shadowing**, where a camera cannot clearly see a nearby solder joint;
- difficult manual inspection;
- restricted probe access;
- restricted hot-air or soldering-iron access.

Large connectors and heavier components deserve extra attention because they may also affect:

- board-edge handling;
- depanelization stress;
- fixture access;
- mechanical support.

The goal is not to group all tall or heavy parts by rule. Instead, avoid arrangements that create unnecessary optical obstruction, tool-access problems, or mechanical risk. Heating effects can also depend on component size and local thermal mass, but detailed reflow behavior belongs in Chapter 4.

A dense layout may be electrically correct and still be difficult to repair. Rework tools such as soldering irons, hot-air nozzles, tweezers, and probes need physical access.

## 3.4.5 - Placement Can Contribute to Tombstoning

A small two-terminal SMT component can sometimes stand upright during reflow. This defect is called **tombstoning**.

Tombstoning occurs when the forces acting at the two soldered ends become sufficiently unbalanced while the solder is melting and wetting.

Placement can contribute when the component is significantly off-center, but placement is only one factor.

Other important contributors include:

- unequal solder-paste deposits;
- asymmetric pad geometry;
- unequal copper area or thermal conditions around the two pads;
- heating and reflow-process conditions.

For placement DFM:

- center the component on its intended land pattern;
- avoid unnecessary asymmetry around the two ends;
- make sure the placement data and footprint are correct.

## 3.4.6 - Applied Placement Review

A useful DFM review connects what you observe on the layout to the manufacturing concern and a practical response.

| Placement observation | Why it matters | Typical design response |
|---|---|---|
| A polarized part faces the opposite direction from similar parts | More opportunity for programming, assembly, or inspection mistakes | Use a consistent orientation where practical and keep polarity marking clear |
| A component is about 1 mm from a routed board edge on a design with no special edge-placement need | Less margin for handling, tooling, and depanelization than the representative 2.5 mm planning value used in this chapter | Move the component inward if practical or verify that the supplier/panel process supports the tighter placement |
| A tall connector blocks the view of nearby small SMT joints | AOI or manual inspection may be obstructed | Reposition one of the parts or confirm an appropriate inspection strategy |
| A chip component is visibly offset from the center of its pads | Reduced geometric margin and possible soldering imbalance | Check centroid/placement data and center the component on the intended land pattern |
| A dense cluster leaves no room for a hot-air nozzle or tweezers | Rework can damage adjacent parts or become impractical | Increase local access where feasible or plan the service/rework method intentionally |
| A large or heavy connector sits close to a depanelization edge | Board-separation loads can be transferred into the component or solder joints | Increase separation from the edge and review panelization/support with the supplier |

This is a placement review, not the complete Chapter 3 DFM checklist.

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- pick-and-place equipment follows design data for location, centroid, rotation, footprint, and orientation;
- current placement accuracy is typically on the order of tens of micrometers, so normal placement variation must be considered;
- similar and polarized parts should use clear, consistent orientation where practical;
- about 2.5 mm (0.10 in) is the representative component-to-edge planning value used in this chapter, but actual supplier/panel rules control production release;
- tall, heavy, and densely packed components can affect handling, inspection, and rework;
- placement offset can contribute to tombstoning, but tombstoning is a multi-factor soldering defect;
- a good DFM review connects an observed layout condition to its manufacturing consequence and corrective action.

Section 3.5 next develops the **spacing and clearance rules** used to turn these placement concepts into more detailed measurable checks.

Teach component placement and orientation as a practical DFM task using the primary instructional material's concrete examples, numbers, and visual concepts, while correcting or qualifying items that are process-dependent.

### Teaching-inventory disposition

- **Retained:** X-Y/centroid/rotation, finite accuracy, orientation/polarity, edge risk, height/visibility, rework access, tombstoning, practical checklist logic.
- **Retained with qualification:** component-to-edge value around 2.5 mm as representative planning guidance rather than a universal requirement.
- **Corrected/updated:** lecture statement of `±50-75 μm (common)` placement accuracy. Fresh official manufacturer verification shows current modular systems around ±35 μm and ±25 μm under their stated accuracy conditions, while specialized high-accuracy systems can be around 20 μm @ 3 sigma or tighter. Public text therefore teaches `tens of micrometers` and uses current manufacturer examples rather than presenting one universal range.
- **De-emphasized:** reduced pick-and-place rotation time as a primary reason for uniform orientation. Modern equipment can rotate parts automatically; consistency is taught mainly for programming clarity, polarity control, inspection, and rework.
- **Deferred:** detailed SMT land-pattern dimensions to Section 3.6; THT hole sizing to Section 3.7; detailed component-to-component spacing to Section 3.5; detailed inspection/test access to Section 3.11; detailed rework access to Section 3.12; reflow-process mechanisms to Chapter 4.

### Current numerical verification

Placement-accuracy cross-check:

- JUKI RS-2: ±35 μm placement accuracy with the manufacturer's stated `Cpk ≥ 1` condition;
- ASMPT SIPLACE TX: standard accuracy 25 μm; the CPP head is specified up to ±35 μm @ 3 sigma;
- ASMPT SIPLACE TX micron: standard accuracy 20 μm @ 3 sigma, with tighter 15 μm and 10 μm accuracy classes available.

Conclusion:

- `tens of micrometers` is the correct scale;
- current manufacturer values use different accuracy definitions/conditions and should not be collapsed into one universal specification;
- the public text therefore uses concrete examples (±35 μm and ±25 μm) and keeps the durable lesson at `tens of micrometers`.

Board-edge cross-check:

- the primary instructional material uses about 2.5 mm (0.10 in) as a general placement guideline;
- current supplier guidance confirms that board-edge placement, manufacturing courtyard, panelization, depanelization, and overhanging-component constraints are supplier/process dependent;
- Eurocircuits, for example, defines the board edge as part of the component manufacturing courtyard/keepout concept and separately documents panel/depanelization constraints for edge/overhanging components;
- because a universal 2-3 mm component-body rule is not supported across suppliers, the public text now identifies **2.5 mm (0.10 in)** specifically as the representative DFM planning value used in this chapter rather than as an industry-wide requirement.
