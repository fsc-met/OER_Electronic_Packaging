# 3.10 - Silkscreen and Markings

Section 3.9 focused on the solder-mask openings that determine which copper remains exposed. The next surface layer to review is the **legend**, commonly called the **silkscreen** or **nomenclature** layer.

The legend does not create an electrical connection. Its job is to communicate useful information to people during assembly, inspection, troubleshooting, and service. Although **silkscreen** is the familiar design term, the physical marking may be produced by traditional screen printing, digital inkjet, or another qualified marking process.

Typical markings include:

- component reference designators;
- polarity and orientation marks;
- pin-1 indicators;
- connector identifiers;
- test-point labels;
- board part number and revision information where required.

A useful design logic is:

> **Required information + printing capability + registration tolerance + assembled-state visibility -> marking decision**

The main checks are:

- marking content is correct and unambiguous;
- text and line features are large enough for the selected printing process;
- legend is kept away from pads and other functional surfaces;
- polarity and pin-1 marks remain visible after assembly;
- reference designators can still be associated with the intended component;
- unnecessary clutter does not make inspection or rework harder.

---

## 3.10.1 - Markings Should Communicate Useful Assembly Information

A PCB legend can reduce ambiguity during manufacturing and service.

Common examples include:

- `R12`, `C8`, and `U3` as reference designators;
- a pin-1 dot or corner mark near an integrated circuit;
- `+` and `-` marks where polarity must be obvious;
- diode or capacitor polarity indicators;
- connector labels such as `J1`, `POWER`, or `IN/OUT`;
- test-point labels such as `TP1`;
- board identification or revision markings where required.

The important issue is not how much text can be placed on the board. The issue is whether the information helps someone correctly identify, orient, inspect, test, or service the assembly.

A marking should also be **unambiguous**. A visible dot near an integrated circuit is useful only if it is obviously associated with the pin-1 corner. A polarity mark is useful only if its meaning agrees with the approved footprint and assembly documentation.

## 3.10.2 - Legend Registration Is Also Finite

The legend pattern is not positioned with perfect accuracy relative to copper and solder mask.

Therefore, a design should not place legend strokes directly against a solderable feature.

A useful conventional planning range is:

- **legend-to-solderable-area clearance: about 0.10-0.15 mm or more**.

The exact requirement depends on the fabricator and marking process. Different fabricators may define the clearance to the copper pad, the exposed finished copper, or the solder-mask opening, so the rule definition must be checked along with the numerical value.

If the legend is too close to a pad or exposed finished copper:

- printing variation can place marking material on a functional surface;
- the fabricator may clip or delete part of the legend to protect solderability;
- characters may become incomplete or ambiguous.

A useful design rule is:

> **Keep legend outside the fabricator's required clearance from exposed copper and solderable openings.**

---

## 3.10.3 - Text Height and Line Width Control Readability

A character that is clear on a CAD screen may be difficult to reproduce on the actual board.

Two useful conservative planning dimensions are:

- **text height: about 1.0 mm or larger**;
- **printed line/stroke width: about 0.15 mm or larger**.

These values match some mainstream fabrication services, but they are not universal. Current published capabilities include examples around **1.0 mm text height with 0.10-0.15 mm line width**, and some services support smaller text. Treat the values here as planning references rather than standards.

Small text and thin strokes can:

- print incompletely;
- fill in or break apart;
- become difficult to distinguish after fabrication;
- become unreadable after normal handling or assembly.

The selected fabricator's capability should control the final design rule.

## 3.10.4 - Markings Must Remain Useful After Assembly

A marking may be perfectly readable on the bare PCB and still become useless after components are installed.

Common examples include:

- a reference designator placed under the component body;
- a polarity mark hidden beneath the installed part;
- a connector label blocked by the connector housing;
- a test-point label separated from the test point by another installed component.

Important markings should therefore be reviewed in the **assembled state**, not only as a bare-board artwork layer.

## 3.10.5 - Avoid Marking Clutter Near Inspection and Rework Areas

More legend is not always better.

The legend supports human interpretation, but it does **not** replace the controlled bill of materials, centroid/pick-and-place data, assembly drawing, or other manufacturing documentation used to build the board.

Dense outlines, repeated labels, or oversized text can make it harder to:

- see the relationship between a component and its pads;
- recognize polarity and pin-1 information quickly;
- identify nearby test points;
- inspect a crowded region;
- determine which reference designator belongs to which component.

Markings should support inspection and rework rather than compete with them.

Detailed optical-inspection and test-access requirements are developed in Section 3.11.

## 3.10.6 - Applied Silkscreen Review

The table below uses the representative values in this section for engineering judgment. Final release must use the selected fabricator's actual legend rules.

| Design observation | DFM interpretation | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| Legend stroke is 0.05 mm from the edge of a solderable feature | Below the 0.10-0.15 mm planning range | Move the legend; do not assume CAM clipping will preserve the intended information |
| Legend is 0.15 mm or more from the solderable feature | Consistent with the conservative planning range | Still verify how the selected fabricator defines the clearance |
| Text height is 0.70 mm | Smaller than the conservative 1.0 mm planning reference | Do not automatically fail; verify whether the selected process supports smaller readable text |
| Text height is 1.0 mm with 0.15 mm line width | Conservative mainstream example supported by current vendor guidance | Good planning starting point; verify the fabricator |
| Reference designator is completely under the component body | Readable on bare PCB but hidden after assembly | Relocate it where practical |
| Pin-1 mark is outside the component body but close to the wrong corner | Mark is visible but ambiguous | Place the indicator so its relationship to pin 1 is unmistakable |
| Test-point label overlaps the test point | Marking interferes with a functional feature | Move the text away from the test surface |
| Dense group of designators cannot be associated with individual components | Marking clutter reduces usefulness | Reposition, simplify, or support with assembly documentation |

A practical review sequence is:

> **Check information -> check pad/feature clearance -> check text size and line width -> check registration margin -> check visibility after assembly -> check inspection/rework clarity.**

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- the PCB legend is a manufacturing and service communication layer;
- reference designators, polarity marks, pin-1 indicators, connector labels, and test-point labels should be unambiguous;
- legend registration relative to copper and solder mask is finite;
- **0.10-0.15 mm** is a useful conventional planning range for legend clearance from solderable/exposed features;
- **1.0 mm text height** and **0.15 mm line width** are useful conservative planning references, while some current processes support smaller line width or text;
- actual values depend on the selected fabricator and marking process;
- markings must be reviewed after components are installed;
- legend should help inspection and rework rather than create clutter or cover functional features.

Section 3.11 next examines **design for inspection and test access**, where component geometry and board layout determine what can actually be seen, probed, or electrically tested.
