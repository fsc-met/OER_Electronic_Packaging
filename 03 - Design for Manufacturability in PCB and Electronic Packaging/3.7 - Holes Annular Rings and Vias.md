# 3.7 - Holes, Annular Rings, and Vias

Section 3.6 focused on the land pattern that connects a component termination to PCB copper. Section 3.7 turns to **plated holes and vias**, where the design must also tolerate drilling, plating, positional variation, and three-dimensional board geometry.

Chapter 2 introduced what these structures are. The DFM question here is different:

> **Does the hole/via geometry provide enough manufacturing and assembly margin for the intended process?**

The main checks are:

- lead-to-finished-hole fit for through-hole components;
- annular-ring margin;
- drill and plating capability;
- via size together with board thickness;
- copper connections around vias;
- via-in-pad construction.

---

## 3.7.1 - THT Lead Diameter and Finished-Hole Size

For a through-hole component, the component lead or pin must fit through the **finished plated hole** after fabrication.

A useful first-order relationship is:

$$
D_f = D_{\text{lead,max}} + C
$$

where:

- $D_f$ = finished-hole diameter;
- $D_{\text{lead,max}}$ = maximum expected lead or pin diameter;
- $C$ = total diametral insertion clearance.

For ordinary soldered THT parts, the primary instructional material uses a representative **diametral** clearance range of:

- **0.15-0.30 mm** larger than the lead diameter.

For example, if:

- maximum lead diameter = **0.50 mm**;
- selected diametral clearance = **0.20 mm**;

then:

$$
D_f = 0.50 + 0.20 = 0.70\ \text{mm}
$$

This value is a practical teaching example, not a universal requirement for every lead shape, connector, or assembly process.

Too little clearance can cause:

- difficult or impossible insertion;
- sensitivity to lead-size variation;
- interference when the hole is at the small side of its tolerance.

Too much clearance can:

- allow excessive component movement;
- change the intended solder-joint geometry;
- consume unnecessary pad area.

### Finished hole is not the same as production drill

Plated holes are normally drilled larger than the desired finished opening because copper is later deposited on the hole wall.

The instructional material uses **20-25 μm** as a useful engineering-scale awareness value for plated-hole barrel copper. Current fabrication processes can specify different minimum or finished plating values.

The important DFM rule is:

> **Specify the required finished-hole result and use the intended fabricator's drilling/plating rules rather than manually assuming one fixed drill compensation.**

Detailed solder-fill acceptance belongs in Chapter 4.

## 3.7.2 - Annular Ring Is Manufacturing Margin Around the Hole

An **annular ring** is the copper surrounding a plated hole on a conductive layer.

For an ideal centered circular geometry:

$$
a_{\text{nominal}}
=
\frac{D_p-D_h}{2}
$$

or equivalently:

$$
D_p = D_h + 2a_{\text{nominal}}
$$

where:

- $D_p$ = pad or land diameter;
- $D_h$ = the hole/drill diameter used by the applicable design or fabrication rule.
- $a_{\text{nominal}}$ = nominal annular-ring width.

The primary instructional material uses:

- **0.15 mm** as a representative minimum annular-ring value;
- **0.20-0.25 mm** as a preferred design range when space allows.

These are useful **DFM review values**, not universal requirements across every supplier, copper weight, layer count, or product class.

### Example: pad diameter from hole and ring

Suppose:

- hole/drill value defined by the example rule = **0.30 mm**;
- selected nominal annular ring = **0.20 mm**.

Then:

$$
D_p
=
0.30 + 2(0.20)
=
0.70\ \text{mm}
$$

## 3.7.3 - Nominal Ring and Realized Ring Are Not the Same

A perfectly centered CAD drawing does not guarantee a perfectly centered manufactured hole.

Real fabrication includes:

- drill-position variation;
- layer-to-layer registration variation;
- plating and etching variation.

A useful first-order screening model is:

$$
a_{\text{min}}
\approx
a_{\text{nominal}} - e
$$

where $e$ represents the combined relative pad-to-hole offset in the worst direction.

For example, if:

- nominal annular ring = **0.20 mm**;
- relative offset = **0.05 mm**;

then:

$$
a_{\text{min}}
\approx
0.20 - 0.05
=
0.15\ \text{mm}
$$

The design started with preferred margin and moved toward the common 0.15 mm reference value after variation.

By contrast, a design that begins with only **0.15 mm nominal ring** would fall to approximately **0.10 mm** under the same simplified 0.05 mm offset.

That is why:

> **Preferred annular-ring margin can matter even when the nominal geometry already meets a stated minimum.**

Actual acceptance depends on the applicable supplier/process and product requirements.

## 3.7.4 - Via Size Is More Than the Hole Diameter

A through via combines:

- a drilled/plated barrel;
- pads or lands on connected layers;
- annular-ring margin;
- clearances or antipads on nonconnected layers.

The primary instructional material gives **0.20-0.30 mm** as a useful routine mechanical-via drill range.

Current mainstream fabrication can support mechanical drills down to about **0.15 mm** in some standard/advanced services, but values below about **0.20 mm** can move into tighter or extra-cost processing depending on board thickness and supplier.

> **A quoted minimum drill size is not automatically a good default design size.**

A larger via can provide more manufacturing margin, but larger pads and clearances also consume routing area.

### Via pad and surrounding copper

For each via, review:

- pad diameter and annular ring;
- connection to the intended trace or plane;
- clearance from nonconnected copper;
- available routing space;
- board thickness and aspect ratio.

A via that is electrically connected to one layer may need an **antipad** or clearance through a nearby plane that is not connected to that via.

The exact clearance belongs to the applicable net/rule set.

---

## 3.7.5 - Via Aspect Ratio Couples Drill Size to Board Thickness

A small drill can be easy on a thin board and difficult on a thick board.

A useful through-hole aspect-ratio definition is:

$$
AR = \frac{T_b}{D_d}
$$

where:

- $AR$ = aspect ratio;
- $T_b$ = board thickness;
- $D_d$ = production drill/hole diameter used by the fabricator for the capability definition.

For mainstream rigid-board work, a useful engineering-scale reference is:

- **8:1**: routine through-hole geometry;
- near **10:1**: tighter, more process-dependent geometry.

For a **1.6 mm** board with a **0.20 mm** mechanical via drill:

$$
AR = \frac{1.6}{0.20} = 8:1
$$

For the same board with a **0.15 mm** drill:

$$
AR = \frac{1.6}{0.15} \approx 10.7:1
$$

The second design uses a smaller drill, but the deeper/narrower hole is more difficult to clean, metallize, and plate consistently.

This is why via size should be chosen from the intended fabricator's capability rather than from routing density alone.

## 3.7.6 - Current and Thermal Implications of Via Geometry

Via geometry also affects electrical and thermal performance.

At recognition level:

- increasing conductive barrel area can reduce via resistance;
- multiple vias can share current or heat flow;
- thicker barrel plating can increase conductive cross-section;
- larger via pads and antipads consume more routing/plane area.

A statement such as **"larger vias carry more current"** is therefore incomplete.

Current and thermal behavior also depends on:

- plating thickness;
- copper connections;
- number of vias;
- temperature rise;
- layer geometry;
- surrounding copper.

Detailed current-capacity and thermal-via design are developed later in the book.

## 3.7.7 - Via-in-Pad Requires an Intentional Process

A via placed directly in a solderable component pad can create a solder path into the via barrel.

For an ordinary open through-via in a pad, molten solder can wick or drain into the hole during assembly. This can reduce the solder available at the intended joint or contribute to voiding and uneven standoff.

    width="900"
    alt="Side-by-side cross-sections compare an open through via located in a component pad with a filled and capped via-in-pad. The open via shows a path for molten solder to wick into the barrel, while the filled and capped construction presents a closed, planar solderable surface."
  >
  <p><em>Figure 3.7.4 – An open via in a solderable pad can provide a path for solder loss into the barrel. A properly specified filled/capped or plated-over via-in-pad uses additional fabrication steps to create a controlled solderable surface.</em></p>
</div>

For fine-pitch BGA and similar applications, **filled and plated-over/capped via-in-pad** is a common controlled solution when routing density requires the via to be in the pad.

However:

> **Via-in-pad is not automatically wrong, and filling/capping is not the only possible package-specific strategy.**

Some exposed-pad designs intentionally use open, tented, or mask-controlled thermal vias with package-specific stencil and solder-mask guidance.

The correct DFM question is:

> **Is the via-in-pad construction intentionally designed and supported by the selected PCB fabrication and assembly process?**

Do not use an ordinary open via in a solderable pad by accident.

Detailed solder-paste aperture design and reflow behavior belong in Chapter 4.

## 3.7.8 - Thermal Relief Around Plated Holes and Vias

When a plated through-hole pad connects directly to a large copper plane, the plane can remove heat from the joint during soldering.

A **thermal relief** uses narrower copper spokes between the pad and plane so the pad can heat more locally during assembly.

From a DFM perspective:

- thermal relief can improve solderability of ordinary plane-connected THT pads;
- a solid connection may be required for high-current, grounding, mechanical, or thermal reasons;
- the electrical/thermal function and the assembly process must be considered together.

Chapter 2 introduced the physical thermal-relief structure, so another duplicate figure is not needed here.

Detailed soldering behavior belongs in Chapter 4.

A compact DFM comparison is:

| More robust default | More process-sensitive condition |
|---|---|
| THT finished hole selected from maximum lead size plus verified insertion allowance | Hole chosen only from nominal lead size with little tolerance margin |
| Annular ring above the selected supplier's limiting capability | Nominal annular ring designed exactly at a limiting value |
| Via drill selected together with board thickness | Smallest available drill selected without checking aspect ratio |
| Via placed beside a solderable pad when routing space allows | Open via placed unintentionally inside a solderable pad |
| Thermal relief used where solderability needs it and function allows | Large plane connection used without considering assembly heating |

## 3.7.9 - Applied Hole and Via Review

Use the applicable package data and supplier capability rather than treating the values below as universal limits.

| Design observation | DFM interpretation | Typical response |
|---|---|---|
| 0.50 mm THT lead in a 0.55 mm finished hole | Only 0.05 mm diametral allowance in the example rule set | Increase the finished hole or verify package/process-specific fit |
| 0.50 mm THT lead in a 0.70 mm finished hole | 0.20 mm diametral allowance | Within the representative instructional range; still verify actual lead tolerance and supplier hole tolerance |
| 0.30 mm hole with a 0.60 mm pad | 0.15 mm nominal annular ring | At the common instructional minimum; little extra design margin |
| 0.30 mm hole with a 0.70 mm pad | 0.20 mm nominal annular ring | Within the preferred instructional range |
| 0.20 mm via drill through a 1.6 mm board | 8:1 aspect ratio | Routine engineering-scale reference for many rigid-board processes |
| 0.15 mm via drill through a 1.6 mm board | About 10.7:1 aspect ratio | Tighter/process-dependent; verify the fabricator rather than assuming the small drill alone is acceptable |
| Open through via located in an ordinary solderable pad | Solder-loss/voiding risk | Move the via or specify a controlled via-in-pad construction |
| Filled/capped/plated-over via-in-pad specified for a fine-pitch package | Intentional advanced construction | Verify fill/cap/plating requirements with fabricator and assembler |
| Plane-connected THT pad is difficult to heat during soldering | Excessive local heat sinking may be present | Consider thermal relief if compatible with electrical/thermal requirements |

The practical review sequence is:

> **Define function -> identify finished-hole/drill requirement -> check annular ring -> check aspect ratio and copper clearances -> check special via processing -> verify against the selected process.**

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- THT component fit is controlled by the **finished hole**, not simply the production drill;
- **0.15-0.30 mm** is a useful representative THT lead-to-hole allowance range for ordinary soldered components, not a universal rule;
- annular ring provides conductive area and manufacturing margin around a plated hole;
- **0.15 mm** is a useful common minimum annular-ring reference, while **0.20-0.25 mm** provides more design margin when space allows;
- nominal annular ring must be reviewed together with drill-position and layer-registration variation;
- **0.20-0.30 mm** is a useful routine mechanical-via drill scale, while smaller drills may require tighter or more expensive processing;
- via drill diameter and board thickness must be reviewed together through aspect ratio;
- via electrical/thermal capability depends on more than hole diameter alone;
- open via-in-pad structures can create solder-loss/voiding risk, while intentionally filled/capped or other package-specific via-in-pad constructions can be manufacturable;
- thermal relief is a DFM tradeoff between solderability and electrical/thermal function.

Section 3.8 next examines **trace and copper geometry**, including narrow necks, copper slivers, pad-entry geometry, teardrops where appropriate, and acute copper geometries with modern-process context.

### Fresh technical verification

Verification cycle performed against current public manufacturer/fabricator guidance and the approved reference hierarchy.

#### THT lead-to-hole allowance

The primary material's **+0.15-0.30 mm** diametral allowance remains a defensible instructional range.

A current PCB-design explanation based on IPC land-pattern density concepts gives approximately:

- +0.25 mm for a more generous density level;
- +0.20 mm for a nominal density level;
- +0.15 mm for a tighter density level.

Therefore the public OER retains +0.15-0.30 mm as an instructional planning range while requiring package/process verification.

The public example intentionally uses:

`0.50 mm lead + 0.20 mm clearance = 0.70 mm finished hole`.

#### Finished-hole tolerance and drilling/plating

Current Eurocircuits guidance:

- plated-hole standard tolerance is commonly about ±0.10 mm for holes <=4 mm;
- tighter plated-hole tolerance of about ±0.05 mm is available;
- production holes are drilled oversize to achieve the required finished hole after plating.

Current PCBWay capability gives a representative PTH finished-hole tolerance of approximately ±0.08 mm.

Conclusion:

- finished-hole result is the correct design focus for THT fit;
- drill compensation should be left to the selected fabricator unless its data format explicitly requires otherwise.

#### Barrel copper

The primary instructional material uses **20-25 μm** as a typical-awareness scale.

Eurocircuits currently publishes **20 μm minimum copper thickness** for hole-wall copper in its standard table.

Conclusion:

- 20-25 μm remains useful engineering-scale awareness;
- do not present it as a universal required finished thickness.

#### Annular ring

Current published capability examples include:

- PCBWay: **0.15 mm** minimum annular ring;
- JLCPCB rigid multilayer, 1 oz: **0.15 mm absolute minimum**, **0.20 mm recommended or above**;
- JLCPCB rigid 2-layer, 1 oz: **0.18 mm absolute minimum**, **0.25 mm recommended or above**;
- Eurocircuits classifications use process/class-dependent inner/outer annular-ring values and explicitly recommend design margin above class limits.

Conclusion:

- **0.15 mm** is a useful common minimum reference;
- **0.20-0.25 mm** is a well-supported preferred instructional range;
- layer count, copper thickness, service/class, and supplier matter.

#### Via drill and aspect ratio

Current PCBWay capability:

- mechanical drill capability can reach **0.15 mm**;
- holes below **0.20 mm** can carry extra cost;
- 0.20 mm drill with 1.6 mm maximum board thickness corresponds to **8:1**;
- drill below 0.20 mm with aspect ratio >=10 is identified as more difficult.

Current Eurocircuits standard aspect-ratio guidance uses **1:8**.

Conclusion:

- 0.20-0.30 mm remains a good routine instructional drill range;
- 0.15 mm is available but should be treated as tighter/process-dependent;
- the public 8:1 versus 10.7:1 examples are technically sound.

#### Via-in-pad

Current TI guidance for fine-pitch BGA work states that plated through-hole via-in-pad structures should be filled and plated over to prevent solder wicking and trapped-air effects.

Current Microchip QFN guidance shows a necessary nuance:

- thermal vias in exposed pads can wick solder;
- filling or top-side tenting can be used;
- other package-specific strategies are possible.

Conclusion:

- accidental open via-in-pad beneath an ordinary solderable termination remains a strong DFM warning;
- filled/capped/plated-over via-in-pad is a common controlled advanced solution;
- the lecture's `filled and capped only` statement is too absolute for every thermal-pad/package application.

#### Thermal relief

Chapter 2 already established the physical solid-versus-thermal-relief tradeoff.

Section 3.7 should only apply the concept as a DFM decision:

- use thermal relief when solderability benefits and electrical/thermal requirements allow;
- retain solid connection when functional requirements dominate.

No new numerical thermal-relief rule is introduced.
