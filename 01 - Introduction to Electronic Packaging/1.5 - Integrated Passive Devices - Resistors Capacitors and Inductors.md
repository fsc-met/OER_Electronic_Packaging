# 1.5 - Integrated Passive Devices: Resistors, Capacitors, and Inductors

Section 1.4 introduced **active semiconductor devices**, especially BJTs and MOSFETs. Those devices can control current and provide switching or amplification.

A complete electronic circuit also requires **passive components**.

Three of the most important passive components are:

- **resistors**;
- **capacitors**;
- **inductors**.

These devices are familiar as discrete parts mounted on a PCB, but their electrical functions can also be created as patterned structures within or above a semiconductor die.

The purpose of this section is to answer a practical question:

> **How can resistor, capacitor, and inductor functions be physically realized on an integrated circuit?**

The goal is not to survey every passive-device technology or teach detailed passive-component design. Instead, this section introduces the major physical ideas and shows **one representative integrated structure for each passive device**.

Later sections of this book may examine resistor, capacitor, and inductor technologies in greater detail.

> **Engineering Perspective:** An integrated passive device is not normally a tiny packaged resistor, capacitor, or inductor hidden inside the silicon. Its electrical behavior comes from the materials and geometry of structures fabricated as part of the IC process.

## 1.5.1 What Makes a Component Passive?

A **passive component** does not provide electrical power gain in the way an active transistor circuit can.

Instead, passive components primarily:

- **dissipate energy**, as a resistor does;
- **store energy in an electric field**, as a capacitor does;
- **store energy in a magnetic field**, as an inductor does.

Passive components are essential for functions such as:

- establishing voltages and currents;
- biasing circuits;
- filtering;
- timing;
- energy storage;
- impedance matching;
- noise reduction;
- RF tuning.

The same schematic symbol may represent either a discrete component on a PCB or an integrated structure on a die.

The electrical function can be similar even though the physical implementation is very different.

> **Key Idea:** Circuit schematics describe electrical behavior. IC fabrication must create a physical material structure that produces that behavior.

## 1.5.2 Integrated Resistors

A resistor limits current and produces a voltage drop when current flows through it.

For a uniform resistive material, the basic relationship between resistance and geometry is:

$$
R=\rho\frac{L}{A}
$$

where:

- $R$ is resistance;
- $\rho$ is the material resistivity;
- $L$ is the current-path length;
- $A$ is the cross-sectional area of the current path.

This relationship explains the physical idea behind an integrated resistor.

Resistance is created by forming a controlled current path from a material with known electrical resistivity. The final resistance depends on both the **material properties** and the **geometry** of the path.

For example:

- a longer path generally produces more resistance;
- a narrower path generally produces more resistance;
- a material with higher resistivity produces more resistance.

### Common Integrated Resistor Configurations

Integrated resistors can be implemented in several ways depending on the semiconductor process.

Common examples include:

- **diffused resistors**, formed from a doped semiconductor region;
- **polysilicon resistors**, formed from patterned polysilicon;
- **thin-film resistors**, formed from a deposited resistive material;
- other process-specific resistive structures.

Each configuration offers different resistance ranges, tolerances, temperature behavior, area requirements, and fabrication constraints.

For this introductory chapter, Figure 1.5.1 shows **one representative example: a diffused resistor**.

A **diffused resistor** is formed by creating a doped region with controlled electrical resistivity in the semiconductor. Metal contacts connect to the two ends of this region and form the resistor terminals. When a voltage is applied across the terminals, current flows laterally through the doped path. Because the path has finite resistivity, it produces electrical resistance. The resistance depends on the resistive material and on the path geometry: a longer or narrower path generally produces a larger resistance. An insulating oxide separates the resistive region from surface conductors except where contact openings are intentionally provided.

The term *diffused resistor* is commonly used for this type of structure. Depending on the semiconductor process, the doped region may be formed using diffusion, ion implantation followed by thermal processing, or another process-specific doping method.

### Why Integrated Resistors Are Useful

Integrated resistors are commonly used in:

- analog bias networks;
- feedback circuits;
- voltage dividers;
- sensor interfaces;
- reference circuits;
- mixed-signal circuits.

They are especially useful when the required resistance can be created economically within the available semiconductor process and die area.

### Practical Limitations

An integrated resistor is not identical to a discrete precision resistor.

Its usable value and accuracy can be influenced by:

- fabrication tolerances;
- temperature;
- resistor geometry;
- material properties;
- parasitic capacitance to nearby structures;
- available die area;
- allowable current and power dissipation.

For applications requiring high power, very tight absolute tolerance, or large physical size, an external discrete resistor may still be more practical.

Detailed resistor structures, materials, and performance tradeoffs may be discussed later in the book.

## 1.5.3 Integrated Capacitors

A capacitor stores electrical energy in an **electric field** between conductive regions separated by an insulating dielectric.

For a simplified parallel-plate capacitor:

$$
C=\frac{\varepsilon A}{d}
$$

where:

- $C$ is capacitance;
- $\varepsilon$ is the permittivity of the dielectric;
- $A$ is the effective overlapping conductor area;
- $d$ is the dielectric thickness.

This relationship shows why capacitor construction is naturally compatible with layered IC fabrication.

### Common Integrated Capacitor Configurations

Integrated capacitors can be implemented using several structures.

Common examples include:

- **MOS capacitors**, which use a gate electrode, dielectric, and semiconductor region;
- **metal-insulator-metal (MIM) capacitors**, which use two conductive layers separated by a dielectric;
- **junction capacitors**, which use the capacitance associated with a semiconductor junction;
- other process-specific capacitor structures.

The appropriate configuration depends on factors such as:

- required capacitance;
- operating voltage;
- linearity;
- leakage;
- available die area;
- fabrication process.

For this introductory chapter, Figure 1.5.2 shows **one representative example: a MOS capacitor**.

A **MOS capacitor** stores electrical energy by separating charge across a thin insulating dielectric. When a voltage is applied between the gate and the semiconductor body, charge builds up on the gate and an opposite net charge is induced near the semiconductor surface. Because the gate dielectric blocks normal DC current, the separated charges create an electric field through the dielectric and the structure behaves as a capacitor. The amount of capacitance depends on the device geometry, dielectric properties, and applied bias.

The MOS capacitor shown here is only one possible implementation. MIM capacitors and other structures are also widely used depending on the process and circuit requirements.

### Why Integrated Capacitors Are Useful

On-chip capacitors can be used for:

- analog signal processing;
- filtering;
- timing;
- charge storage;
- data converters;
- RF circuits;
- compensation;
- local decoupling or noise control, depending on the circuit and process.

### Why Large Capacitors Often Remain Off-Chip

Capacitance increases with conductor area and decreases as dielectric thickness becomes smaller.

This creates an important limitation:

> **Large capacitance values can require substantial die area.**

Die area is expensive, and dielectric thickness and voltage capability are constrained by the fabrication technology.

For applications requiring:

- large capacitance;
- high voltage;
- substantial stored energy;
- high current capability;

a package-level, substrate-level, or PCB-mounted capacitor may be more practical.

Detailed integrated-capacitor technologies and design tradeoffs may be discussed later in the book.

## 1.5.4 Integrated Inductors

An inductor stores energy in a **magnetic field** produced by current.

Unlike a resistor or capacitor, useful inductance depends strongly on conductor geometry, loop area, magnetic coupling, and frequency-dependent effects.

On an IC, an inductor is typically created from patterned metal conductors in the interconnect layers.

> **Important:** A typical on-chip inductor is not a doped coil carved into silicon. Its inductive behavior comes primarily from the geometry of a patterned conductive path and the magnetic field produced by current flowing through that path.

### Common Integrated Inductor Configurations

Integrated inductors can be implemented using several geometries.

Common examples include:

- **planar spiral inductors**;
- **multilayer or stacked spiral inductors**;
- other patterned-metal loop structures designed for RF or high-frequency applications.

Different geometries trade off:

- inductance;
- conductor resistance;
- quality factor;
- parasitic capacitance;
- coupling;
- die area;
- fabrication complexity.

For this introductory chapter, Figure 1.5.3 shows **one representative example: a planar spiral metal inductor**.

A **planar spiral inductor** is formed by patterning a metal conductor into a spiral path on the chip. When current flows through the spiral, it produces a magnetic field mainly perpendicular to the plane of the spiral, allowing the structure to store energy magnetically and behave as an inductor. Because the inner end of the spiral is not directly accessible from the top metal layer, a **via and lower-metal underpass** are commonly used to connect it to the second terminal.

### Where Integrated Inductors Are Used

Integrated inductors are especially associated with:

- RF circuits;
- oscillators;
- resonant networks;
- impedance-matching networks;
- high-frequency filters;
- communication circuits.

They are more application-dependent than integrated resistors and capacitors.

### Why Integrated Inductors Are More Difficult

A useful inductor can consume significant die area.

Its performance can also be affected by:

- resistance of the metal conductor;
- parasitic capacitance;
- losses in the semiconductor substrate;
- coupling to nearby conductors;
- magnetic coupling to neighboring structures;
- operating frequency.

One important measure of inductor performance is the **quality factor**, or $Q$, which describes the relationship between energy stored and energy lost.

For many applications requiring:

- larger inductance;
- high current;
- high magnetic-energy storage;
- high $Q$;

a discrete, package-integrated, or substrate-integrated inductor may be more practical than an on-chip spiral.

Detailed integrated-inductor structures and RF design considerations may be discussed later in the book.

## 1.5.5 Why Not Integrate Every Passive Component?

If passive components can be fabricated on a chip, it may seem reasonable to place every resistor, capacitor, and inductor directly on the die.

In practice, engineers must make tradeoffs.

### Die Area

On-chip structures consume semiconductor or interconnect area.

Large resistors, large capacitors, and especially large inductors can occupy valuable die area that could otherwise be used for active circuitry.

### Electrical Value and Performance

The IC process limits:

- available materials;
- layer thicknesses;
- conductor resistance;
- dielectric properties;
- voltage capability;
- current capability.

A discrete component may provide a much larger capacitance, inductance, power rating, or voltage rating in a more practical form.

### Accuracy and Tolerance

Some passive values vary with fabrication process and temperature.

Circuit designers may use matching, calibration, trimming, or external precision components when tighter performance is required.

### Parasitic Effects

Integrated structures interact with their surroundings.

Examples include:

- resistor capacitance to the substrate;
- capacitor series resistance and leakage;
- inductor resistance and substrate loss;
- capacitive and magnetic coupling between neighboring structures.

These effects become increasingly important at high frequency.

### Cost and System-Level Optimization

Integrating a passive can reduce:

- external component count;
- PCB area;
- assembly operations;
- some package and PCB interconnections.

However, increasing die area or adding specialized process steps can increase semiconductor cost.

The best solution therefore depends on the complete system rather than on a simple rule that integration is always better.

> **Engineering Judgment:** Integrate a passive when the electrical performance, available process, die area, cost, and system requirements make integration advantageous. Use an external or package-level passive when it provides a better overall solution.

## Packaging Implications of Integrated Passive Devices

Integrated passives affect packaging because the choice of **where a passive function is implemented** changes the physical interconnect structure of the product.

When a passive device is integrated on the die:

- fewer separate PCB components may be required;
- some external solder joints and interconnect paths can be eliminated;
- electrical paths may become shorter;
- certain parasitic effects associated with long package or PCB connections may be reduced.

However, moving passive functions onto the die can also:

- consume die area;
- add process complexity;
- concentrate additional electrical and thermal interactions near active circuitry;
- create coupling between nearby structures.

When the passive remains outside the die, its package, placement, solder joints, PCB traces, vias, and reference structures become part of the electrical behavior.

This leads to an important packaging principle:

> **The schematic value of a resistor, capacitor, or inductor is only part of the real circuit. Its physical implementation and interconnections also influence system performance.**

Later chapters develop these physical effects in more detail through discussions of parasitic resistance, capacitance, inductance, power integrity, signal integrity, EMI, and thermal behavior.

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- resistors, capacitors, and inductors are important passive circuit elements;
- passive components can exist as discrete PCB components or as structures integrated into an IC process;
- integrated resistors can be implemented as diffused, polysilicon, thin-film, or other process-specific structures;
- Figure 1.5.1 uses a **diffused resistor** as one representative integrated-resistor example;
- integrated capacitors can be implemented as MOS, MIM, junction, or other process-specific structures;
- Figure 1.5.2 uses a **MOS capacitor** as one representative integrated-capacitor example;
- integrated inductors can use planar spiral, multilayer/stacked spiral, or other patterned-metal geometries;
- Figure 1.5.3 uses a **planar spiral metal inductor** as one representative integrated-inductor example;
- integrated inductors are more application-dependent and are especially important in RF and other high-frequency circuits;
- large capacitance, large inductance, high power, high voltage, or other demanding requirements may make off-chip components more practical;
- passive-device integration is a tradeoff involving electrical performance, die area, process capability, parasitics, and cost;
- physical implementation matters because interconnections and surrounding structures become part of the real electrical system.
