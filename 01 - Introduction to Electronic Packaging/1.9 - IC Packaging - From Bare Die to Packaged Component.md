# 1.9 - IC Packaging: From Bare Die to Packaged Component

Section 1.8 ended with individual **bare IC dies** separated from a processed semiconductor wafer. Each die may already contain millions or billions of microscopic electronic features and may have passed wafer-level electrical tests.

However, a bare die is usually **not yet a practical component for ordinary PCB assembly**. Specialized products can use direct bare-die attachment such as chip-on-board or other controlled die-level assembly, but those approaches require dedicated handling, interconnection, protection, and process controls.

The die is:

- physically small and fragile;
- difficult to handle safely;
- equipped with very small I/O pads or bump locations;
- unable to connect directly to ordinary PCB feature sizes without an interconnection strategy;
- vulnerable to mechanical damage and environmental contamination;
- dependent on an engineered path for heat removal.

**IC packaging** converts the bare semiconductor die into a component that can be handled, electrically connected, protected, cooled, tested, and assembled into a larger electronic system.

The central transformation is:

> **bare IC die -> IC packaging -> packaged IC component**

This is the first major packaging transformation after semiconductor fabrication.

> **Critical Distinction:** IC packaging does not create the PCB. The packaged IC and the bare PCB are manufactured in separate streams and come together later during PCB assembly.

## 1.9.1 Why Does a Bare IC Die Need Packaging?

The electronic circuit may already be complete on the die, but the die still needs several physical functions before it can be used conveniently in a product.

### Electrical fan-out and interconnection

The electrical connection features on a die can be much smaller and more closely spaced than the connection features used at the PCB level.

The package provides an interconnection system that can:

- connect die pads or bumps to larger package terminals;
- redistribute signals, power, and ground;
- convert fine die pitch into a more manufacturable board-level pitch;
- provide external leads, pads, lands, pins, or solder balls.

The package therefore performs **redistribution** or **fan-out** of electrical connections, depending on the package structure and terminology used.

Here, **fan-out** refers generally to expanding or redistributing connections to a larger pitch. This should not be confused with **fan-out wafer-level packaging**, a specific packaging approach introduced later in this section.

### Mechanical support and handling

A bare silicon die is thin and brittle.

Packaging provides:

- support during handling and assembly;
- a controlled attachment surface;
- protection from direct mechanical contact;
- a practical component body that automated equipment can pick, place, inspect, and test.

### Environmental protection

The die surface and its fine interconnections can be damaged by:

- particles;
- moisture;
- contamination;
- handling;
- mechanical contact.

A package may use:

- mold compound;
- encapsulant;
- lid;
- seal;
- underfill;
- other protective materials

to reduce these risks.

The exact protection level depends on the package technology and product environment.

### Thermal path

Heat is generated inside active semiconductor regions.

The package provides part of the heat-flow path from the die toward:

- package leads or exposed pads;
- a package substrate;
- a lid or heat spreader;
- the PCB;
- a heat sink or other cooling hardware.

A package therefore has an important thermal role even though the heat originates inside the semiconductor die.

### Testability and manufacturability

Packaging creates a repeatable physical interface for:

- final electrical test;
- handling;
- marking and traceability;
- PCB assembly;
- inspection;
- shipping and storage.

> **Engineering Perspective:** The IC package is not simply a protective shell. It is an engineered electrical, mechanical, thermal, and manufacturing interface between the semiconductor die and the rest of the product.

## 1.9.2 Basic Anatomy of an IC Package

Although package structures vary widely, many conventional IC packages contain the same basic functional elements.

### Semiconductor die

The **die** is the fabricated semiconductor chip that performs the electronic function.

### Die attach

The die must be mechanically attached to some supporting structure.

Depending on the package, the die may be attached to:

- a leadframe die pad;
- a package substrate;
- an interposer;
- another die;
- another package-level structure.

The attachment material may also contribute to the thermal path.

### Leadframe or package substrate

Two common package support/interconnect structures are:

- **leadframe** - a patterned metal structure used in many leaded and leadless packages;
- **package substrate** - a routing structure, often multilayer, used in many BGA, CSP, flip-chip, and high-I/O packages.

A leadframe can provide:

- die support;
- internal electrical leads;
- external terminals;
- thermal conduction.

A package substrate can provide:

- fine-pitch routing near the die;
- redistribution to larger terminal pitch;
- power and ground routing;
- multiple routing layers;
- connection to solder balls, lands, or other external terminals.

### Internal die-to-package interconnection

The die must be electrically connected to the package structure.

Common methods include:

- **wire bonding**;
- **flip-chip bonding using solder bumps or copper pillars**.

These are **internal die-to-package interconnection methods**. They are not the same thing as the external package family.

### Encapsulation, mold compound, or lid

The die and internal interconnections may be protected by:

- molded epoxy;
- encapsulant;
- underfill;
- a lid;
- another protective structure.

### External package terminals

The package then presents a larger-scale interface to the next assembly level.

External connections may include:

- through-hole pins;
- gull-wing leads;
- J-leads;
- flat lands or exposed pads;
- solder balls;
- other package-specific terminals.

> **Key Idea:** A package connects two very different physical scales: microscopic die-level connections and much larger board-level interconnections.

## 1.9.3 Common Die-to-Package Interconnection and Packaging Approaches

The method used to connect the die to the package, and in some cases the broader packaging approach, strongly affects:

- electrical path length;
- I/O density;
- package size;
- thermal design;
- manufacturing cost;
- inspection;
- reliability.

Two major methods are especially important for introductory packaging study: **wire bonding** and **flip-chip bonding**.

### Wire bonding

Wire bonding is one of the most widely used IC interconnection methods.

A typical wire-bond package has:

1. a die attached **face-up** to a leadframe or package substrate;
2. accessible die bond pads on the top surface, often arranged near the die perimeter or in other process-specific layouts;
3. fine metal wires connecting die pads to package bond fingers, substrate pads, or leadframe terminals;
4. an encapsulant, molded body, or lid protecting the die and wires.

Common wire materials include copper, gold, and aluminum, depending on package technology and application.

Advantages include:

- mature manufacturing infrastructure;
- extensive production experience;
- broad package compatibility;
- relatively low cost for many applications.

Practical limitations include:

- longer interconnect paths than direct flip-chip connections;
- added resistance and inductance;
- wire-loop height;
- routing and I/O-density limitations as pad count increases;
- mechanical sensitivity of fine wire bonds.

Wire bonding remains important in:

- analog ICs;
- microcontrollers;
- sensors;
- memory;
- automotive devices;
- power devices;
- many general-purpose ICs.

### Flip-chip bonding

In a **flip-chip** package, the active surface of the die faces downward toward the package substrate or other receiving structure.

Electrical connections are formed directly between the die and the substrate using structures such as:

- solder bumps;
- copper pillars with solder;
- other fine-pitch bump structures.

A simplified sequence is:

1. create bump or pillar connection structures on the die;
2. flip the die so its active surface faces the substrate;
3. align die bumps with substrate pads;
4. join the connections, commonly by solder reflow or another bonding process;
5. add **underfill** where required to support the joint structure and redistribute thermomechanical stress.

Important advantages can include:

- shorter electrical interconnections;
- higher I/O density;
- area-array connection rather than only peripheral wire-bond pads;
- lower interconnect inductance;
- potential for compact package structures.

Challenges include:

- more demanding bump and substrate fabrication;
- tight alignment requirements;
- hidden interconnections;
- underfill/process-control requirements where used;
- more complex inspection and rework.

Flip-chip is widely used in high-performance processors, graphics devices, communication ICs, and many high-I/O BGA and CSP packages.

### Wafer-level and chip-scale packaging

Some package structures are created largely or partly **before the wafer is diced**.

In **wafer-level packaging (WLP)**, packaging-related structures may be formed across the wafer, such as:

- redistribution layers (RDL);
- under-bump metallurgy (UBM);
- solder bumps or copper posts;
- protective or dielectric layers.

The wafer is then singulated into individual packaged devices.

A **wafer-level chip-scale package (WLCSP)** can be close to the physical size of the die because it may not require a conventional leadframe or package substrate extending far beyond the die outline.

WLP and WLCSP can provide:

- very small package size;
- short electrical paths;
- efficient wafer-level batch processing.

They also create challenges involving:

- fine PCB pitch;
- board-level thermomechanical strain;
- inspection;
- handling;
- limited fan-out area for some designs.

**Fan-out wafer-level packaging** extends redistribution beyond the original die area by embedding one or more dies in a reconstituted carrier or mold structure before forming redistribution layers.

These advanced forms are introduced here only at the recognition level.

> **Terminology Note:** **Wire bond** and **flip-chip** are die-to-package interconnection methods, while **wafer-level packaging** is a broader packaging approach. **DIP, QFP, QFN, and BGA** describe package families, while **CSP** is primarily a package-size classification. **SMT** and **through-hole technology** describe PCB assembly approaches. These terms should not be treated as interchangeable.

## 1.9.4 Common IC Package Families

Many package families have developed because no single package can simultaneously optimize:

- cost;
- I/O count;
- size;
- electrical performance;
- thermal performance;
- manufacturability;
- inspection;
- reliability.

The goal here is **recognition and packaging implications**, not detailed package design.

### DIP - Dual In-Line Package

A **DIP** has two parallel rows of through-hole leads.

Characteristics:

- easy to recognize;
- relatively large pitch;
- convenient for sockets and prototyping;
- easy visual access to leads;
- larger board area than most modern surface-mount packages.

DIP is historically important and still appears in selected applications.

### SOIC and related leaded surface-mount packages

A **SOIC** is a surface-mount package with gull-wing leads along two opposite sides.

Related package families include:

- SOP;
- SSOP;
- TSSOP;
- MSOP.

These packages provide:

- smaller size than traditional DIP;
- visible solder joints;
- straightforward SMT assembly for many applications;
- moderate I/O counts.

### QFP - Quad Flat Package

A **QFP** has gull-wing leads on all four sides.

Common variants include:

- LQFP;
- TQFP.

QFP allows more I/O connections than two-sided leaded packages while keeping solder joints visible around the package perimeter.

Practical concerns include:

- fine lead pitch;
- lead coplanarity;
- bent leads;
- solder bridging;
- board routing density.

### QFN and DFN - leadless packages

A **QFN** uses flat metal terminals around the underside perimeter rather than projecting gull-wing leads.

A **DFN** is a related package commonly used for lower terminal counts.

Many QFN/DFN packages also include an exposed thermal pad.

Advantages can include:

- small size;
- short electrical paths;
- low package inductance;
- good thermal conduction through an exposed pad;
- high board-area efficiency.

Challenges can include:

- solder-joint visibility;
- voiding beneath exposed pads;
- inspection and rework.

### BGA - Ball Grid Array

A **BGA** uses an array of solder balls on the underside of the package.

The package often uses a multilayer package substrate to route connections from the die to the solder-ball array.

Advantages include:

- high I/O count;
- area-array interconnection;
- efficient use of package area;
- good electrical routing capability.

Challenges include:

- solder joints hidden underneath the package;
- dependence on X-ray or other inspection methods for many defects;
- more demanding rework;
- thermomechanical stress in solder joints;
- PCB escape-routing requirements.

A **PGA (Pin Grid Array)** is another area-array family in which pins rather than solder balls form the external connections. PGA packages are less common in many modern high-volume PCB assemblies but remain useful recognition context.

### CSP - Chip-Scale Package

A **chip-scale package (CSP)** has a body size close to the die size.

CSP is a size/classification concept rather than one single internal construction method.

A CSP may use:

- wire bonding;
- flip-chip;
- a small package substrate;
- wafer-level processing.

WLCSP is one important chip-scale approach.

### Related discrete semiconductor packages

Discrete semiconductor devices also use packaging structures such as:

- axial and radial diode packages;
- SOT packages;
- TO packages;
- power QFN/DFN packages;
- power modules.

Although these are not IC package families in the strict sense, they perform many of the same packaging functions:

- electrical connection;
- protection;
- mechanical support;
- thermal management;
- board-level assembly compatibility.

### Related sockets and connectors

**IC sockets** and **electrical connectors** are also related board-level interconnect hardware, but they are not themselves IC package families.

- An IC socket provides a removable mechanical/electrical interface between a packaged IC and a PCB.
- A connector provides an interface between boards, cables, modules, or other system elements.

They are mentioned here because they influence handling, assembly, serviceability, contact reliability, board area, and signal integrity, but their detailed design is outside the scope of this section.

## 1.9.5 Package Type, Mounting Technology, and Internal Interconnection Are Different

Three packaging terms are often confused:

| Question | Example Categories | What It Describes |
|---|---|---|
| **What is the package family or size class?** | DIP, SOIC, QFP, QFN, BGA, CSP | Physical package form, external terminal arrangement, or package-size classification |
| **How is the package mounted to the PCB?** | Through-hole, SMT | Board-assembly method |
| **How is the die connected inside the package?** | Wire bond, flip-chip | Internal die-to-package interconnection |

For example:

> A **BGA** is a package family. It is normally assembled to a PCB using **SMT**, and the die inside the BGA may use either **wire bonding** or **flip-chip** interconnection.

Likewise:

> A **QFN** is a leadless surface-mount package family. Many QFNs use wire bonding, while some package designs use flip-chip interconnection.

This distinction is essential when reading package drawings, manufacturing specifications, and assembly requirements.

## 1.9.6 A Typical IC Packaging Manufacturing Process

IC packaging is not one universal manufacturing recipe.

The exact process depends on:

- package family;
- leadframe or substrate construction;
- wire-bond or flip-chip interconnection;
- number of dies;
- mold or lid structure;
- external terminals;
- electrical and thermal requirements.

However, a conventional packaging flow can be understood through several major stages.

### Step 1 - Receive and handle tested dies

The packaging process begins with dies that have completed semiconductor fabrication and appropriate wafer-level testing.

After dicing:

- selected dies are identified using wafer-map and test information;
- dies are picked and handled using controlled equipment;
- incoming visual or electrical screening may be performed depending on the manufacturing flow.

In some wafer-level packaging flows, important packaging operations occur **before** singulation, so this conventional die-first sequence should not be treated as universal.

### Step 2 - Die attach

The die is attached to:

- a leadframe die paddle;
- a package substrate;
- another carrier structure.

Die-attach materials may include:

- epoxy;
- conductive adhesive;
- solder;
- sintered materials;
- other package-specific attachment systems.

The attachment must provide adequate:

- mechanical support;
- alignment;
- thermal performance;
- reliability.

### Step 3 - Internal electrical interconnection

The die is connected using the selected technology.

For many **wire-bond packages**, die attach is a distinct operation completed before wire bonding. For **flip-chip packages**, die placement and electrical joining are commonly part of the flip-chip attachment/interconnection process rather than a separate conventional die-attach step.

For a **wire-bond package**:

> die attach -> wire bonding

For a **flip-chip package**:

> bump/pillar preparation -> die placement face-down -> bonding/reflow -> underfill where required

The exact sequence varies by technology.

### Step 4 - Encapsulation or protective closure

The die and internal interconnections are protected using a method such as:

- transfer molding;
- compression molding;
- glob-top encapsulation;
- package lid;
- other protective structures.

Plastic leadframe packages commonly use molded thermoset material. Molded protection should not automatically be interpreted as a hermetic seal.

### Step 5 - Form external package terminals

The next operation depends on package family.

Examples include:

- plating and trim/form for leaded leadframe packages;
- exposing underside lands for QFN/DFN packages;
- solder-ball attach and reflow for BGA packages;
- wafer-level bump/ball formation for WLCSP.

### Step 6 - Singulation where required

Packages manufactured in:

- leadframe strips;
- substrate panels;
- molded arrays;
- wafer or reconstituted-wafer format

must be separated into individual components at the appropriate point in the process.

### Step 7 - Marking, inspection, and final test

The completed package may undergo:

- visual inspection;
- dimensional inspection;
- X-ray or other internal inspection where appropriate;
- electrical test;
- functional test;
- burn-in or stress screening for selected products;
- package marking and traceability operations.

The final result is a **packaged IC component** ready for storage, shipment, and later PCB assembly.

> **Important:** The process branches according to package technology. Wire bonding and flip-chip are alternative internal-interconnection routes, and terminal formation differs among leaded packages, QFN/DFN, BGA, CSP, and wafer-level packages.

### Example: leadframe wire-bond package flow

A simplified leadframe package may follow:

> **die attach -> wire bond -> mold -> cure -> plate / trim / form -> mark -> final test**

### Example: substrate-based BGA flow

A simplified substrate-based BGA may follow:

> **die attach or flip-chip attach -> wire bond or bump interconnect -> underfill / mold cap as required -> ball attach -> reflow -> singulate -> mark / inspect / test**

These examples are representative only. Manufacturing details vary among package suppliers and products.

## 1.9.7 Why Package Choice Matters to PCB Assembly

Package selection affects much more than the appearance of the component.

### Assembly process

Package geometry determines:

- SMT versus through-hole assembly;
- stencil and solder-paste requirements;
- placement accuracy;
- reflow-process sensitivity;
- special handling requirements.

### Electrical performance

Package interconnections create:

- resistance;
- inductance;
- capacitance;
- coupling.

Shorter interconnects and optimized package routing can improve high-frequency and power-delivery behavior.

### Thermal path

Package construction determines important heat-flow paths through:

- exposed pads;
- leads;
- solder balls;
- package substrate;
- mold compound;
- lid or heat spreader.

### Mechanical reliability

Package and PCB materials expand differently with temperature.

Package:

- body size;
- terminal compliance;
- solder-joint geometry;
- substrate stiffness;
- thermal expansion

can affect board-level thermomechanical reliability.

### Inspection

Package style affects what can be inspected directly.

For example:

- QFP solder joints are largely visible around the perimeter;
- QFN/DFN joints are largely beneath the package body, although side fillets may be visible depending on terminal design and assembly;
- BGA solder joints are hidden underneath the package.

This difference influences the usefulness of:

- visual inspection;
- AOI;
- X-ray inspection;
- electrical test.

### Rework and service

Packages with accessible leads are often easier to inspect and rework than dense area-array packages.

### Board area and routing

Higher I/O packages may reduce package footprint but increase:

- fan-out routing difficulty;
- via requirements;
- layer-count pressure;
- escape-routing complexity.

These tradeoffs become important in later chapters on PCB design, manufacturability, assembly, electrical performance, thermal design, and reliability.

## 1.9.8 From Packaged IC to PCB Assembly

At the end of IC packaging, the result is a separate physical component.

The component stream is now:

> **semiconductor fabrication -> bare IC die -> IC packaging -> packaged IC component**

A separate manufacturing stream produces:

> **PCB materials -> PCB fabrication -> bare PCB**

Only later do these streams converge:

> **packaged ICs + discrete components + connectors + bare PCB -> PCB assembly -> PCBA**

This distinction prevents a common misunderstanding:

> **The IC package does not become the PCB. The packaged component is mounted onto a separately manufactured PCB.**

Section 1.10 follows that convergence.

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- a bare IC die is usually too fragile and too fine-pitch for ordinary PCB assembly;
- IC packaging provides electrical fan-out, mechanical support, environmental protection, thermal paths, manufacturability, and testability;
- common package support structures include **leadframes** and **package substrates**;
- **wire bonding** connects a face-up die to package terminals using fine metal wires;
- **flip-chip bonding** connects a face-down die directly through bumps or pillars;
- wafer-level packaging forms important package features before singulation and can produce very compact chip-scale packages;
- common external package families include DIP, SOIC, QFP, QFN/DFN, BGA, and CSP;
- **package family**, **PCB mounting method**, and **internal die interconnection** are different concepts;
- packaging manufacturing typically includes die attach, internal interconnection, protection, external-terminal formation, singulation where required, inspection, marking, and final test;
- package choice affects assembly, electrical parasitics, thermal behavior, mechanical reliability, inspection, rework, board area, and routing;
- the output of IC packaging is a **packaged electronic component**, not a PCB.

The next section explains how packaged ICs, discrete components, connectors, and a separately fabricated bare PCB come together during **PCB assembly** to create a **PCBA**.

## Authoring and Technical Verification Notes

### Section purpose

Section 1.9 is the major bridge from:

- **Section 1.8:** fabricated and singulated bare IC die; to
- **Section 1.10:** packaged components assembled onto a separately fabricated PCB.

The section should make clear **why packaging is required**, **how common die-to-package methods work**, **how common package families are recognized**, and **how a typical IC packaging manufacturing process is organized**.

### Scope control

Keep this section at the applied-recognition and manufacturing-awareness level.

Do not expand into:

- detailed wire-bonder operation;
- bond-force / ultrasonic-power optimization;
- bump metallurgy process recipes;
- package-substrate stack-up design;
- detailed mold-flow analysis;
- advanced warpage modeling;
- detailed package reliability modeling;
- JEDEC qualification test specifications;
- detailed wafer-level redistribution design;
- 2.5D / 3D chiplet architecture.

Advanced packaging remains awareness-level in Chapter 1.

### Technical distinctions to preserve

- **IC package family / size class != PCB mounting method != internal die interconnection**.
- A BGA may contain a wire-bonded or flip-chip die.
- A QFN may use wire bonding or flip-chip depending on design.
- Wire bonding generally uses a face-up die.
- Flip-chip generally uses a face-down die with bumps or pillars.
- Underfill is common in many flip-chip assemblies but should not be described as universally required.
- CSP is a size/package-class concept, not one single interconnection process.
- WLCSP forms packaging-related structures at wafer level.
- Fan-out packaging redistributes beyond the original die footprint and should remain awareness-level.
- Leadframe and package substrate are different support/interconnect structures.
- Passivation on the die is not the same thing as package encapsulation.
- Package molding/protection does not automatically create a hermetic seal.
- Final test and burn-in requirements vary by product.
- The packaged IC remains a component that is later assembled onto a separate PCB.
