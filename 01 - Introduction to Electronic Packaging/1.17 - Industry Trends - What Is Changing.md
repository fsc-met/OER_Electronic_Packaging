# 1.17 - Industry Trends: From Moore's Law to Advanced Packaging

Electronic packaging is becoming more important because the semiconductor industry can no longer rely on simple transistor shrinking to deliver the same steady improvements that characterized earlier decades.

For many years, semiconductor progress was strongly associated with **Moore's law**: the observation that the number of transistors that could be placed on an integrated circuit increased rapidly over time, commonly summarized today as roughly doubling every two years.

That historical trend was supported by repeated reductions in transistor dimensions. Smaller transistors allowed more devices to fit on a chip and, for many generations, also brought major improvements in performance, power, and cost.

Today, that simple scaling picture has changed.

> **Modern semiconductor progress increasingly depends on both continued transistor innovation and advanced packaging at the package and system level.**

---

## 1.17.1 - Moore's Law and Process Nodes

Semiconductor manufacturing generations are commonly described by **process-node names**, such as:

> **180 nm -> 90 nm -> 45 nm -> 22 nm -> 14 nm -> 7 nm -> 5 nm -> 3 nm -> 2 nm-class**

Historically, node names were more closely connected to important physical transistor dimensions. In modern leading-edge technologies, however, the node name is better understood as a **technology-generation label**.

> **A "3 nm" or "2 nm" process does not mean that every transistor feature is literally 3 nm or 2 nm in size.**

Different physical features in a modern transistor can have different dimensions, and manufacturers may use different naming conventions. A smaller node name generally indicates a more advanced semiconductor manufacturing generation, but it should not be interpreted as one exact transistor measurement.

This distinction is important when discussing modern semiconductor technology.

## 1.17.2 - Why Traditional Transistor Scaling Is Slowing

Moore's law is an observation about historical semiconductor progress, not a physical law that guarantees the same rate of improvement forever.

The **classical expectation that simple two-dimensional transistor shrinking will continue to provide the same regular gains in density, performance, power, and cost is no longer a sufficient roadmap for the industry**.

Further scaling is becoming more difficult because engineers face several interacting limits:

- transistor dimensions are approaching increasingly severe physical and materials constraints;
- short-channel effects and leakage become harder to control;
- interconnect resistance and power-delivery problems become more important;
- heat generation and heat removal become more difficult;
- advanced fabrication requires increasingly complex lithography, materials, and process integration;
- the cost of developing and manufacturing each new leading-edge generation continues to increase.

There is not one single point at which all transistor scaling suddenly stops. Instead, the industry is approaching **multiple physical, materials, electrical, thermal, manufacturing, and economic limits**.

As a result, semiconductor progress increasingly depends on new transistor structures, new materials, three-dimensional integration, design-technology co-optimization, and system-level integration.

---

## 1.17.3 - Transistor Structures Have Evolved

As conventional planar transistors became more difficult to scale, the semiconductor industry changed the physical shape of the transistor to improve control of the channel.

| Transistor generation | Approximate development / industry adoption | Basic structure | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Planar MOSFET** | MOSFET demonstrated in **1959-1960**; MOS integrated circuits emerged in the **1960s**; planar CMOS remained the mainstream logic-transistor approach for decades | The channel lies mainly in the wafer plane, with the gate controlling it primarily from above | Supported many generations of traditional CMOS scaling |
| **FinFET** | FinFET research prototypes were demonstrated in **1999**; high-volume industry use began around **2011-2012** with 22 nm-class logic technology | The channel is formed as a vertical fin, allowing the gate to control multiple sides of the channel | Improved electrostatic control as planar MOSFET scaling became more difficult |
| **Gate-All-Around (GAA) / nanosheet FET** | Research developed through the **2000s-2010s**; first 3 nm-class GAA production began in **2022**, with broader leading-edge adoption continuing through the **mid-2020s** | The gate surrounds the channel on all sides; modern implementations commonly use stacked nanosheet-shaped channels | Provides stronger channel control and supports continued scaling beyond FinFET |
| **Future vertically stacked structures, such as CFET** | Active research and technology-roadmap development in the **2020s**; **not yet mainstream production as of 2026** | n-type and p-type transistor structures can be stacked vertically rather than placed only side by side | Provides another path toward greater logic density when lateral scaling becomes increasingly difficult |

The dates in the table are approximate milestones. Transistor architectures are usually researched for years before high-volume manufacturing, and adoption timing varies among semiconductor manufacturers.

The simplified progression is:

> **Planar MOSFET -> FinFET -> GAA / nanosheet -> increasingly three-dimensional transistor integration**

This evolution shows that the industry has already moved beyond simply making the same transistor shape smaller.

## 1.17.4 - Advanced Packaging Becomes a Major Path for Continued Improvement

Even with new transistor architectures, transistor-level scaling alone is no longer enough to deliver all desired system improvements.

Increasingly, engineers also improve electronic systems by changing **how dies, memory, interconnects, power delivery, and cooling are integrated**.

Important approaches include:

- **System-in-Package (SiP)**;
- **heterogeneous integration**;
- **chiplets**;
- **2.5D integration** using interposers or advanced substrates;
- **3D die stacking**;
- **advanced package substrates and redistribution structures**;
- **high-density die-to-die interconnection**;
- **advanced thermal-management solutions**.

These technologies allow designers to combine dies made for different functions or manufacturing processes rather than forcing every function onto one very large monolithic die.

> **Advanced packaging does not replace transistor scaling. It complements transistor scaling by moving more performance improvement to the package and system level.**

This is one reason electronic packaging has become a major focus of the semiconductor and electronics industries.

## 1.17.5 - Major Industry Trends

The table below summarizes several important trends that students are likely to encounter in modern electronic-packaging work.

| Industry trend | Why it matters to electronic packaging |
|---|---|
| **Miniaturization and higher power density** | More functionality is placed into smaller volumes, creating tighter routing, cooling, mechanical, inspection, and reliability constraints |
| **Finer-pitch and higher-density interconnection** | Smaller features and more I/O connections make PCB fabrication, assembly, inspection, electrical performance, and reliability more demanding |
| **SiP and heterogeneous integration** | Multiple dies or components can be integrated within one package or package-like assembly, blurring traditional boundaries between package and system |
| **Chiplets** | Complex systems can be partitioned among multiple dies, increasing the importance of die-to-die interconnection, package routing, thermal management, test, and reliability |
| **2.5D and 3D integration** | Interposers and die stacking increase integration density but create additional thermal, manufacturing, test, and thermomechanical challenges |
| **Advanced substrates and interposers** | Finer routing and dense die-to-die connections increase the importance of substrate materials, warpage, thermal expansion, fabrication capability, and cost |
| **Advanced thermal solutions** | Increasing power density can require improved heat spreaders, thermal interface materials, vapor chambers, liquid cooling, or other enhanced cooling approaches where appropriate |
| **Automation and advanced inspection** | Smaller features and hidden joints increase the need for automated optical inspection, X-ray, machine vision, automated test, and stronger process control |
| **Data-driven manufacturing** | Production, inspection, and test data support traceability, process monitoring, troubleshooting, yield improvement, and corrective action |
| **Increasing reliability challenges** | Higher power density, finer interconnections, new materials, more interfaces, and demanding mission profiles increase interactions among thermal, mechanical, electrical, manufacturing, and environmental effects |

The overall industry direction is therefore not simply toward "smaller transistors."

It is toward **more advanced transistors, more three-dimensional integration, more sophisticated packaging, and more system-level optimization**.

---

## Section Takeaway

Moore's law described an important historical trend in semiconductor development, but modern progress can no longer be explained by simple transistor shrink alone.

Students should remember four main ideas:

- a process-node name such as `5 nm`, `3 nm`, or `2 nm` identifies a semiconductor technology generation and is not one literal transistor dimension;
- traditional dimensional scaling is becoming increasingly difficult because of physical, materials, electrical, thermal, manufacturing, and economic limits;
- transistor structures have evolved from planar MOSFETs to FinFETs and then to gate-all-around nanosheet devices, with further three-dimensional structures under development;
- advanced packaging, heterogeneous integration, chiplets, 2.5D/3D integration, and improved thermal management are increasingly important ways to continue improving electronic-system performance.

> **The future of semiconductor progress depends increasingly on combining transistor innovation with packaging and system-level integration.**

The next section summarizes Chapter 1 and provides review and practice activities.

### Technical distinctions to preserve

- Moore's law is an empirical observation, not a physical law.
- It is acceptable to summarize modern Moore's-law cadence as approximately doubling transistor count every two years, but do not present this as an exact rule that must continue indefinitely.
- Do not state simply that "Moore's law is false" or that transistor scaling has stopped.
- The more accurate teaching point is that **traditional dimensional scaling no longer provides the same automatic performance/power/area/cost benefits that characterized earlier generations**.
- Modern node names are technology-generation labels and should not be interpreted as one exact physical transistor dimension.
- A smaller node name generally indicates a more advanced process generation, but naming conventions vary among manufacturers.
- Do not claim that a 2 nm node contains a universal 2 nm gate length.
- Do not claim that one single atomic or materials limit has already ended all CMOS scaling.
- Scaling now encounters interacting physical, materials, electrical, interconnect, thermal, manufacturing, and economic limits.
- Planar MOSFET, FinFET, and GAA/nanosheet should be presented as a simplified architecture progression.
- GAA/nanosheet technology is now part of leading-edge production technology.
- CFET should be presented as a future / roadmap architecture rather than established mainstream production.
- Development and industry-adoption years in the transistor table are approximate milestones rather than universal transition dates.
- Research demonstrations generally precede high-volume manufacturing by years, and different manufacturers adopt new transistor architectures at different times.
- Advanced packaging complements transistor scaling; it does not replace continued transistor innovation.
- SiP, heterogeneous integration, chiplets, 2.5D, and 3D integration should remain recognition-level topics.
- Advanced thermal solutions are application dependent.
- Automation and data-driven manufacturing complement engineering judgment rather than eliminating it.

### Current technical verification

Current industry information was checked against authoritative sources before this draft was produced.

- **ASML 2025 Annual Report:** defines a semiconductor `node` as a generation or stepping stone in the chipmaking roadmap used to differentiate manufacturing technologies, supporting the explanation that modern node names are technology-generation labels rather than one universal physical dimension.
- **imec, CMOS Scaling:** states that dimensional scaling no longer provides the same performance-per-unit benefit as in the past and that continued progress increasingly requires new architectures, materials, design-technology co-optimization, and system-technology co-optimization.
- **imec, logic-scaling roadmap:** describes the architecture progression beyond FinFET toward GAA nanosheet devices, forksheet concepts, and CFET.
- **Computer History Museum, Silicon Engine:** documents the first successful MOSFET work in 1959 and its demonstration in 1960, supporting the planar-MOSF historical milestone.
- **UC Berkeley historical FinFET materials:** document the FinFET research prototype reported in 1999.
- **Intel 22 nm / Tri-Gate materials:** document the transition to high-volume 3-D multi-gate transistor production around 2011-2012, supporting the FinFET industry-adoption milestone.
- **Samsung 3 nm GAA production announcement:** documents initial 3 nm GAA production in 2022, supporting the first-industry-production milestone used in the table.
- **Intel 18A official process information:** identifies RibbonFET as Intel's gate-all-around transistor architecture, providing current industrial confirmation that GAA technology has moved beyond research.
- **TSMC 2025 Annual Report / N2 technology information:** reports N2 high-volume manufacturing beginning in 4Q 2025 using nanosheet transistor technology, confirming broader leading-edge GAA adoption in the mid-2020s.
- **imec CFET roadmap and research materials:** place CFET on the future logic-scaling roadmap and describe active 2020s research rather than mainstream production.

These current sources are used to verify the technical framing. Public prose remains vendor-neutral and independently authored.
