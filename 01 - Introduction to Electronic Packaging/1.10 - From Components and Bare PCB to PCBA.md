# 1.10 - From Components and Bare PCB to PCBA

Section 1.9 ended with a **packaged IC component**. That packaged IC is now mechanically robust enough to handle, test, store, ship, and assemble using normal electronic-manufacturing processes.

At the same time, a completely separate manufacturing stream produces the **bare printed circuit board (PCB)**.

These two streams do not become one another. They remain separate until **PCB assembly**, where electronic components are attached and electrically connected to the bare PCB.

The key manufacturing convergence is:

> **components + bare PCB -> PCB assembly -> PCBA**

A **PCBA**, or **printed circuit board assembly**, is a fabricated PCB that has been populated with components and other required board-level hardware to form an electrically interconnected assembly.

> **Critical Distinction:** Semiconductor fabrication produces the IC die. IC packaging produces the packaged component. PCB fabrication produces the bare PCB. These separately manufactured items come together during PCB assembly.

## 1.10.1 Two Manufacturing Streams Converge at PCB Assembly

The electronics inside a product arrive at PCB assembly through different manufacturing paths.

### Component stream

For an IC, the path introduced in Sections 1.8 and 1.9 is:

> **semiconductor fabrication -> IC die -> IC packaging -> packaged IC component**

Other board-mounted components, such as:

- resistors;
- capacitors;
- inductors;
- diodes;
- transistors;
- connectors;
- switches;
- sensors

also arrive at assembly as separately manufactured components.

### PCB stream

The PCB follows a different manufacturing path:

> **PCB materials -> PCB fabrication -> bare PCB**

The bare PCB already contains the physical features needed to support and electrically connect components, such as:

- copper traces;
- pads;
- plated holes;
- vias;
- solder mask;
- surface finish.

The detailed structure and fabrication of the PCB are covered in Chapter 2.

### Assembly convergence

The two streams finally converge:

> **packaged ICs + discrete components + connectors + bare PCB -> PCB assembly -> PCBA**

This distinction is fundamental to the rest of this book.

The packaged IC is **mounted onto** the PCB. It does not become the PCB, and PCB fabrication is not part of semiconductor fabrication.

## 1.10.2 What Changes When a Bare PCB Becomes a PCBA?

A **bare PCB** provides the electrical routing and mechanical platform, but it does not yet contain the mounted components needed for the intended electronic circuit or subsystem.

During assembly, components are added to the board and connected to its conductive features.

The result is a **PCBA** containing:

- a fabricated PCB;
- packaged ICs;
- discrete passive and active components;
- connectors and other electromechanical parts;
- electrical and mechanical interconnections between those components and the board.

The PCB provides more than electrical routing. It also serves as a mechanical support structure and can participate in heat spreading and structural load transfer.

> **Key Idea:** PCB assembly creates the physical interface between separately manufactured electronic components and the interconnection structure of the board.

## 1.10.3 Two Major Component-Mounting Approaches

At an introductory level, two major mounting approaches are important:

- **surface-mount technology (SMT)**;
- **through-hole technology (THT)**.

Many real PCBAs use both.

### Surface-mount technology

In **surface-mount technology (SMT)**, component terminals are connected to pads on the surface of the PCB.

Typical surface-mount components include:

- chip resistors and capacitors;
- SOIC and QFP packages;
- QFN/DFN packages;
- BGA and many CSP packages.

A common simplified SMT sequence is:

> **solder paste -> component placement -> reflow soldering**

At a recognition level:

1. solder paste is deposited on selected PCB pads;
2. components are placed onto the paste deposits;
3. the assembly is heated so the solder melts, wets the mating surfaces, and forms solder joints;
4. the assembly is cooled so the joints solidify.

Modern SMT is highly compatible with automated production and high component density.

The exact printing, placement, reflow, inspection, and process-control requirements are covered later in Chapter 4.

### Through-hole technology

In **through-hole technology (THT)**, component leads are inserted through plated through holes in the PCB.

For soldered THT assembly, the leads are then joined to the plated-hole structure using solder.

Typical through-hole applications include selected:

- connectors;
- switches;
- transformers;
- large capacitors;
- mechanically loaded components;
- legacy or specialized components.

Through-hole joints may be formed using processes such as:

- wave soldering;
- selective soldering;
- hand soldering;
- other process-specific methods.

The choice depends on product design and manufacturing requirements.

### Mixed assembly

A single PCBA may contain both SMT and THT components.

For example:

- small passive devices and ICs may use SMT;
- a large connector may use THT;
- other hardware may use press-fit, sockets, fasteners, or specialized joining methods.

Therefore:

> **SMT and THT are board-level assembly approaches, not IC package types.**

## 1.10.4 Solder Joints Connect Components to the Board

For most soldered electronic components, the **solder joint** is the physical connection between the component terminal and the PCB conductor.

A solder joint performs two important functions.

### Electrical connection

The joint provides a conductive path between:

- the component terminal;
- the PCB pad or plated hole;
- the copper routing inside the board.

Signals, power, and ground connections depend on these paths.

### Mechanical attachment

The same joint also helps hold the component in position.

During product use, joints may experience:

- handling loads;
- vibration;
- shock;
- thermal expansion and contraction;
- board bending;
- component weight or external mechanical loads.

Large or mechanically loaded components may therefore require additional support beyond the solder joints themselves.

This is why solder joints are both **electrical interconnections** and **mechanical connections**.

> **Engineering Perspective:** A solder joint can be electrically conductive and still have poor mechanical reliability, or appear mechanically intact while having an electrical defect. Assembly quality therefore requires both electrical and physical considerations.

Not every board-level connection is soldered. Some products also use:

- press-fit connectors;
- sockets;
- mechanical fasteners;
- conductive adhesives;
- other specialized interconnection methods.

However, solder remains the dominant board-level interconnection method for many electronic assemblies.

## 1.10.5 High-Level PCB Assembly Process

PCB assembly is not one universal manufacturing recipe. The exact process depends on:

- SMT versus THT content;
- component types;
- board geometry;
- production volume;
- soldering technology;
- inspection requirements;
- product quality and reliability requirements.

At the Chapter 1 level, the process can be viewed as four major activities:

> **placement / insertion -> soldering / joining -> inspection -> test**

For a typical SMT line, solder-paste printing occurs before placement; it is included here as part of the joining-process preparation rather than developed as a separate Chapter 1 topic.

### Step 1 - Place or insert components

For SMT:

- automated pick-and-place equipment commonly positions components onto prepared PCB pads.

For THT:

- component leads are inserted into plated through holes.

Placement must establish the correct:

- component location;
- orientation;
- polarity where applicable;
- alignment with pads or holes.

### Step 2 - Form the board-level interconnections

For SMT, solder joints are commonly formed by **reflow soldering** after solder paste printing and component placement.

For THT, soldering may use:

- wave soldering;
- selective soldering;
- hand soldering;
- other suitable methods.

The purpose is not simply to "stick" components to the board. The joining process must create reliable electrical and mechanical connections.

### Step 3 - Inspect the assembly

Inspection helps identify manufacturing defects before the assembly moves farther downstream.

Depending on the package and process, inspection may include:

- visual inspection;
- automated optical inspection (AOI);
- X-ray inspection for hidden or difficult-to-view interconnections;
- dimensional or process-specific inspection.

Package style strongly influences what can be seen.

For example:

- many QFP solder joints are visible around the package perimeter;
- QFN/DFN joints are largely underneath the package body;
- BGA solder joints are hidden beneath the package.

The detailed acceptance criteria and inspection methods are covered later in the book.

### Step 4 - Test the PCBA

Inspection can find physical defects, but it does not prove that the complete electrical assembly works correctly.

Depending on the product and manufacturing strategy, testing may include:

- continuity or shorts testing;
- in-circuit or flying-probe testing;
- programming;
- functional testing;
- other product-specific electrical tests.

Inspection and test therefore provide different but complementary information.

> **Key Distinction:** Inspection asks whether the assembly appears to have been built correctly. Electrical and functional tests ask whether electrical connections and circuit functions behave as intended.

## 1.10.6 Package and Board Design Decisions Affect Assembly Quality

PCB assembly is where many earlier design and packaging choices become manufacturing realities.

The package and board design affect:

### Placement

Manufacturing becomes more difficult when components are:

- very small;
- closely spaced;
- difficult to orient;
- difficult for placement equipment to access.

### Soldering

Different components can have very different:

- terminal geometries;
- thermal mass;
- heat-transfer behavior;
- solder-joint accessibility.

These differences affect how uniformly the assembly can be soldered.

### Inspection

Package geometry determines whether joints are:

- directly visible;
- partly hidden;
- completely hidden.

This influences which inspection methods are useful.

### Test and rework

The arrangement of components and test access can affect:

- probe accessibility;
- troubleshooting;
- rework;
- replacement of failed components.

The manufacturing question is therefore not only:

> **Will the circuit work?**

It is also:

> **Can it be built repeatedly, inspected, tested, and reworked when necessary?**

These ideas are developed much more deeply in Chapters 3 and 4.

## 1.10.7 What a PCBA Is—and What It Is Not

At the end of PCB assembly, the two manufacturing streams have physically converged, and the result is:

> **bare PCB + components + assembly processes -> PCBA**

A PCBA is therefore:

- a populated PCB;
- a mechanical platform carrying components;
- an electrical interconnection system;
- an important thermal and structural element of the product;
- a manufactured subassembly that can be inspected and tested.

However, a PCBA is **not necessarily the finished electronic product**.

A complete product may still require:

- an enclosure or chassis;
- cables and connectors;
- displays and controls;
- power supplies or batteries;
- heat sinks, fans, spreaders, or cold plates;
- sensors and actuators;
- structural mounting hardware;
- additional PCBAs or modules.

Those elements are introduced in Section 1.11.

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- electronic components and the bare PCB are manufactured in separate streams;
- semiconductor fabrication produces an IC die;
- IC packaging converts the die into a usable packaged component;
- PCB fabrication produces the bare PCB;
- the component and PCB streams converge during **PCB assembly**;
- the result is a **PCBA**;
- **SMT** places components on surface pads, while **THT** inserts component leads through plated holes;
- many real assemblies use both SMT and THT;
- solder joints provide both electrical connection and mechanical attachment;
- high-level PCB assembly includes placement or insertion, joining, inspection, and electrical/functional test;
- package and board choices affect manufacturability, assembly quality, inspection, test, and rework;
- a PCBA is an important electronic subassembly, but it may still require system-level hardware before it becomes a finished product.

The next section follows the PCBA into **system integration**, where boards, enclosures, cooling hardware, cabling, power systems, controls, sensors, and other elements are combined into a finished electronic product.

## Authoring and Technical Verification Notes

### Section purpose

Section 1.10 is the manufacturing-convergence bridge between:

- **Section 1.9:** bare IC die -> packaged IC component; and
- **Section 1.11:** PCBA -> finished electronic product.

The central teaching message is:

> **components and the bare PCB are manufactured separately and converge during PCB assembly**

The section should introduce PCB assembly only at the recognition/process-awareness level.

### Scope control

Keep this section introductory.

Do not expand into:

- PCB stack-up or fabrication detail;
- stencil-design rules;
- solder-paste rheology;
- SPI process metrics;
- pick-and-place programming;
- detailed reflow profiles;
- wave/selective solder process windows;
- detailed defect mechanisms;
- IPC acceptance criteria;
- DFM numerical rules;
- detailed AOI/X-ray programming;
- detailed ICT/flying-probe strategy;
- process capability or SPC.

These topics belong mainly in Chapters 2–4.

### Technical distinctions to preserve

- Semiconductor fabrication produces the **IC die**.
- IC packaging produces the **packaged IC/component**.
- PCB fabrication produces the **bare PCB**.
- PCB assembly produces the **PCBA**.
- A packaged IC is mounted onto a separately manufactured PCB.
- **SMT** and **THT** are board-level mounting/assembly approaches.
- **DIP, SOIC, QFP, QFN, BGA, CSP** are package-family or size-class terms, not assembly-process names.
- A board can use mixed SMT/THT assembly.
- Solder joints provide electrical and mechanical connection.
- Not every board-level connection must be soldered.
- Inspection and electrical/functional test are complementary, not interchangeable.
- Hidden solder joints may require methods such as X-ray; the use of X-ray is not itself evidence of poor design.
- The PCBA is not necessarily the finished product.
