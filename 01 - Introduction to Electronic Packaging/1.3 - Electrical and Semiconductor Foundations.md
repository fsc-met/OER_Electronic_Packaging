# 1.3 - Electrical and Semiconductor Foundations

Electronic packaging is primarily concerned with the physical realization of electronic products, but the physical structures only make sense if we understand what the electronic devices are doing.

For students who have not taken a dedicated electronics or semiconductor course, this section provides the minimum electrical and semiconductor background needed for the rest of Chapter 1. The goal is not to design semiconductor devices or solve semiconductor-physics equations. The goal is to understand:

- what voltage and current mean in a physical circuit;
- why silicon can be engineered to carry current in useful ways;
- how N-type and P-type semiconductor regions are created;
- what happens when P-type and N-type regions form a PN junction;
- how external voltage changes the behavior of that junction;
- why a PN junction can act as a diode.

These ideas lead directly to Section 1.4, where semiconductor structures are combined into transistors and integrated circuits.

> **Engineering Perspective:** You do not need to become a semiconductor-fabrication engineer to work effectively with electronic packaging. You do need enough device background to understand what is inside the component you are packaging, assembling, cooling, testing, and troubleshooting.

## 1.3.1 Basic Electrical Language

Before discussing semiconductors, we need a small set of electrical terms that will appear throughout this book.

### Electric charge

**Electric charge** is a basic property of matter. Charge can be positive or negative. The SI unit of charge is the **coulomb (C)**.

An **electron** carries negative electric charge. In metal conductors such as copper PCB traces and wires, electrons are the primary mobile charge carriers.

In semiconductor materials, current can involve both electrons and **holes**, which will be introduced later in this section.

### Voltage

**Voltage** is an electric potential difference between two points. It describes the electrical driving potential available to move charge through a circuit.

Voltage is measured in **volts (V)**.

A voltage is always measured **between two points**. Saying that a node is at "5 V" is incomplete unless a reference is understood. In most electronic circuits, one node is selected as the reference and assigned a potential of 0 V.

### Current

**Electric current** is the rate at which electric charge passes through a location in a circuit.

Current is measured in **amperes (A)**.

For circuit diagrams and engineering calculations, we normally use **conventional current**, defined in the direction that positive charge would move.

In a metal conductor, the mobile charges are electrons, so the average electron motion is opposite the conventional-current direction. Both descriptions represent the same physical current; they simply use different direction conventions.

This distinction becomes useful in semiconductor devices because both negatively charged electrons and positively represented holes can contribute to current.

### Resistance

**Electrical resistance** describes how strongly a component or material opposes current.

Resistance is measured in **ohms ($\Omega$)**.

For an ohmic resistor, voltage, current, and resistance are related by Ohm's law:

$$
V = IR
$$

where:

- $V$ is the voltage across the resistor;
- $I$ is the current through the resistor;
- $R$ is the resistance.

Ohm's law is not a universal description of every electronic device. A diode, for example, has a strongly nonlinear voltage-current relationship. This simple resistor relationship will be used more extensively in Chapter 5. Here, the important idea is that a voltage difference can drive current through an available conducting path, while resistance limits that current.

### DC voltage and current

**Direct current (DC)** is current whose conventional direction does not reverse. Its magnitude may be steady or may vary with time. A **DC voltage** maintains a fixed polarity, although its magnitude can also vary.

Batteries and regulated power supplies are common DC sources in electronic products.

Real electronic systems can contain rapidly changing voltages and currents, but DC concepts are enough for the introductory semiconductor behavior discussed in this section.

### Ground and electrical reference

The word **ground** is used frequently in electronics, but it does not always mean a physical connection to Earth.

In many low-voltage electronic circuits, a ground symbol identifies the node chosen as the common electrical reference, often called **0 V**, **GND**, or **circuit common**.

The voltage at another node is then described relative to that reference.

A product may also include protective Earth, chassis ground, signal ground, or other reference structures. Those distinctions become important in practical electronic systems, but for this introductory section, remember:

> **Voltage is measured relative to a reference. A circuit-ground symbol does not automatically mean Earth ground.**

### Bias

To **bias** an electronic device means to apply a voltage or current that establishes a desired electrical operating condition.

For the PN junction introduced later, the two basic conditions are:

- **forward bias**, which encourages strong junction conduction;
- **reverse bias**, which suppresses junction conduction under normal operating conditions.

The exact meaning of bias depends on the device, but the general idea is always the same: an externally applied electrical condition changes how the device operates.

### Basic electrical quantities at a glance

| Quantity | Symbol commonly used | SI unit | Practical meaning in this section |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Charge | $Q$ | coulomb (C) | Electrical property carried by particles such as electrons |
| Voltage | $V$ | volt (V) | Electrical potential difference between two points |
| Current | $I$ | ampere (A) | Rate of charge flow |
| Resistance | $R$ | ohm ($\Omega$) | Opposition to current |

## 1.3.2 Conductors, Insulators, and Semiconductors

Electronic products use materials with very different electrical behavior.

A **conductor** provides many mobile charge carriers and therefore allows current to flow relatively easily. Copper is the most important conductor in ordinary PCBs because it is used for traces, planes, pads, and plated holes.

An **insulator** strongly restricts the motion of charge under normal operating conditions. Insulating materials are used to separate conductors that must remain electrically isolated. PCB laminates, solder mask, cable insulation, and many package dielectrics perform insulating functions.

A **semiconductor** is distinguished by the fact that its electrical behavior can be deliberately engineered over a very wide range. Its conductivity and current paths can be changed through controlled impurities, device structure, electric fields, temperature, illumination, and other physical influences.

This controllability is what makes semiconductors useful for electronic devices.

It is common to describe a semiconductor as having conductivity "between" that of a conductor and an insulator. That description can be useful as a first approximation, but it misses the most important engineering point:

> **A semiconductor is valuable because its charge-carrier population and current path can be controlled.**

Silicon is the dominant semiconductor material for mainstream integrated circuits. Other semiconductor materials are important for power, RF, optical, and specialized applications and will be introduced briefly in Section 1.5.

## 1.3.3 Pure Silicon

Silicon has four valence electrons. In a pure silicon crystal, each silicon atom shares valence electrons with neighboring atoms through **covalent bonds**.

This regular bonding creates a stable crystal structure.

At ordinary temperatures, thermal energy can occasionally give an electron enough energy to leave a bond and become mobile within the material. When that happens:

- a mobile electron becomes available to carry charge;
- the missing electron in the bond leaves an unoccupied state called a **hole**.

A nearby bound electron can move into the hole, leaving a new hole behind. Instead of tracking many individual bond electrons, engineers can treat the hole as a mobile **positive charge carrier**.

Pure semiconductor material is called an **intrinsic semiconductor**.

Intrinsic silicon is not a perfect insulator, but at ordinary temperatures its concentration of mobile carriers is relatively low compared with deliberately doped silicon. For practical electronic devices, engineers intentionally change the carrier population through **doping**.

## 1.3.4 Semiconductor Doping

**Doping** is the deliberate addition of a small, controlled amount of impurity atoms to a semiconductor crystal to change its electrical behavior.

The dopant concentration is small compared with the number of silicon atoms, but it can strongly change the number and type of mobile charge carriers.

Two important cases are N-type and P-type silicon.

### N-type silicon

Silicon has four valence electrons. A common **donor** dopant such as phosphorus has five valence electrons.

When a phosphorus atom substitutes for a silicon atom in the crystal:

- four of its valence electrons participate in bonding;
- the additional electron is much more easily made available as a mobile carrier;
- after donating that electron, the dopant site is a fixed positively ionized donor.

The resulting material is **N-type semiconductor**.

In N-type material:

- **electrons are the majority carriers**;
- holes are the minority carriers.

### P-type silicon

A common **acceptor** dopant such as boron has three valence electrons.

When a boron atom substitutes for silicon:

- one bond lacks an electron;
- the dopant can accept an electron from a neighboring bond;
- that transfer leaves a mobile hole elsewhere in the bonding structure;
- after accepting an electron, the dopant site is a fixed negatively ionized acceptor.

The resulting material is **P-type semiconductor**.

In P-type material:

- **holes are the majority carriers**;
- electrons are the minority carriers.

> **Common Mistake:** N-type material is not a negatively charged block of silicon, and P-type material is not a positively charged block of silicon. Each material is electrically neutral overall. The labels identify the dominant mobile carrier type, not a net bulk charge.

The distinction between **mobile carriers** and **fixed ionized dopants** becomes especially important when N-type and P-type regions are joined.

## 1.3.5 The PN Junction

When a P-type region and an N-type region are formed next to each other in the same semiconductor crystal, the boundary between them is called a **PN junction**.

Before the junction forms, the N-type region has a high concentration of mobile electrons and the P-type region has a high concentration of mobile holes.

Once the two regions contact each other, carriers begin to **diffuse** because of the concentration difference:

- some electrons diffuse from the N side toward the P side;
- some holes diffuse from the P side toward the N side.

Near the junction, electrons and holes can recombine.

When mobile carriers leave the junction region, they expose the ionized dopant atoms that are fixed in the crystal:

- positive donor ions remain on the N side near the junction;
- negative acceptor ions remain on the P side near the junction.

These fixed charges create an internal electric field.

The region near the junction becomes depleted of most **mobile majority carriers** and is called the **depletion region** or **space-charge region**. It still contains the fixed ionized dopant atoms that create the junction's internal electric field.

The electric field associated with the fixed ions opposes continued majority-carrier diffusion. At thermal equilibrium, diffusion and electric-field-driven carrier motion balance so that there is no net current through an unbiased junction.

This built-in electrical barrier is the key to diode behavior.

> **Important:** The depletion region is not an empty gap between two pieces of material. It is still semiconductor crystal. It is called "depleted" because it contains relatively few mobile majority carriers compared with the neutral regions farther from the junction.

## 1.3.6 Biasing a PN Junction

An external voltage can change the width and electrical barrier of the depletion region. This is called **biasing** the junction.

### Forward bias

A PN junction is **forward biased** when the P side is made positive relative to the N side.

The external voltage opposes part of the junction's built-in electric field. As a result:

- the effective barrier to majority-carrier motion is reduced;
- the depletion region becomes narrower;
- more electrons and holes can cross the junction;
- the junction current increases strongly.

For a practical silicon diode, the exact voltage-current relationship depends on device design, current level, temperature, and other factors. It is therefore better to understand forward conduction as a continuous device characteristic rather than assume one perfectly fixed "turn-on voltage."

### Reverse bias

A PN junction is **reverse biased** when the P side is made negative relative to the N side.

The applied voltage reinforces the built-in junction field. As a result:

- the effective barrier increases;
- the depletion region becomes wider;
- majority-carrier conduction is strongly suppressed;
- a relatively small reverse current, associated mainly with minority-carrier and leakage mechanisms, normally remains.

If the reverse voltage becomes sufficiently large, the junction can enter **breakdown**, where reverse current increases sharply.

Breakdown is not automatically destructive. Some semiconductor devices are intentionally designed to operate in a controlled breakdown region. However, excessive voltage or current can damage devices that are not designed for that operating condition.

## 1.3.7 The Semiconductor Diode

A **semiconductor diode** uses a PN junction to produce strongly direction-dependent current behavior.

For a basic PN-junction diode:

- the P-type side is associated with the **anode**;
- the N-type side is associated with the **cathode**.

When the diode is forward biased, substantial current can flow once the applied conditions support strong carrier injection across the junction.

When the diode is reverse biased, current is normally much smaller until the device approaches its reverse-breakdown region.

This makes the diode useful when a circuit needs current behavior that depends strongly on direction or voltage polarity.

Common applications include:

- **rectification** - converting or steering current based on polarity;
- **reverse-polarity protection** - reducing the chance that current flows through an unintended path;
- **flyback or transient protection** - providing a controlled path for current associated with inductive loads;
- **voltage clamping and ESD protection** - limiting some transient voltages when used in an appropriate protection circuit.

The last two applications depend on the complete device and circuit design, not only the idealized PN-junction picture.

A real diode is therefore not a perfect one-way switch. It has:

- a nonlinear forward voltage-current relationship;
- a small reverse leakage current;
- a finite breakdown voltage;
- temperature-dependent behavior;
- electrical and thermal limits set by its construction and package.

These non-ideal behaviors become important when selecting, packaging, cooling, and troubleshooting real devices.

## Why This Matters for Packaging

At first glance, doping and PN-junction behavior may seem far removed from PCB assembly. They are not.

The package and PCBA must support a semiconductor device whose electrical behavior depends on very small internal structures.

Several practical connections are important:

### Junction temperature matters

Semiconductor behavior changes with temperature. Leakage current, voltage-current characteristics, switching behavior, and allowable operating limits can all depend on junction temperature.

Packaging therefore needs a thermal path that keeps the device within acceptable operating conditions.

### Semiconductor structures can be electrically damaged

Very small junctions and insulating layers can be damaged by excessive voltage, current, or electrostatic discharge (ESD).

This is one reason electronic manufacturing uses ESD-control procedures when handling sensitive components.

### The package must connect microscopic structures to the outside world

The active semiconductor regions discussed in this section exist inside the die. They eventually connect through on-die interconnects to bond pads or bump interfaces, then through the IC package, solder joints, PCB traces, connectors, and the rest of the electronic system.

Each transition introduces physical structures that affect electrical, thermal, mechanical, manufacturing, and reliability performance.

### Device behavior and packaging behavior are connected

A packaging problem can become an electrical problem.

For example:

- excessive temperature can change semiconductor operation;
- an ESD event can damage internal device structures;
- a poor package or solder interconnect can interrupt current paths;
- inadequate heat removal can force a device to reduce performance or fail;
- mechanical damage can break the electrical path between the die and the PCB.

This is why an engineering technologist working with PCBAs benefits from understanding what is happening inside the semiconductor device, even if semiconductor fabrication is not the technologist's primary job.

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- **Voltage** is an electric potential difference between two points.
- **Current** is the rate of charge flow; conventional current direction is opposite electron motion in ordinary metal conductors.
- **Resistance** opposes current and, for an ohmic resistor, follows $V=IR$.
- A circuit **ground** is often an electrical reference and is not automatically Earth ground.
- A **semiconductor** is useful because its charge-carrier population and current behavior can be deliberately controlled.
- Pure silicon is an **intrinsic semiconductor** whose electrical behavior can be strongly changed through **doping**.
- **N-type silicon** has electrons as majority carriers.
- **P-type silicon** has holes as majority carriers.
- N-type and P-type materials remain electrically neutral overall.
- Joining P-type and N-type regions creates a **PN junction** with a depletion region and built-in electric field.
- **Forward bias** reduces the junction barrier and promotes strong conduction.
- **Reverse bias** increases the barrier and normally suppresses conduction until breakdown becomes important.
- A **diode** uses this asymmetric junction behavior to perform useful circuit functions.

The next section builds on these ideas by showing how semiconductor structures become transistors and how transistors become integrated circuits.
