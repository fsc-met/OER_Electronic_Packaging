# 1.12 - Packaging Hierarchy

Sections 1.3 through 1.11 followed the physical development of electronics from semiconductor material to a finished electronic product. Before introducing the packaging hierarchy, it is useful to place that entire manufacturing pathway in one view.

## 1.12.1 - Manufacturing Pathway So Far

A typical semiconductor and electronic-product pathway can be summarized as follows.

### Semiconductor and IC path

> **Semiconductor material -> semiconductor devices and circuits -> integrated circuit fabricated on a wafer -> wafer test -> singulation -> bare IC die -> IC packaging -> packaged IC**

The detailed route varies with technology. For example, some packaging operations may begin while the dies are still on the wafer, and some products use bare-die assembly rather than a conventional individually packaged IC. The important point is that **semiconductor fabrication creates the electronic circuit on the die**, while **IC packaging provides the physical interface between that die and the next level of integration**.

### PCB path

A separate manufacturing stream creates the printed circuit board:

> **PCB materials -> PCB fabrication -> bare PCB**

The bare PCB contains the patterned conductors, pads, vias, insulating materials, solder mask, and other board features required for later assembly. It does not yet contain the electronic components that make the board perform its intended function.

### PCB assembly

The component and PCB streams then converge:

> **packaged ICs + discrete components + connectors + bare PCB -> PCB assembly -> PCBA**

During PCB assembly, components are positioned and electrically and mechanically joined to the board. Surface-mount technology (SMT), through-hole technology (THT), or a combination of both may be used depending on the product.

### Product integration

A PCBA is often only one subsystem. It may then be combined with other hardware:

> **PCBA + power + interconnections + cooling + mechanical supports + enclosure + interfaces -> system integration -> finished electronic product**

After integration, the product may undergo final functional testing, qualification, production release, field operation, maintenance, and eventual feedback into future design and manufacturing improvements.

The complete pathway therefore moves from **microscopic semiconductor structures** to **packaged components**, then to **board assemblies**, and finally to **integrated products and systems**.

This manufacturing pathway answers the question:

> **What happens next as an electronic product is built?**

The **packaging hierarchy** answers a different question:

> **At what physical level of the electronic product are we working?**

---

## 1.12.2 - Why Use a Packaging Hierarchy?

Electronic packaging exists at several physical scales. A problem that appears at one scale can depend on structures and interfaces created at another.

For example:

- heat generated in a semiconductor die must pass through the IC package, PCB, and possibly product-level cooling hardware before reaching the environment;
- a signal may travel from an on-die interconnect through package connections, PCB traces, connectors, and cables before reaching another subsystem;
- vibration applied to an enclosure may create motion at the PCB level and stress at component solder joints;
- a manufacturing defect at a solder joint can become an electrical failure at the product level.

A packaging hierarchy provides an **organizational model** for these physical scales. It helps engineers identify:

- what physical object is being analyzed;
- what structures connect it to the next level;
- where heat, electrical signals, power, and mechanical loads cross an interface;
- where manufacturing, inspection, and reliability problems may occur.

Different textbooks, industries, and companies may number packaging levels differently. Therefore, this book uses **descriptive level names** rather than treating one numbering system as universal.

## 1.12.3 - Wafer / Device Level

At the **wafer/device level**, many microscopic semiconductor devices and interconnections are fabricated across a semiconductor wafer.

Representative structures include:

- transistor and diode regions;
- integrated resistors and capacitors;
- on-die metal interconnect;
- passivation;
- I/O pad structures;
- wafer-level redistribution or bump structures where the technology requires them.

This level deals with extremely small features and is closely connected to semiconductor fabrication.

For this book, the wafer/device level is the physical starting point of the packaging hierarchy. It establishes the electronic circuit that later packaging levels must electrically connect, mechanically support, protect, and cool.

---

## 1.12.4 - Bare-Die Level

After singulation, the wafer becomes individual **bare IC dies**.

A bare die already contains the integrated electronic circuit, but it is still a small and fragile semiconductor structure. Its I/O features are typically too small and mechanically vulnerable for ordinary PCB assembly without an appropriate interconnection and handling strategy.

The bare-die stage is useful to identify separately because it represents the transition between:

> **wafer fabrication -> individual semiconductor device -> packaging or direct die-level assembly**

Not every packaging reference treats the bare die as a separately numbered hierarchy level. In this OER, it is shown explicitly because students have already followed the manufacturing pathway through wafer probe, dicing, and IC packaging.

---

## 1.12.5 - IC Package / Component Level

At the **IC package/component level**, the bare die is converted into a component that can be handled and connected at the next level.

Depending on the package technology, this level may include:

- die attach;
- wire bonds, flip-chip bumps, copper pillars, or other die-to-package interconnections;
- a leadframe or package substrate;
- encapsulation, molding, or a lid;
- external leads, pads, pins, or solder balls;
- thermal features such as an exposed pad, heat spreader, or lid.

The package creates practical electrical, mechanical, and thermal interfaces between the microscopic die and the larger structures around it.

The exact construction varies widely among package families, so the hierarchy describes the **physical integration level**, not one universal package design.

---

## 1.12.6 - PCB / PCBA Level

At the **PCB/PCBA level**, packaged ICs and other components are assembled onto a separately fabricated printed circuit board.

This level includes structures such as:

- PCB laminate and copper layers;
- traces and planes;
- pads and vias;
- solder joints;
- surface-mount and through-hole components;
- board-level connectors;
- test points and other assembly features.

The distinction between the two terms remains important:

> **PCB = bare printed circuit board**  
> **PCBA = PCB with assembled components**

Many practical electronic-packaging issues become highly visible at this level, including solder-joint quality, board bending, component placement, electrical routing, heat spreading, inspection access, and reworkability. These topics are developed in detail in later chapters.

---

## 1.12.7 - Module / Motherboard / Subassembly Level

Many products contain more than one board or electronic module. These may be connected through:

- board-to-board connectors;
- backplanes;
- cables or harnesses;
- sockets or card-edge connections;
- shared mechanical supports;
- shared power and cooling structures.

This physical scale can be described as the **module, motherboard, or subassembly level**, depending on the product.

Examples include:

- a motherboard carrying several daughter cards;
- a controller containing separate processing and power PCBAs;
- a plug-in electronic module;
- a backplane connecting several boards.

Not every electronic product has a distinct motherboard level. The descriptive hierarchy is therefore more useful than assuming that every product follows the same architecture.

---

## 1.12.8 - Product / Enclosure / System Level

At the **product or system level**, electronic subassemblies are integrated into the complete product.

This level may include:

- one or more PCBAs;
- enclosure or chassis;
- power supplies or batteries;
- cables and external connectors;
- displays and controls;
- sensors and actuators;
- heat sinks, fans, heat spreaders, or other cooling hardware;
- structural mounting hardware;
- environmental and electromagnetic protection features.

The system level is where the electronics must operate successfully in the actual product environment.

A design that performs correctly at the component or board level can still fail at the system level because of issues such as inadequate airflow, mechanical loading, environmental exposure, poor cable routing, or difficult maintenance access.

---

## 1.12.9 - System-to-System / Rack / Cluster Level

Some applications extend beyond a single product.

Examples include:

- equipment racks;
- data-center servers;
- telecommunications cabinets;
- industrial control cabinets;
- interconnected instruments;
- systems with external peripherals.

At this larger scale, packaging considerations may include:

- system-to-system electrical and data interconnection;
- aggregate power delivery;
- combined heat generation;
- airflow and cooling infrastructure;
- cable management;
- equipment access;
- replacement and maintenance;
- physical support and installation.

This level is useful when the surrounding infrastructure affects the operation or reliability of the individual electronic systems. It is not a required hierarchy level for every product.

---

## 1.12.10 - Interfaces Connect the Levels

The hierarchy is not only a list of objects. The **interfaces between levels** are often where important engineering problems occur.

Representative interfaces include:

- die-to-package attachment;
- wire bonds or flip-chip connections;
- package-to-PCB solder joints;
- board-to-board connectors;
- cable and harness connections;
- thermal interface materials;
- PCB mounting points and fasteners;
- enclosure and chassis interfaces.

Across these interfaces, the product must transfer some combination of:

- electrical signals;
- electrical power;
- heat;
- mechanical loads;
- environmental protection.

An interface that works well for one function may create difficulty for another. For example, a mechanically stiff attachment may improve support while increasing thermomechanical stress, and a compact arrangement may reduce size while making cooling or rework more difficult.

This is why packaging problems should be considered across levels rather than only within one component or board.

---

## 1.12.11 - Different Problems Appear at Different Physical Scales

The same engineering disciplines appear throughout the hierarchy, but the physical questions change with scale.

| Physical level | Representative engineering questions |
|---|---|
| Wafer / device | How are microscopic devices and I/O structures created and protected before later integration? |
| Bare die | How will the fragile die be handled, interconnected, protected, and cooled? |
| IC package / component | How will signals, power, heat, and mechanical loads pass between the die and the board? |
| PCB / PCBA | Can components be assembled reliably, electrically interconnected, inspected, cooled, and mechanically supported? |
| Module / motherboard | How will multiple boards or modules connect, align, receive power, exchange signals, and share mechanical or thermal resources? |
| Product / system | Will the integrated electronics survive the product's thermal, mechanical, electrical, and environmental conditions? |
| Rack / cluster, where applicable | Can multiple systems be powered, cooled, interconnected, serviced, and supported effectively? |

The hierarchy therefore helps locate a problem, but it does not isolate the problem. Electrical, thermal, mechanical, manufacturing, and reliability effects can cross several levels at the same time.

---

## 1.12.12 - Modern Packaging Can Blur Traditional Boundaries

The hierarchy remains useful, but modern packaging technologies do not always fit into simple traditional categories.

Examples include:

- **wafer-level packaging**, where packaging structures are created while the dies are still part of the wafer;
- **System-in-Package (SiP)**, where several dies or components may be integrated inside one package;
- **chiplet-based systems**, where multiple dies perform different functions within one package or package-like assembly;
- **2.5D integration**, where dies may communicate through an interposer or advanced substrate;
- **3D integration**, where dies are stacked vertically;
- **hybrid bonding**, which can directly connect fine-pitch structures between dies or wafers.

These technologies can move functions that were traditionally associated with a board or module into the package itself.

The useful engineering question is therefore not simply:

> **What level number is this?**

A better question is:

> **What physical structures are being connected, what interface is created, and what electrical, thermal, mechanical, manufacturing, and reliability requirements must cross that interface?**

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- the Chapter 1 manufacturing pathway progresses from semiconductor fabrication to bare die, IC packaging, PCB assembly, system integration, and the finished product;
- the PCB is manufactured in a separate stream and converges with components during PCB assembly;
- a **manufacturing pathway** describes how the product is built, while a **packaging hierarchy** organizes the physical scales within the product;
- this book uses descriptive hierarchy levels because packaging-level numbering varies among references and industries;
- useful physical levels include wafer/device, bare die, IC package/component, PCB/PCBA, module/motherboard, product/system, and larger rack/cluster integration where applicable;
- interfaces between levels carry electrical signals, power, heat, and mechanical loads and are often critical locations for packaging problems;
- electrical, thermal, mechanical, manufacturing, and reliability issues can propagate across multiple hierarchy levels;
- modern technologies such as wafer-level packaging, SiP, chiplets, 2.5D/3D integration, and hybrid bonding can blur traditional level boundaries.

Now that the physical hierarchy has been established, the next section examines the **major functions of electronic packaging** that must be performed across these levels, including electrical interconnection, thermal management, mechanical support, environmental protection, manufacturability, inspection, service, and reliability.

## Authoring and Technical Verification Notes

### Section purpose

Section 1.12 first consolidates the manufacturing pathway developed throughout Chapter 1, then converts that pathway into an organizational model based on physical packaging levels.

The two central teaching distinctions are:

> **Manufacturing pathway = how the product is built**

and

> **Packaging hierarchy = the physical scale at which the product is being analyzed**

The section should prevent students from confusing a manufacturing sequence with a universal hierarchy numbering system.

### Scope control

Keep this section at the recognition, conceptual-understanding, and manufacturing-awareness level.

Do not expand into:

- detailed wafer-level packaging process recipes;
- detailed IC-package construction already covered in Section 1.9;
- detailed PCB fabrication from Chapter 2;
- detailed PCB assembly from Chapter 4;
- advanced 2.5D/3D package design;
- interposer electrical design;
- detailed thermal modeling;
- detailed vibration or structural analysis;
- detailed reliability modeling;
- vendor-specific hierarchy terminology.

Modern advanced-packaging examples should remain brief and should be used only to explain why hierarchy boundaries can blur.

### Technical distinctions to preserve

- Semiconductor fabrication produces the electronic circuit on a wafer and ultimately the bare die.
- IC packaging and PCB fabrication are different manufacturing processes.
- The PCB stream remains separate from semiconductor/component manufacturing until PCB assembly.
- PCB and PCBA are not interchangeable terms.
- A PCBA is not automatically the finished product.
- A bare die is shown explicitly in this OER as a useful transitional physical level, although not every reference numbers it as a separate packaging level.
- Module/motherboard architecture is product dependent.
- Rack/cluster integration is relevant only where the surrounding infrastructure forms part of the packaging problem.
- No one hierarchy numbering system should be presented as universal.
- Modern technologies can combine or blur levels without eliminating the usefulness of the hierarchy.
- The hierarchy describes physical integration levels; it is not itself a required manufacturing sequence.
