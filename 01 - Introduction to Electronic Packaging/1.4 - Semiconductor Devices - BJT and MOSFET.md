# 1.4 - Semiconductor Devices: BJT and MOSFET

Section 1.3 introduced the semiconductor-material concepts behind doping, PN junctions, and diodes. This section takes the next step by introducing the two major transistor families students are most likely to encounter: the **bipolar junction transistor (BJT)** and the **field-effect transistor (FET)**, with particular emphasis on the **MOSFET**.

The purpose is not to teach transistor circuit design or semiconductor-device equations. The purpose is to give enough device-level understanding to answer practical questions such as:

- What are the major transistor families?
- What do the emitter, base, collector, source, drain, gate, and body terminals mean?
- How can a small electrical input control a larger current path?
- How does a MOSFET behave like a voltage-controlled switch?
- Why are NMOS and PMOS important in modern integrated circuits?
- Why does transistor operation matter to electronic packaging?

This section is one step in the larger Chapter 1 progression:

> **semiconductor materials → semiconductor devices → electronic circuits → integrated circuits → IC fabrication → IC packaging**

Section 1.5 will show how transistors are combined into useful analog and digital circuits. Section 1.6 will then explain how active devices, passive structures, and interconnections can be integrated together on one semiconductor die.

> **Engineering Perspective:** A transistor shown as a simple schematic symbol may physically exist as a discrete packaged component on a PCB or as one microscopic device among many fabricated inside an IC. Electronic packaging must connect, power, cool, protect, and support the physical device regardless of how the circuit is represented schematically.

## 1.4.1 Basic Electronic Components and Schematic Symbols

Electronic circuits are represented using **schematic symbols**. A schematic is a functional map of the circuit: it shows which electrical elements are connected and how they are intended to interact.

Common symbols that appear throughout electronic products include:

- **resistor** - limits or controls current and helps establish voltages;
- **capacitor** - stores electrical charge and energy in an electric field and is widely used for filtering, timing, coupling, and local energy storage;
- **inductor** - stores energy in a magnetic field and is widely used in filtering, power conversion, and RF circuits;
- **diode** - provides strongly direction-dependent current behavior;
- **transistor** - controls current and can be used as a switch or amplifier;
- **voltage source** - provides an electrical potential difference;
- **ground/reference** - identifies a selected circuit reference node.

The same schematic symbol can represent devices that look very different physically. A resistor might be a small surface-mount component on a PCB or a resistive structure fabricated inside an IC. A MOSFET symbol can represent a discrete packaged power transistor or one transistor among many fabricated on a semiconductor die.

> **Important:** A schematic symbol represents **electrical function and connectivity**, not the exact physical geometry of the component or the way it is manufactured.

## 1.4.2 Transistor Families: BJT and FET

A **transistor** is a semiconductor device used to control electrical current. Transistors are fundamental building blocks of electronic circuits because they can be used for switching, amplification, signal processing, power control, and many other functions.

Two major transistor families are useful to recognize:

- **bipolar junction transistors (BJTs)**;
- **field-effect transistors (FETs)**.

A useful classification is:

- **Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT)**
  - NPN
  - PNP
- **Field-Effect Transistor (FET)**
  - JFET
  - MOSFET
    - NMOS
    - PMOS

For this course, the **MOSFET** is the most important type of FET because MOSFET-based circuits are widely used in digital integrated circuits, power electronics, analog circuits, and many other electronic systems.

### Comparing BJT and FET Transistors

| Feature | Bipolar Junction Transistor (BJT) | Field-Effect Transistor (FET) |
|---|---|---|
| Basic control idea | A base-emitter electrical condition and associated base current control a larger collector-emitter current | An electric field produced by gate voltage controls a conducting path between source and drain |
| Main terminals | **Emitter (E), Base (B), Collector (C)** | **Source (S), Gate (G), Drain (D)**; MOSFETs also include a body connection |
| Common types | **NPN, PNP** | **JFET, MOSFET** |
| Important MOSFET forms | Not applicable | **NMOS, PMOS** |
| Simplified description | Commonly introduced as **current-controlled** | Commonly introduced as **voltage-controlled** |
| Main circuit roles | Switching and amplification | Switching and amplification |
| Can exist as a discrete packaged device? | Yes | Yes |
| Can be fabricated inside an IC? | Yes | Yes |
| Main emphasis in this section | NPN/PNP structure and base-controlled current | MOSFET structure and gate-controlled channel formation |

The terms **current-controlled** and **voltage-controlled** are useful introductory models, but real transistor behavior is more detailed. This book uses these models only to develop the physical understanding needed for electronic packaging.

> **Key Idea:** BJT and FET describe semiconductor-device families, not package types. Either family may appear as an individual packaged component or as devices fabricated within an integrated circuit.

## 1.4.3 Bipolar Junction Transistor

A **bipolar junction transistor (BJT)** is a three-terminal semiconductor device used for switching and amplification.

The three terminals are:

- **emitter (E)**;
- **base (B)**;
- **collector (C)**.

The semiconductor regions are arranged in one of two orders:

- **N-P-N**, forming an **NPN transistor**;
- **P-N-P**, forming a **PNP transistor**.

### Why the Terminals Are Called Emitter, Base, and Collector

The names describe the physical roles of the regions.

The **emitter** supplies, or *emits*, charge carriers into the thin base region.

The **base** is the narrow control region between the emitter and collector. The electrical condition at the base determines whether the transistor allows substantial current through its main current path.

The **collector** collects most of the carriers that successfully pass through the base region.

The emitter and collector therefore have different intended functions even though both are part of the main current path.

### How Base Current Controls an NPN Transistor

The NPN transistor provides a useful example of BJT operation.

When the base-emitter junction is not sufficiently forward biased:

- very little base current flows;
- only a very small collector-emitter current normally flows;
- the transistor is treated as **OFF** in a simplified switching model.

To turn the transistor on, the base is driven positive relative to the emitter so that the base-emitter junction becomes forward biased.

A small **base current** can then flow.

That small base current is associated with carrier injection from the emitter into the thin base region. In an NPN transistor, electrons are injected from the N-type emitter into the P-type base.

The base is intentionally thin, so only a portion of the injected electrons recombine in the base. Most cross the base region and are swept into the collector.

As a result:

- a relatively small current is required at the base;
- a much larger current can flow through the collector-emitter path.

This is the physical basis of the simplified statement:

> **A small base current controls a larger collector-emitter current.**

The base does **not** supply all of the collector current. The external collector circuit provides the electrical energy for the larger collector-emitter current. The base controls whether the transistor permits that larger current path.

This distinction is important because a transistor is a **control device**, not a source of electrical energy.

### BJT as a Switch

For a simple switching application, we can think of the transistor as having two approximate states.

**Base not driven sufficiently:**

- transistor approximately OFF;
- collector-emitter current is very small.

**Base driven sufficiently:**

- transistor turns ON;
- substantial collector-emitter current can flow, subject to the external circuit and device limits.

A real BJT does not change between perfect zero-current and zero-resistance states. The switch analogy is only a useful first model.

### What About a PNP Transistor?

A **PNP transistor** operates according to the same general BJT principle but with opposite semiconductor polarities and reversed electrical polarities and conventional-current directions.

For a PNP transistor:

- the emitter is normally at a higher electrical potential;
- the base must be driven sufficiently lower than the emitter to produce the appropriate base-emitter condition;
- conventional current in the main path normally flows from emitter toward collector.

At this level, the important idea is not to memorize detailed carrier transport for both devices. It is to recognize that:

> **NPN and PNP are complementary BJT structures. Both use the base region to control a larger current through the emitter-collector structure.**

### Discrete and Integrated BJTs

A BJT can be manufactured as an individual semiconductor device placed inside its own package and mounted on a PCB.

A BJT can also be fabricated directly inside an integrated circuit. Analog, RF, interface, and mixed-signal ICs may contain many BJTs, and some technologies combine BJTs and MOSFETs on the same die.

Section 1.6 develops the distinction between **discrete components** and **integrated circuit elements** in more detail.

## 1.4.4 MOSFET Structure

The **metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor (MOSFET)** is one of the most important semiconductor devices used in modern electronics.

MOSFETs can be manufactured as individual packaged components, such as power MOSFETs mounted on a PCB, or they can be fabricated in very large numbers inside an integrated circuit.

Two complementary forms are especially important:

- **NMOS**, which uses an electron-rich N-type channel when turned on;
- **PMOS**, which uses a hole-rich P-type channel when turned on.

Both use an insulated **gate** to control a conducting path between the **source** and **drain**.

### The Four MOSFET Terminals

A MOSFET is commonly described using four electrical regions or terminals:

- **gate (G)**;
- **source (S)**;
- **drain (D)**;
- **body (B)**, also called the substrate or well connection depending on the device structure.

The **gate** is the control terminal.

It is separated from the semiconductor by a very thin **gate dielectric**, so normal MOSFET operation does not require a continuous current flowing through the gate dielectric.

Instead, voltage applied between the gate and source creates an **electric field** that changes the carrier population near the semiconductor surface.

The **source** and **drain** form the two ends of the controlled current path.

In the simplified operating convention used here:

- the **source** is the terminal from which the majority carriers enter the controlled channel;
- the **drain** is the terminal toward which those carriers move through the channel;
- the **body** is the semiconductor region in which the source and drain regions are formed.

> **Important:** In some MOSFET structures, the source and drain regions can be physically very similar. Their names describe their normal circuit roles and bias conditions rather than necessarily identifying two completely different semiconductor structures.

### NMOS Structure

A simplified planar enhancement-mode **NMOS** transistor contains:

- a **P-type body or well**;
- two heavily doped **N+ regions** that form the source and drain;
- a gate electrode above the semiconductor surface;
- a thin gate dielectric separating the gate from the semiconductor.

With the gate not sufficiently positive relative to the source, there is no continuous N-type inversion channel between the source and drain.

When the gate becomes sufficiently positive relative to the source, the electric field through the gate dielectric can create an electron-rich inversion channel at the semiconductor surface.

### PMOS Structure

A simplified planar enhancement-mode **PMOS** transistor uses the complementary arrangement:

- an **N-type body or well**;
- two heavily doped **P+ regions** that form the source and drain;
- an insulated gate above the semiconductor surface.

For a PMOS device, the gate must normally be driven sufficiently **lower than the source** to create a hole-rich P-type inversion channel.

### BJT Control Compared with MOSFET Control

The physical control mechanisms are different.

| BJT | MOSFET |
|---|---|
| Base is the control terminal | Gate is the control terminal |
| Base-emitter condition and base current control the main current path | Gate-to-source voltage controls the conducting channel |
| Emitter supplies carriers and collector collects them | Source supplies majority carriers to the channel and drain receives them |
| Base current is part of normal operation | Ideally, no steady current passes through the insulated gate dielectric |

These are simplified engineering descriptions, but they provide the physical picture needed to understand transistor switching.

## 1.4.5 How a MOSFET Works: NMOS Example and PMOS Comparison

A useful first model of a MOSFET is a **voltage-controlled switch**.

We will use an enhancement-mode **NMOS** transistor as the main example because its operation provides a clear bridge between semiconductor structure and digital switching.

### Gate-to-Source Voltage Controls the Channel

The important control voltage is the **gate-to-source voltage**:

$$
V_{GS}=V_G-V_S
$$

where:

- $V_G$ is the gate voltage;
- $V_S$ is the source voltage;
- $V_{GS}$ is the gate voltage relative to the source.

For the enhancement-mode NMOS used here:

- gate not sufficiently positive relative to source → transistor approximately **OFF**;
- gate sufficiently positive relative to source → an inversion channel forms → transistor can turn **ON**.

A device parameter called the **threshold voltage**, commonly written $V_{TH}$, is associated with the beginning of strong channel formation.

The exact value depends on the device and operating conditions. Do not assume one universal threshold voltage.

### NMOS OFF State

When $V_{GS}$ is below the required threshold condition:

- the N+ source and N+ drain remain separated by the P-type semiconductor near the surface;
- there is no continuous electron-rich inversion channel;
- drain-source current is very small compared with the ON state.

For a simplified switching model, the transistor is considered **OFF**.

A real MOSFET can still have leakage current, so OFF does not mean exactly zero current.

### Forming the NMOS Inversion Channel

When the gate becomes sufficiently positive relative to the source, the gate voltage creates an electric field through the gate dielectric.

The electric field changes the carrier distribution near the P-type semiconductor surface:

1. holes are repelled from the region beneath the gate;
2. electrons are attracted toward the surface;
3. the electron concentration increases;
4. a continuous electron-rich **inversion channel** forms between source and drain.

The surface is called *inverted* because it began as P-type material but now behaves locally as an N-type conducting region.

> **Important:** The conducting channel is an **inversion channel**. It is not a new depletion region, and it is not a physical metal wire inserted between source and drain.

### Drain-to-Source Voltage Drives Current

Forming a channel makes conduction possible, but it does not by itself create drain-source current.

A voltage difference must also exist between the drain and source:

$$
V_{DS}=V_D-V_S
$$

The two voltages have different roles:

| Voltage | Main role |
|---|---|
| $V_{GS}$ | Controls whether the conducting channel forms |
| $V_{DS}$ | Provides the electrical driving potential for current through the channel |

Therefore:

> **$V_{GS}$ controls the channel. $V_{DS}$ drives current through the channel.**

For the common NMOS operating condition used in this introductory example, the drain is at a higher potential than the source.

When the channel is present:

- electrons move mainly from **source toward drain**;
- conventional current is in the opposite direction, from **drain toward source**.

### NMOS OFF and ON States at a Glance

| Condition | Gate-to-source condition | Channel beneath gate | Drain-source behavior |
|---|---|---|---|
| **OFF** | Not sufficiently positive | No continuous inversion channel | Only small leakage current in a real device |
| **ON** | Sufficiently positive | Electron-rich inversion channel forms | Current can flow when $V_{DS}$ is applied |

The words **OFF** and **ON** are useful circuit-level descriptions, but a real MOSFET is not an ideal mechanical switch.

When ON, the device still has electrical resistance and can dissipate heat. When OFF, leakage current is not exactly zero. The device also contains capacitance, so changing the gate voltage requires charging and discharging the gate-related capacitances.

These non-ideal behaviors become important later in electronic packaging because they affect:

- heat generation;
- power delivery;
- switching current;
- electrical noise;
- device and package temperature;
- reliability.

### PMOS Uses the Same Principle with Opposite Polarity

A PMOS transistor uses the same basic field-effect principle but with complementary semiconductor types and opposite gate-polarity behavior.

For a simplified enhancement-mode PMOS:

- the source is normally at the higher electrical potential;
- the gate must be driven sufficiently **lower than the source** to create a hole-rich P-type inversion channel;
- holes are the majority carriers in the channel.

Using the same definition,

$$
V_{GS}=V_G-V_S
$$

the PMOS turns on when $V_{GS}$ becomes sufficiently negative for that device.

In the common PMOS operating condition:

- holes move from **source toward drain**;
- conventional current also flows from **source toward drain**.

This differs from NMOS, where electrons move from source toward drain while conventional current is in the opposite direction.

### NMOS and PMOS Compared

| Feature | NMOS | PMOS |
|---|---|---|
| Source/drain regions | N+ | P+ |
| Body or well | P-type | N-type |
| ON-state majority carrier | Electrons | Holes |
| Gate condition for ON | Gate sufficiently **positive relative to source** | Gate sufficiently **lower than source** |
| ON-state channel | N-type inversion channel | P-type inversion channel |
| Carrier motion in common operation | Source → drain | Source → drain |
| Conventional-current direction | Drain → source | Source → drain |

The most important idea for later digital circuits is:

> **NMOS and PMOS respond to opposite gate-polarity conditions.**

This complementary behavior is the basis of **CMOS** technology.

Section 1.5 will use NMOS and PMOS devices to show how transistors can be combined into logic circuits and other functional electronic blocks.

## 1.4.6 Why MOSFETs Are Used in Most Modern Integrated Circuits

Both **BJTs** and **MOSFETs** can be fabricated inside integrated circuits. However, most modern high-density digital ICs are based primarily on MOSFET technology.

This does not mean that BJTs are obsolete or that MOSFETs are always better. The two transistor families have different strengths.

For processors, memory, digital controllers, and many other highly integrated chips, MOSFETs have several major advantages.

### Very Small Steady-State Gate Current

A MOSFET uses an insulated gate.

Ideally, no continuous current flows through the gate dielectric while the transistor is held in a stable state. The gate primarily behaves like a small capacitance that must be charged or discharged when the transistor switches.

A BJT operates differently. Base current is normally part of its operating condition.

For a single transistor, this difference may not seem important. For an IC containing millions or billions of transistors, however, continuously supplying control current to a very large number of devices would create substantial power consumption.

This is one reason MOSFETs are especially attractive for large digital circuits.

> **Key Idea:** A MOSFET is controlled primarily by **gate voltage**, while a BJT normally requires **base current** as part of its control.

### NMOS and PMOS Can Form Low-Power CMOS Logic

An even more important advantage comes from combining **NMOS** and **PMOS** transistors.

This complementary arrangement is called **CMOS**, or **complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor**.

In an idealized CMOS logic circuit:

- when one transistor path is ON, the complementary path is OFF;
- in a stable logic state, there is ideally no continuous low-resistance path directly from the power supply to ground.

Real CMOS circuits still consume power because of leakage and because transistor and interconnect capacitances must be charged and discharged during switching. However, CMOS makes it possible to build very large digital systems with much lower static power than many traditional bipolar logic approaches.

Section 1.5 introduces CMOS logic in more detail.

### MOSFETs Can Be Integrated at Very High Density

MOSFET structures are well suited to semiconductor fabrication methods based on lithography, thin films, doping, dielectric formation, and patterned interconnections.

As semiconductor manufacturing has advanced, MOSFET dimensions have been reduced dramatically, allowing increasingly large numbers of transistors to be fabricated on a single die.

This high integration density is essential for products such as:

- microprocessors;
- microcontrollers;
- memory;
- graphics processors;
- digital signal processors;
- programmable logic;
- system-on-chip devices.

Modern transistor structures no longer always use the simple planar MOSFET geometry shown earlier in this section. Advanced technologies use structures such as **FinFETs** and **gate-all-around (GAA) transistors** to improve electrostatic control as dimensions become smaller.

These devices still use the same basic field-effect idea:

> **An insulated gate creates an electric field that controls the conducting channel.**

### MOSFETs Are Easy to Combine into Large Logic Networks

Because a MOSFET gate requires very little steady-state input current, the output of one logic stage can control the gates of other MOSFETs without continuously supplying substantial control current to each gate.

This property helps engineers build large networks of interconnected logic gates efficiently.

Together with CMOS operation and continued device scaling, this makes MOS technology particularly suitable for extremely complex digital ICs.

### Why Not Use BJTs for Everything?

BJTs also have important advantages.

Depending on the application, BJTs can provide useful characteristics such as:

- high current gain;
- strong analog performance;
- high transconductance for a given operating current;
- useful high-frequency behavior;
- accurate analog relationships that can be exploited in circuit design.

BJTs therefore remain important in many:

- analog circuits;
- RF circuits;
- precision references;
- sensor interfaces;
- high-speed communication circuits;
- power and driver circuits.

Some semiconductor technologies deliberately combine bipolar transistors and CMOS circuitry on the same die. These are commonly called **BiCMOS** technologies.

The question is therefore not:

> **“Are BJTs good or bad?”**

The better question is:

> **“Which transistor technology provides the best combination of power, speed, density, cost, and electrical performance for the intended circuit?”**

For very high-density digital logic, MOSFET-based CMOS technology has generally provided the most practical combination.

### BJT and MOSFET Integration Compared

| Characteristic | BJT | MOSFET / CMOS |
|---|---|---|
| Control mechanism | Base-emitter condition with associated base current | Electric field controlled by gate voltage |
| Steady control current | Normally requires base current | Ideally very small gate current |
| Complementary digital operation | Possible circuit implementations, but not the dominant high-density approach | NMOS + PMOS naturally form CMOS logic |
| Static power in large digital logic | Can be relatively high depending on circuit family | Very low in the ideal CMOS stable-state model |
| Integration density | Can be highly integrated, but generally less attractive for massive digital logic | Extremely high integration density is practical |
| Common strengths | Analog, RF, precision, specialized high-speed and interface functions | Digital logic, processors, memory, controllers, highly integrated systems |
| Still used today? | **Yes** | **Yes — dominant for high-density digital ICs** |

> **Engineering Perspective:** MOSFETs did not replace BJTs because BJTs stopped working. MOSFET-based CMOS became dominant in high-density digital ICs because it combines very high integration density with low steady-state control power and efficient complementary switching.

### The Packaging Consequence

The ability to integrate enormous numbers of MOSFETs on one die has transformed electronic packaging.

More transistor integration can provide greater computational capability and reduce the number of separate components required on a PCB, but it also creates more demanding package requirements.

Highly integrated ICs can require:

- greater electrical power delivery;
- lower-impedance power and ground paths;
- removal of concentrated heat from a small die area;
- very large numbers of high-speed signal connections;
- control of package and interconnect parasitics;
- high-density package substrates and interconnects;
- careful management of thermal-mechanical reliability.

In other words:

> **MOSFET scaling made extraordinarily complex chips possible, but packaging is what allows those chips to receive power, communicate with the rest of the system, remove heat, and operate reliably.**

## Why This Matters for Electronic Packaging

The device concepts in this section are included because transistor operation creates physical requirements that electronic packaging must support.

### Semiconductor devices generate heat

BJTs and MOSFETs dissipate power during conduction and switching.

A discrete transistor may therefore need:

- a low-resistance electrical connection;
- PCB copper for heat spreading;
- a thermal pad;
- a heat sink;
- airflow;
- or another thermal path.

Inside an IC, large numbers of switching transistors collectively generate heat that must leave the die through the package and surrounding assembly.

### Packages must provide the device terminals

The microscopic transistor structures exist inside the semiconductor die.

A discrete transistor package must convert those microscopic structures into practical external connections such as:

- leads;
- pads;
- tabs;
- or solderable terminations.

An IC package must provide the same basic function for many internal circuit connections.

### Switching affects power and signal behavior

Rapid transistor switching creates rapidly changing current.

The physical package and PCB interconnect therefore influence:

- voltage drop;
- power integrity;
- signal integrity;
- electromagnetic interference;
- and switching performance.

These topics are developed later in Chapter 5.

### Device structures can be electrically damaged

Small semiconductor structures and insulating layers can be sensitive to:

- electrostatic discharge;
- excessive voltage;
- excessive current;
- excessive temperature.

Packaging, manufacturing, grounding, ESD control, and thermal management therefore interact directly with the semiconductor-device behavior introduced here.

> **Engineering Perspective:** Understanding a transistor does not require becoming a semiconductor-device designer. For electronic packaging, the important question is how the physical device must be connected, powered, cooled, protected, manufactured, and kept reliable.

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- schematic symbols represent electrical function and connectivity rather than exact physical geometry;
- **BJTs** and **FETs** are two major transistor families;
- both BJT and MOSFET devices can exist as discrete packaged components or as devices fabricated inside an IC;
- an NPN BJT uses a small base-controlled input to control a larger collector-emitter current;
- the BJT **emitter** supplies carriers, the **base** controls the device, and the **collector** collects most of the carriers that cross the base;
- MOSFETs use an insulated **gate** and an electric field to control a conducting channel between **source** and **drain**;
- a typical enhancement-mode NMOS turns on when the gate is sufficiently positive relative to the source;
- $V_{GS}$ controls NMOS channel formation, while $V_{DS}$ provides the driving potential for drain-source current;
- PMOS uses the same field-effect principle with complementary semiconductor structure and opposite gate-polarity behavior;
- NMOS and PMOS together provide the device foundation for CMOS circuits.

The next section moves from **individual semiconductor devices** to **electronic circuits**, showing how transistors can be interconnected to create analog functions, digital logic, and functional circuit blocks.
