# 1.7 - From Discrete Components to Integrated Circuits

Section 1.6 showed how individual devices can be connected to create useful circuit functions. A transistor can become part of an inverter, logic gates can be combined into a half-adder, and many simple circuits can be organized into larger functional blocks.

The next question is physical:

> **Where do all of those devices and interconnections actually exist?**

One possibility is to build the circuit from **discrete components**. Each transistor, diode, resistor, capacitor, or other device is manufactured as a separate component, packaged individually, mounted on a PCB, and connected through PCB traces and other interconnects.

Another possibility is to fabricate many of the required active devices, passive structures, and electrical interconnections together on a single semiconductor die. The result is an **integrated circuit (IC)**.

This section compares those two approaches and explains why integration became one of the foundations of modern electronics.

The goal is not to teach IC physical design or semiconductor fabrication. Section 1.8 introduces fabrication at a conceptual level. Here, the important idea is the change in **physical realization**:

> **Separate components connected externally → many circuit elements and interconnections fabricated together on one semiconductor die**

> **Engineering Perspective:** A schematic may show the same electrical function regardless of how it is physically implemented. Packaging engineers and technologists must understand whether that function is built from separate board-level components, integrated inside a die, or divided between an IC and external components.

## 1.7.1 What Is a Discrete-Component Circuit?

For this section, a **discrete-component circuit** means a circuit implemented using individual physical components rather than fabricating those device functions together on one semiconductor die.

Typical discrete circuit elements include:

- a packaged transistor;
- a packaged diode;
- a chip resistor;
- a ceramic capacitor;
- a discrete inductor.

In electronics, the phrase **discrete semiconductor device** is commonly used for an individual semiconductor device such as a diode or transistor, in contrast with an IC that contains many devices on one die. At the PCB-assembly level, however, both discrete devices and packaged ICs appear as separate physical components mounted on the board.

In a typical PCB assembly, discrete components are mounted onto the board and electrically connected by:

- copper traces;
- planes;
- vias;
- solder joints;
- package leads or terminals;
- connectors or wiring where required.

The components retain their own physical identities.

For example, a simple transistor switching circuit could use:

- one packaged transistor;
- several separate resistors;
- one or more capacitors;
- a PCB that provides the electrical connections.

The circuit function comes from the way those separate components are connected.

### Why discrete components are still important

Integrated circuits did not eliminate discrete components.

Discrete devices remain useful when a circuit requires characteristics that are difficult, inefficient, or uneconomical to integrate on the same semiconductor die.

Examples include:

- large capacitance;
- large inductance;
- high voltage isolation;
- high current or high power handling;
- large energy storage;
- specialized sensing or actuation;
- connectors and external interfaces;
- components that must be selected or changed independently.

A modern PCBA therefore usually contains a combination of:

- integrated circuits;
- discrete passive components;
- discrete semiconductor devices;
- connectors and other electromechanical components.

> **Key Idea:** "Discrete" does not mean old-fashioned or inferior. It means that the component remains a separate physical device rather than being fabricated as part of the same semiconductor die as the rest of the circuit.

## 1.7.2 What Is an Integrated Circuit?

An **integrated circuit (IC)** is an electronic circuit in which many devices and electrical interconnections are fabricated together on a semiconductor die.

For the introductory discussion in this section, the main model is a **monolithic integrated circuit**.

**Monolithic** means that the circuit elements are formed together as part of one semiconductor die rather than assembled from individually packaged components.

Depending on the IC technology and function, the die may contain:

- MOSFETs;
- BJTs;
- diodes;
- resistive structures;
- capacitive structures;
- inductive or other patterned-metal structures where useful;
- electrical isolation structures;
- multiple layers of metal interconnect;
- input/output interface structures.

The exact combination depends on the circuit and fabrication process.

The integrated passive structures introduced in Section 1.5 are therefore not tiny packaged resistors, capacitors, or inductors hidden inside the die. They are physical structures created from semiconductor, dielectric, and metal layers as part of the IC.

Likewise, the logic gates introduced in Section 1.6 are **functional abstractions**. Inside an IC, they are realized by transistor structures and on-die interconnections rather than by placing miniature packaged logic-gate components inside the silicon.

> **Critical Distinction:** An integrated circuit is a circuit fabricated on a semiconductor die. It is **not** a printed circuit board.

## 1.7.3 Same Circuit Function, Different Physical Realization

The difference between a discrete circuit and an integrated circuit is easier to understand if we separate **electrical function** from **physical implementation**.

Suppose a circuit requires several transistor switches, resistors, and interconnections.

### Discrete realization

The circuit may be built from separate packaged components:

1. each transistor is manufactured and packaged individually;
2. each resistor or capacitor is a separate component;
3. the components are mounted onto a PCB;
4. PCB traces and solder joints create the required electrical connections.

The circuit occupies board area and may require many component-to-board interconnections.

### Integrated realization

The same general electrical function may instead be implemented on one semiconductor die:

1. transistor structures are fabricated within the semiconductor;
2. passive functions are created using appropriate on-die structures where practical;
3. patterned metal layers connect the devices;
4. the completed die performs the intended circuit function.

The circuit elements are still physically real. Integration does not eliminate wires, resistances, capacitances, or current paths. Instead, their dimensions, materials, and manufacturing methods are very different from the corresponding PCB-level structures.

This distinction is central to electronic packaging:

> **A schematic tells us what must be connected. Physical implementation determines whether those connections exist as PCB traces between separate packages or as microscopic interconnects within a semiconductor die.**

## 1.7.4 Functional Blocks Inside an IC

A useful way to understand a modern IC is to think in terms of **hierarchy**.

Section 1.6 introduced the progression:

> **transistor → logic gate → functional circuit → larger functional block**

Inside an IC, the same hierarchy continues.

A digital IC may contain blocks such as:

- arithmetic circuits;
- registers;
- memory arrays;
- control logic;
- timing circuits;
- communication interfaces.

An analog IC may contain blocks such as:

- amplifiers;
- references;
- comparators;
- filters;
- bias circuits;
- output drivers.

A mixed-signal IC may combine:

- analog input circuits;
- analog-to-digital converters (ADCs);
- digital processing;
- memory;
- digital-to-analog converters (DACs);
- analog or power-output interfaces.

For example, a microcontroller may include a processor core, memory, timers, communication interfaces, ADCs, and other functions on the same die.

The internal functional blocks are not separate PCB assemblies. They are circuit regions created from large numbers of integrated devices and connected through on-die interconnect layers.

### From a few devices to very large circuits

The half-adder in Section 1.6 showed that a small number of gates can create a useful function.

Larger digital systems repeat this same idea many times:

- gates form adders and registers;
- adders and registers form arithmetic and data-processing blocks;
- many functional blocks form processors, controllers, memory systems, and other complex ICs.

The same hierarchical idea applies to analog and mixed-signal circuits, although the circuit structures and design methods are different.

> **Engineering Perspective:** The number of internal devices can become extremely large, but the packaging problem still requires the die to communicate with the outside world through a manageable number of physical power, ground, and signal connections.

## 1.7.5 Why Integrate a Circuit?

Integrating many circuit elements onto one semiconductor die provides several important advantages.

### Smaller physical size

A transistor fabricated inside an IC can occupy far less physical space than a separately packaged transistor mounted on a PCB.

When many functions are integrated:

- fewer separate packages may be required;
- less PCB area may be needed for the same electronic function;
- the overall product can become smaller and lighter.

This is one of the major reasons highly functional portable electronics are possible.

### Shorter internal interconnections

Connections between devices inside an IC can be much shorter than connections between separately packaged components on a PCB.

Shorter interconnections can reduce:

- propagation distance;
- many package- and board-scale parasitic effects;
- the number of package-to-board transitions required inside a functional block.

These advantages can support higher operating speed and more compact circuit organization.

However, on-die interconnects are not ideal. Their resistance, capacitance, coupling, delay, and current-carrying limits remain important parts of IC design.

### Very high device count

Semiconductor fabrication allows large numbers of devices to be patterned and processed together across a wafer.

This makes it practical to place many transistors and functional blocks on one die rather than assembling each transistor as a separate packaged component.

High integration enables products such as:

- microprocessors;
- microcontrollers;
- memory devices;
- graphics processors;
- communication ICs;
- sensor-interface ICs;
- mixed-signal systems.

### Repeatable batch fabrication

Many nominally identical devices and interconnections can be fabricated through the same controlled wafer-processing sequence.

Processing many nominally identical structures through the same controlled wafer-level sequence supports repeatable manufacturing of complex circuits without assembling the equivalent internal function from very large numbers of separately packaged devices.

"Repeatable" does not mean that every device is physically identical. Semiconductor processes still have manufacturing variation, manufacturing yield is not 100%, and completed dies must be electrically tested.

### Lower cost per function at production volume

Integrated circuits can require very large investments in:

- circuit design;
- verification;
- masks and process development;
- semiconductor manufacturing equipment.

However, once a suitable design and manufacturing process are established, large numbers of circuit functions can be fabricated together across many dies.

For high production volume, this can produce a very low **cost per electronic function** compared with building the same complex function from thousands or millions of separately packaged components.

> **Important:** Integration does not automatically mean lower total cost for every application. Development cost, wafer cost, die area, manufacturing yield, package cost, production volume, and required performance all influence the final economics.

## 1.7.6 Why Not Integrate Everything?

An IC can contain many functions, but not every component or system requirement is best placed on the same die.

Integration is an engineering tradeoff.

### Large passive values can require too much die area

Section 1.5 showed that resistors, capacitors, and inductors can be fabricated on a die.

However:

- large capacitance can require substantial area;
- large inductance is difficult to obtain efficiently on a small semiconductor die;
- integrated inductors can have significant resistance and parasitic effects;
- some precision or high-energy-storage components are more practical off-chip.

This is why a processor or controller may contain large numbers of transistors while still requiring many external capacitors, resistors, and inductors on the PCB.

### High current and high power create physical limits

Higher current requires sufficiently capable conductors, device structures, contacts, and package connections.

High power also produces heat.

As more active devices operate in a small die area, heat generation can become concentrated, creating:

- high power density;
- local hot spots;
- demanding thermal paths;
- greater requirements for the IC package and system cooling solution.

Integration therefore reduces physical size while potentially making thermal management more difficult.

### External connections are still necessary

An IC cannot operate as an isolated piece of semiconductor.

It still needs connections for some combination of:

- power;
- ground;
- input signals;
- output signals;
- clocks;
- communication interfaces;
- sensors;
- actuators;
- external memory or storage;
- external passive components.

These connections eventually pass through the IC package and into the PCB or another system-level interconnect.

### Different functions may need different technologies

Not every electronic function benefits from the same semiconductor process.

For example, a system may require:

- dense digital logic;
- high-voltage power switching;
- RF circuitry;
- precision analog circuitry;
- optical devices;
- MEMS sensors.

Some products integrate several of these functions on one die. Others use multiple specialized dies or packaged components because different technologies provide better performance, cost, manufacturing yield, or reliability for different functions.

Modern packaging can also combine separately fabricated dies within one package or module. That is an important form of **package-level integration**, but it should not be confused with a monolithic IC in which the circuit is fabricated on one semiconductor die. This distinction becomes increasingly important when discussing system-in-package (SiP), chiplet, and heterogeneous-integration technologies later in the book.

## 1.7.7 Discrete Circuit and Integrated Circuit Compared

| Feature | Discrete-Component Circuit | Monolithic Integrated Circuit |
|---|---|---|
| Physical devices | Separate individual components, such as packaged transistors and passive components | Many devices fabricated on one semiconductor die |
| Main internal connections | PCB traces, solder joints, package leads/terminals, wiring | Patterned on-die metal interconnects |
| Typical physical scale | Board/package level | Semiconductor-die level |
| Passive components | Wide range of discrete values and technologies available | Values and structures limited by process, area, parasitics, and performance requirements |
| Power/current capability | Can use separately optimized high-power components and large conductors | Limited by device, on-die interconnect, thermal, and package constraints |
| Functional density | Lower for a given physical area | Can be extremely high |
| Modification or replacement | Individual components may sometimes be replaced | Internal devices are not individually replaced; the IC is treated as one component |
| Manufacturing economics | Practical for low volume, prototyping, specialized functions, and external components | High development cost can be offset by low cost per function at sufficient production volume |
| Packaging need | Each component requires an appropriate physical form and board connection | The completed die normally still requires IC packaging or another controlled die-attachment/interconnection method |
| Role in a modern PCBA | Common and essential | Common and essential |

The two approaches are therefore complementary.

A typical electronic product does not choose between "all discrete" and "all integrated." Instead, the designer decides which functions should be integrated and which should remain external.

## 1.7.8 An Integrated Circuit Is Still a Component in a Larger System

A completed IC die may contain an enormous amount of circuit functionality, but it is not yet the finished electronic product.

The die still has to interface with the physical world.

In a typical product, the progression is:

> **integrated circuit on a die → IC package → packaged IC component → PCB assembly → larger electronic product**

The **IC package** provides the physical bridge between microscopic on-die connections and the much larger structures used at the PCB level.

The PCB then connects that packaged IC to:

- power sources and regulators;
- discrete passive components;
- other ICs;
- connectors;
- sensors;
- actuators;
- communication interfaces;
- system hardware.

This gives an important packaging lesson:

> **Integration moves many circuit functions onto the die, but it does not eliminate electronic packaging. It changes the scale and difficulty of the packaging problem.**

Higher functional density can create more demanding requirements for:

- power delivery;
- heat removal;
- signal interconnection;
- input/output density;
- mechanical protection;
- manufacturing and test.

## 1.7.9 An IC Is Not a PCB

Because both ICs and PCBs contain patterned electrical conductors, beginners sometimes confuse the two.

They are fundamentally different structures produced by different manufacturing processes.

### Integrated circuit

An IC contains semiconductor devices and on-die interconnections fabricated on or within a semiconductor wafer.

Its fabrication creates microscopic active devices such as transistors and the interconnections that form the circuit.

### Printed circuit board

A PCB is a separately manufactured interconnect and mechanical-support structure.

It normally contains:

- patterned copper traces and planes;
- dielectric laminate materials;
- pads;
- vias and plated holes;
- solder mask;
- surface finish;
- mechanical features.

A PCB does not normally create the microscopic transistor structures that perform the logic and amplification functions inside a semiconductor IC.

The two manufacturing streams eventually meet during electronic assembly, but one does not turn into the other.

> **Critical Manufacturing Distinction:**  
> **Semiconductor fabrication → IC die → IC packaging → packaged component**  
> is different from  
> **PCB fabrication → bare PCB**.  
> The packaged components and the bare PCB later come together during **PCB assembly**.

Later sections develop this sequence step by step.

## Why This Matters for Electronic Packaging

The transition from discrete circuits to integrated circuits is important to packaging for several reasons.

### Integration changes the interconnection scale

Inside the die, devices are connected by microscopic patterned conductors.

Outside the die, the package must transform those microscopic connections into physical interfaces that can connect to a PCB or another package-level structure.

This transition in scale is one of the central purposes of IC packaging.

### Integration concentrates power and heat

Placing more active devices into a smaller area can increase local heat generation and power density.

The package becomes part of the thermal path from:

> **transistor junctions → die → package → PCB / heat spreader / cooling system → environment**

### Integration can increase input/output demands

A more capable IC may need many power, ground, signal, and communication connections.

The package must provide those connections in a practical physical form.

### Integration does not eliminate external components

Many functions remain more practical outside the die.

The PCBA therefore combines:

- highly integrated ICs;
- discrete passive components;
- discrete semiconductor devices where needed;
- connectors and electromechanical hardware;
- the PCB that interconnects them.

This mixture of integration levels is normal in modern electronic products.

> **Engineering Perspective:** The IC performs a large amount of electronic function in a very small area. The package and PCB allow that concentrated function to receive power, communicate with other parts of the system, remove heat, and survive manufacturing and service conditions.

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- a **discrete-component circuit** is assembled from separately manufactured physical components;
- an **integrated circuit** contains many devices and interconnections fabricated together on a semiconductor die;
- a **monolithic IC** forms its circuit elements and interconnections as part of one semiconductor die;
- integrated devices are physical semiconductor and metal structures, not miniature packaged components hidden inside the die;
- logic gates and functional blocks are circuit abstractions that are physically realized by integrated devices and interconnections;
- ICs can contain analog, digital, and mixed-signal functions;
- integration supports smaller size, shorter internal interconnections, very high device count, repeatable manufacturing, and low cost per function at sufficient production volume;
- integration also creates tradeoffs involving die area, passive-component values, current and power handling, heat concentration, process compatibility, and external-interface requirements;
- modern PCB assemblies normally use both integrated circuits and discrete components;
- a highly integrated IC still requires physical power, signal, thermal, mechanical, and protective interfaces to the rest of the system;
- a semiconductor IC is **not a PCB** and does not become a PCB;
- semiconductor fabrication and PCB fabrication are separate manufacturing streams that later converge during PCB assembly.

The next section explains, at a conceptual level, **how an integrated circuit is physically fabricated on a semiconductor wafer**. It follows the repeated patterning and material-processing steps that create active devices, passive structures, and on-die interconnections, ending with individual IC dies ready for the next stage of packaging.

### Technical points to preserve

- A discrete circuit uses separately manufactured components connected through PCB traces, solder joints, package terminals, and other external interconnects.

- Logic-gate and functional-block symbols are abstractions; do not draw them as miniature packaged components inside the die.

- Do not imply that every passive component should be integrated.

  - semiconductor fabrication → IC die;
  - IC packaging → packaged component;
  - PCB fabrication → bare PCB;
  - components + bare PCB → PCB assembly / PCBA.
