## Suggested Content for Figure 1.3.6

**Instructional purpose:**  
Connect the PN-junction physics developed in Figures 1.3.4 and 1.3.5 to the practical diode component that readers will encounter in circuits, PCB assemblies, troubleshooting, and component selection.

The figure should answer three practical questions:

1. What are the two diode terminals?
2. What happens when the diode is forward biased?
3. What happens when the diode is reverse biased?

The figure should emphasize practical circuit behavior rather than repeat the detailed depletion-region physics already shown in Figure 1.3.5.

**Filename:**  
`1.3.6 - How a Semiconductor Diode Works in a Circuit.png`

**Intended width:** 900 px in Markdown

**Recommended aspect ratio:** 16:9

### Overall layout

Use a clean three-panel horizontal progression:

1. **Diode and terminals**
2. **Forward bias**
3. **Reverse bias**

Use the same restrained engineering-textbook style as the surrounding semiconductor figures in Section 1.3.

The overall figure should visually connect:

**PN-junction device → diode symbol/component → forward-bias conduction → reverse-bias blocking behavior**

Do not embed a separate overall figure title. The external Markdown caption provides the title.

---

### Panel 1 – Diode and Terminals

Show a simple conceptual semiconductor diode as a two-terminal device.

Include:

- P-type region on the left;
- N-type region on the right;
- a PN junction between them;
- external terminal on the P side labeled **Anode (A)**;
- external terminal on the N side labeled **Cathode (K)**;
- a standard semiconductor-diode schematic symbol beside or below the conceptual device;
- clear correspondence between:
  - P side ↔ anode;
  - N side ↔ cathode.

The internal junction representation should be simplified. A depletion region may be shown subtly, but detailed fixed-ion populations are not required because they were already explained in Figures 1.3.4 and 1.3.5.

Recommended labels:

- `P-type`
- `N-type`
- `Anode (A)`
- `Cathode (K)`
- `PN junction`
- `Diode symbol`

The schematic diode symbol must use correct conventional geometry. The cathode side of the symbol must be clearly identified by the cathode bar.

---

### Panel 2 – Forward Bias

Show the same diode connected in a simple DC circuit with a source and a current-limiting resistor or generic load.

Required electrical condition:

- positive source terminal connected toward the **anode**;
- negative source terminal connected toward the **cathode**.

Show:

- conventional current flowing through the external circuit and through the diode;
- current direction through the diode from **anode → cathode**;
- a visually strong current arrow;
- the diode described as conducting strongly.

Recommended labels:

- `Forward bias`
- `Anode positive`
- `Cathode negative`
- `Conventional current`
- `Conducts strongly`

If an internal PN-junction cutaway is included, it may show a relatively narrow depletion region, but this must remain secondary to the circuit-level message.

Do not assign a universal numerical forward voltage such as 0.7 V.

---

### Panel 3 – Reverse Bias

Show the same diode with the source polarity reversed.

Required electrical condition:

- negative source terminal connected toward the **anode**;
- positive source terminal connected toward the **cathode**.

Show:

- no large majority-carrier current;
- if reverse current is shown, use only a very small dashed or thin arrow;
- reverse leakage conventional-current direction through the diode from **cathode → anode**;
- the diode described as blocking most current under normal reverse-bias conditions.

Recommended labels:

- `Reverse bias`
- `Anode negative`
- `Cathode positive`
- `Very small reverse leakage`
- `Blocks most current`

Do not label reverse current as exactly zero.

Do not show breakdown as the normal reverse-bias state. Breakdown is discussed in the accompanying text and is outside the primary purpose of this figure.

### Required relationships and technical checks

The figure must satisfy all of the following:

- P-type side corresponds to the **anode**.
- N-type side corresponds to the **cathode**.
- The diode symbol cathode bar corresponds to the cathode.
- Forward bias is **anode positive relative to cathode**.
- Reverse bias is **anode negative relative to cathode**.
- Forward conventional current flows **anode → cathode** through the diode.
- Reverse leakage, if shown, is **small** and its conventional-current direction through the diode is **cathode → anode**.
- Forward current must visually dominate reverse leakage.
- The external circuit must be closed in both bias examples.
- A current-limiting resistor or generic load should be included in the forward-bias example so the diagram does not imply an ideal voltage source directly shorted through an ideal diode.
- No fixed universal forward-voltage number should appear.
- Reverse current must not be shown as exactly zero.
- The figure should not imply that the diode is a perfect mechanical one-way valve; the emphasis is electrical current behavior.

### Prohibited or misleading elements

- Do not reverse the anode and cathode.
- Do not reverse the diode-symbol cathode bar.
- Do not show forward current flowing cathode → anode.
- Do not show strong reverse current under normal reverse bias.
- Do not show reverse current as exactly zero.
- Do not state that every silicon diode has a fixed 0.7 V turn-on voltage.
- Do not show an ideal voltage source directly across a conducting ideal diode without a current-limiting element.
- Do not imply that the diode operates as a perfect switch.
- Do not repeat detailed donor/acceptor-ion physics from Figure 1.3.5 unless needed as a subtle reference.
- Do not introduce LEDs, Zener diodes, photodiodes, rectifier bridges, or other specialized diode types in this figure.
- Do not include energy-band diagrams, diode equations, or I-V equations.

### Final caption

*Figure 1.3.6 – A semiconductor diode is a two-terminal device based on a PN junction. When the anode is at a higher potential than the cathode, the diode is forward biased and conducts strongly. When the polarity is reversed, the diode is reverse biased and normally allows only a very small leakage current.*
