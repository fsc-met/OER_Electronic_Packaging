## Suggested Content for Figure 1.3.2

**Instructional purpose:**  
Help students distinguish conductors, insulators, and semiconductors without introducing semiconductor energy-band theory. The figure should emphasize the availability and controllability of mobile charge carriers rather than treating a semiconductor merely as a material with "medium resistance."

**Filename:** `1.3.2 - Conductor, Insulator, and Semiconductor.png`  
**Intended width:** 850 px in Markdown  
**Aspect ratio:** 16:9

### Objects/components to show

Create three side-by-side conceptual material panels:

1. **Conductor**
   - many mobile charge carriers;
   - easy current path under an applied voltage.
2. **Insulator**
   - very few mobile charge carriers under normal conditions;
   - strongly restricted current.
3. **Semiconductor**
   - a controllable population of mobile carriers;
   - current behavior that can be deliberately modified by doping and device structure.

Use a simple particle/carrier representation rather than an energy-band diagram.

### Required labels

- Conductor
- Insulator
- Semiconductor
- Many mobile carriers
- Very few mobile carriers
- Carrier population can be controlled
- Applied voltage

### Required relationships / arrows / states

- Apply a comparable voltage direction across all three conceptual panels.
- Show strong carrier motion/current in the conductor panel.
- Show essentially suppressed carrier motion in the insulator panel.
- Show a controlled/intermediate carrier-motion representation in the semiconductor panel.
- Make clear that the semiconductor panel represents controllability, not a fixed halfway value.

### Spatial relationships

- Use three equal-width panels.
- Keep carrier symbols and arrows large enough to read on a laptop screen.
- Use the same basic material-block geometry across the three panels so the comparison is visually fair.

### Color restrictions

- White/light neutral background.
- Restrained technical palette.
- Use consistent colors for electrons/carriers across panels.
- Do not use red-yellow-green "good/bad" coding.

### Suggested visual style

- Simple conceptual engineering diagram.
- No atomically realistic lattice is needed here; the lattice appears in Figure 1.3.3.
- No embedded overall title.
- No decorative icons.

### Prohibited or misleading elements

- Do not imply that every semiconductor always has conductivity numerically halfway between a conductor and an insulator.
- Do not show positive metal ions moving through the conductor.
- Do not introduce energy bands, Fermi levels, or carrier-concentration equations.
- Do not imply an ideal insulator has literally zero charge carriers under every condition.

### Technical verification sources

- MET406 Chapter 1 lecture notes, used for curriculum intent.
- OpenStax, *University Physics Volume 3*, semiconductor and doping sections.
- Rao R. Tummala, *Fundamentals of Microsystems Packaging*, introductory microelectronic-device discussion.

### Copyright/originality note

Use an original three-panel conceptual comparison. Do not reproduce the distinctive layout or artwork of a textbook carrier diagram.

### Final caption

*Figure 1.3.2 – Conductors provide many mobile carriers, insulators strongly restrict carrier motion under normal conditions, and semiconductors are useful because their carrier population and current behavior can be deliberately controlled.*

### Alt-text intent

Describe three conceptual panels comparing a conductor with many mobile carriers, an insulator with very few mobile carriers, and a semiconductor whose carrier population can be deliberately controlled.
