# MET406 OER — How to Use Reference Books and Online Resources

## Purpose

Reference books, standards, technical papers, manufacturer resources, and reputable online materials should be used to:

- verify technical accuracy;
- confirm terminology;
- understand accepted engineering practice;
- identify important topics and omissions;
- cross-check equations and physical interpretations;
- confirm typical material properties and process ranges;
- understand industry methods and standards;
- support further research during OER development.

They are **references for knowledge and verification**, not source material to copy into the OER.

---

## Fundamental Rule

> **All explanatory text, chapter organization, figures, examples, case studies, practice problems, review questions, and lab activities should be independently authored for the MET406 OER.**

Commercial books and online resources may inform our understanding, but the OER should not reproduce or closely paraphrase their presentation.

---

## How Reference Books Should Be Used

Reference books may be used to:

- verify that an explanation is technically correct;
- compare multiple explanations of a difficult concept;
- identify standard engineering terminology;
- check equations and assumptions;
- understand common industry practices;
- identify appropriate depth for a topic;
- find authoritative sources cited by the books;
- guide additional research.

Reference books should **not** be used to:

- copy paragraphs;
- lightly rewrite paragraphs;
- reproduce worked examples with only changed numbers;
- copy chapter organization too closely;
- reproduce tables without permission;
- redraw copyrighted figures in a nearly identical form;
- reuse end-of-chapter problems;
- reproduce proprietary diagrams, screenshots, or photographs.

---

## Independent Writing Requirement

When drafting a section:

1. Study several reliable sources when possible.
2. Understand the engineering concept independently.
3. Decide what an MET undergraduate actually needs to know.
4. Reorganize the concept around the applied goals of this OER.
5. Write the explanation from scratch.
6. Create original examples, figures, and problems.
7. Verify the finished explanation against authoritative references.

The OER should reflect **our own pedagogical structure**, not the structure of any single reference book.

---

## Use of Multiple Sources

For important or potentially variable technical claims, prefer verification from more than one independent source.

Examples include:

- typical PCB fabrication limits;
- solder-process parameter ranges;
- thermal properties;
- CTE values;
- reliability equations;
- accelerated-test models;
- vibration-response relationships;
- standards-based requirements.

When sources disagree:

- do not silently choose one;
- determine whether the values depend on process, material, geometry, technology generation, or test conditions;
- use ranges when appropriate;
- clearly state assumptions;
- prefer current standards, manufacturer data, or authoritative technical sources for numerical requirements.

---

## Figures and Images

### Preferred Sources

Figures used in the OER should preferably be:

1. newly created for the OER;
2. generated from our own diagrams or models;
3. created from our own CAD or simulation work;
4. based on openly licensed material when appropriate;
5. public-domain material when appropriate.

### Avoid

Do not:

- copy textbook figures;
- trace copyrighted figures closely;
- reuse commercial website images without appropriate rights;
- reproduce diagrams merely by changing colors, labels, or layout;
- use screenshots when an original schematic could explain the concept better.

### Recommended Approach

For concepts such as:

- PCB stack-ups;
- vias;
- solder joints;
- DFM errors;
- heat-flow paths;
- thermal resistance networks;
- transmission lines;
- return-current paths;
- crosstalk;
- vibration systems;
- reliability curves;

create clean, original OER diagrams with a consistent visual style.

---

## Tables and Engineering Data

Tables should be independently constructed.

When a table contains standard engineering data:

- verify the values using authoritative sources;
- record the source used for verification;
- use approximate ranges where appropriate;
- state important conditions or assumptions;
- avoid copying the layout and wording of an existing table.

Examples:

- thermal conductivity;
- elastic modulus;
- CTE;
- typical PCB copper thickness;
- process-temperature ranges;
- stencil thickness;
- vibration-test parameters;
- reliability-model parameters.

If data are strongly manufacturer- or process-dependent, the text should say so.

---

## Equations

Standard engineering equations may be used because the equations themselves are not the proprietary expression of a particular textbook.

However:

- notation should be chosen consistently for this OER;
- explanations should be written independently;
- derivations should be our own;
- worked examples should be newly created;
- assumptions and limitations should be explained in the context of electronic packaging.

When an equation is strongly associated with a named model or researcher, use the standard name when appropriate.

Examples:

- Arrhenius model
- Coffin-Manson relation
- Basquin relation
- Goodman correction
- Miner’s rule
- Weibull distribution

---

## Worked Examples

All worked examples should be created specifically for this OER.

A worked example should:

- use a realistic packaging situation;
- use original geometry, data, and wording;
- demonstrate an engineering decision;
- clearly state assumptions;
- show calculations at an appropriate MET level;
- explain what the numerical result means physically.

Do not take an example from a reference and simply change the numbers.

A good OER example should answer:

> **What should the engineer conclude or change based on this result?**

---

## Practice Problems and Review Questions

All problems and questions should be original.

They may assess:

- concept understanding;
- engineering judgment;
- simple calculations;
- troubleshooting;
- comparison of design alternatives;
- interpretation of figures;
- interpretation of test data;
- design improvement;
- root-cause reasoning.

Avoid reproducing or closely imitating textbook end-of-chapter questions.

---

## Case Studies

Industry case studies may use publicly available factual information, but the narrative should be independently written.

When using real products or failures:

- verify facts from reliable sources;
- avoid repeating another author's interpretation without verification;
- distinguish confirmed causes from speculation;
- cite factual claims where appropriate;
- create our own engineering analysis and teaching questions.

---

## Online Resources

Useful online sources may include:

- official standards organizations;
- government agencies;
- manufacturer application notes;
- PCB fabrication houses;
- electronics manufacturing equipment vendors;
- semiconductor manufacturers;
- professional societies;
- university resources;
- peer-reviewed papers;
- reputable technical publications.

Online information should be treated carefully because technical quality varies widely.

### Prefer

- official documentation;
- current standards;
- manufacturer datasheets;
- professional society publications;
- university or government sources;
- established technical publishers.

### Use Caution With

- blogs;
- forums;
- marketing pages;
- unsourced tutorial websites;
- AI-generated pages;
- hobbyist content.

These may be useful for finding terminology or practical examples, but important technical claims should be verified elsewhere.

---

## Standards

Standards are especially important for an applied industry-oriented OER.

Potential sources include:

- IPC
- JEDEC
- IEEE
- ASTM
- ASME
- MIL-STD
- UL
- ISO

When discussing a standard:

- identify the standard correctly;
- avoid reproducing copyrighted standards text;
- summarize requirements in our own words;
- cite the standard;
- avoid presenting an outdated requirement as current;
- distinguish a general engineering guideline from a formal standard requirement.

---

## Citation Philosophy

The OER should cite sources when they provide:

- specific technical data;
- standards requirements;
- historical facts;
- published models;
- industry case-study facts;
- non-obvious quantitative claims.

Routine engineering explanations do not need excessive citation when they are independently written and based on well-established knowledge.

The reference list should help students locate authoritative sources for deeper study.

## Reference Hierarchy for the MET406 OER

### Core references

1. **Rao R. Tummala — _Fundamentals of Device and Systems Packaging: Technologies and Applications_**
   - Broad technical reference for electronic packaging.

2. **Ali Jamnia — _Practical Guide for the Reliable Packaging of Electronics: Thermal and Mechanical Design and Analysis_**
   - Especially useful for the applied thermal and mechanical approach.

3. **Coombs & Holden — _Printed Circuits Handbook_**
   - PCB fabrication, manufacturing, assembly, process control, and reliability.

4. **Eric Bogatin — _Signal and Power Integrity—Simplified_**
   - Electrical interconnects, transmission-line behavior, SI, and PI.

5. **Dave S. Steinberg — _Vibration Analysis for Electronic Equipment_**
   - PCB vibration, shock, and mechanical design.

6. **O'Connor & Kleyner — _Practical Reliability Engineering_**
   - Reliability concepts, statistics, testing, and practical engineering reliability.

### Supporting references

Additional references may be added for:

- soldering and SMT;
- electronics cooling;
- EMI/EMC;
- failure analysis;
- semiconductor/package technology;
- standards;
- reliability testing;
- CAD/FEA/simulation methods.

The reference list should evolve as the OER develops.

---

## Final Project Rule

> **Reference materials are used to improve accuracy and completeness. They are not templates to reproduce.**

The MET406 OER must remain an independently authored work with:

- original prose;
- original pedagogical organization;
- original figures;
- original examples;
- original exercises;
- original labs;
- properly cited technical facts and standards.

This protects the open nature of the book while ensuring that it is technically rigorous and useful to engineering technology students.
