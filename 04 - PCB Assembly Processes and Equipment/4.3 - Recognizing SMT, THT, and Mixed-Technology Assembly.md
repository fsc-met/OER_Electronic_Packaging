# 4.3 - Recognizing SMT, THT, and Mixed-Technology Assembly

Section 4.2 followed one board through a representative PCB assembly factory. Before looking closely at soldering, printing, placement, and reflow, it is useful to recognize **what kind of board assembly you are looking at**.

At an introductory level, two major component-mounting approaches are important:

- **surface-mount technology (SMT)**;
- **through-hole technology (THT)**.

Many PCBAs use both. Some boards also use components on both sides, and some products use non-soldered attachment methods such as press-fit connections.

The first question is therefore not:

> **Which technology is better?**

A more useful question is:

> **How is each component physically connected to the PCB, and what does that mean for the assembly process?**

---

## 4.3.1 - Start with the Physical Connection

The easiest way to distinguish SMT and THT is to look at where the component terminals connect to the board.

### Surface-mount technology

In **SMT**, the component terminals connect to **lands/pads on the PCB surface**. The component leads or terminations do not need to pass through the board for that connection.

A common SMT sequence is:

> **solder paste -> component placement -> reflow soldering**

### Through-hole technology

In **THT**, component leads or pins pass through **plated through-holes** in the PCB.

A common THT route is:

> **component insertion -> soldering**

The soldering step may use wave, selective, or hand soldering. Some suitable through-hole parts can also be processed by other qualified methods such as intrusive reflow/pin-in-paste, but that detail belongs later in the chapter.

> **Practical rule:** A PCB can contain many drilled holes and vias and still be an SMT-only assembly. The classification depends on how the **components are mounted**, not simply on whether holes exist in the board.

---

## 4.3.2 - Recognizing Surface-Mount Technology (SMT)

Typical surface-mount components include:

- chip resistors and capacitors;
- small diodes and transistors;
- SOIC and QFP packages;
- QFN/DFN packages;
- BGA and many other area-array packages.

What you would notice on the board:

- the component body sits on or very near the PCB surface;
- the component terminations connect to surface lands;
- many components can be placed close together;
- small passive components and IC packages can often be placed automatically at high speed.

For a conventional solder-paste/reflow process:

1. solder paste is deposited onto selected pads;
2. components are placed onto the paste deposits;
3. the board is heated in reflow;
4. the solder melts and wets the mating surfaces;
5. the solder solidifies during cooling.

The detailed soldering physics begins in Section 4.4. Printing, placement, and reflow equipment are developed later in the chapter.

SMT is widely used because it supports compact layouts and highly automated assembly. That does **not** mean every product should use only SMT. Component function, package availability, mechanical loading, current path, thermal behavior, service needs, production volume, and manufacturing capability can all affect the choice.

---

## 4.3.3 - Recognizing Through-Hole Technology (THT)

In THT, the component lead or pin passes through a plated hole in the PCB.

Common examples include:

- pin headers and connectors;
- some transformers and inductors;
- axial or radial leaded components;
- switches, relays, and other electromechanical parts;
- selected power components.

What you would notice on the board:

- the component lead or pin passes through the PCB thickness;
- a plated hole surrounds the lead or pin;
- solder is commonly visible around the lead/pin on the solder side;
- connectors and other larger parts are common THT examples, although small THT parts also exist.

Common soldering methods include:

- **wave soldering**, especially when many suitable THT joints can be soldered together;
- **selective soldering**, when only selected THT locations should contact molten solder;
- **hand soldering**, for low-volume, special-access, prototype, repair, or other controlled manual work.

THT is often useful when a product benefits from through-board lead/pin geometry, connector retention, larger leads or terminals, or component forms that do not fit a normal SMT process.

Avoid turning that into a universal rule such as:

> **THT is always mechanically stronger or always carries more current than SMT.**

Mechanical strength and current capability depend on the complete connection design, including the component, lead/termination geometry, copper, plated-hole structure, solder joint, board support, and applied loading.

---

## 4.3.4 - Mixed-Technology Assembly Uses Both SMT and THT

A **mixed-technology PCBA** contains both surface-mount and through-hole components.

A simple example is an industrial controller that uses:

- small SMT resistors, capacitors, and ICs for most of the circuit;
- one or more through-hole connectors or large components where that construction is useful.

The board might therefore follow a sequence such as:

> **SMT printing/placement/reflow -> THT insertion -> THT soldering**

That sequence is only an example. The actual order depends on the product, board sides, component types, available equipment, thermal limits, and tooling.

Detailed mixed-process planning belongs in Section 4.20.

## 4.3.5 - Double-Sided SMT Is Not the Same as Mixed Technology

A common beginner mistake is to assume that any board with components on both sides is a mixed SMT/THT assembly.

That is not correct.

A board can have:

- SMT components on the **top side**;
- SMT components on the **bottom side**;
- no through-hole components at all.

That is a **double-sided SMT assembly**, not a mixed SMT/THT assembly.

A mixed-technology board is defined by the use of **different mounting/attachment approaches**, such as SMT plus soldered through-hole components.

This distinction matters because the manufacturing route can be different. Double-sided SMT commonly requires more than one placement/reflow sequence, while mixed SMT/THT assembly introduces an insertion and THT-joining step somewhere in the production route.

The detailed sequence decisions are intentionally deferred to Section 4.20.

---

## 4.3.6 - Not Every Pin Through a PCB Is a Soldered THT Joint

Some connectors and pins use **press-fit (compliant-pin)** technology.

In a press-fit connection:

- a specially designed pin is pressed into a compatible plated through-hole;
- the pin and hole create the required electrical and mechanical contact;
- solder is not required for that connection.

The geometry may look similar to a through-hole component because the pin enters a plated hole, but the attachment process is different.

In this book, press-fit is treated as a **separate solderless board-level attachment method**, not as a THT soldering process.

This is a useful reminder that recognizing a manufacturing process requires more than seeing a hole in the PCB.

---

## 4.3.7 - A Quick Factory-Floor Recognition Check

When you first look at an unfamiliar PCBA, ask the following questions.

| What you observe | Likely classification |
|---|---|
| small components attached only to surface pads | **SMT** |
| component leads pass through plated holes and are soldered | **THT** |
| surface-mounted components plus soldered through-hole connectors/components | **mixed SMT/THT** |
| surface-mount components on both sides, with no THT component leads | **double-sided SMT** |
| compliant pins pressed into plated holes without solder | **press-fit / non-soldered attachment** |

Then ask one more question:

> **What process would be needed to create the connection I am looking at?**

That question begins to connect the physical board to the factory processes introduced in Section 4.2.

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- **SMT** connects component terminations to lands on the PCB surface;
- **THT** uses component leads or pins that pass through plated holes;
- a **mixed-technology PCBA** uses both SMT and THT;
- **double-sided SMT** means SMT components are assembled on both sides and is not automatically a mixed SMT/THT board;
- press-fit connections can use plated holes without using solder;
- SMT and THT each have useful applications, and neither should be treated as universally superior;
- the physical connection geometry helps determine which manufacturing processes the board will require.

Section 4.4 next looks at the basic physics shared by many soldered assembly processes: **soldering, flux, wetting, heat input, and solderability**.
