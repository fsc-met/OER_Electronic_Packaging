# 4.6 - Solder Paste - What It Is and Why It Matters

Section 4.5 focused on protecting components before they enter the SMT process. The next material a bare PCB encounters in a typical SMT flow is **solder paste**.

Solder paste has to do several jobs before a solder joint exists. It must:

- be deposited in controlled locations and volumes;
- remain where it was printed;
- temporarily hold placed components;
- provide fluxing action during heating;
- provide the solder alloy that will melt and form the final joint.

A useful first model is:

> **solder alloy powder + flux vehicle -> printable solder paste -> component placement -> reflow -> solder joint**

Solder paste therefore behaves both like a **manufacturing process material** before reflow and like the source of the **metallic joint material** after reflow.

---

## 4.6.1 - Solder Paste Is Metal Powder Dispersed in a Flux Vehicle

Solder paste is not simply liquid solder.

It is a mixture of two major parts:

1. **solder alloy powder** - many microscopic metal particles that later melt and become part of the solder joint;
2. **flux vehicle** - the chemical and rheological medium that carries the powder and controls how the paste behaves before and during reflow.

For many conventional SMT stencil-printing pastes, the metal loading is in the **high-80s to about 90% by mass**. That is a useful scale, not a universal formulation rule: exact loading depends on the paste chemistry, powder size, alloy, and intended application.

The remaining fraction is the flux vehicle. It can contain materials such as:

- activators that help remove surface oxides during heating;
- solvents that help control processing behavior;
- resins or other flux solids;
- binders, gels, and rheology modifiers that control flow, shape retention, and tack.

Because solder alloy is much denser than the organic flux vehicle, a high metal **mass percentage** does not mean the paste visually appears to be almost all solid metal.

## 4.6.2 - Solder Paste Is Different from Wire Solder and Bar Solder

The word **solder** describes the joining alloy, but the physical form of the solder changes how it enters the manufacturing process.

| Solder form | Physical form before use | Typical manufacturing role | How flux is supplied |
|---|---|---|---|
| **solder paste** | metal powder suspended in a flux vehicle | printed or dispensed before component placement and reflow | flux is already part of the paste |
| **flux-cored solder wire** | solid wire | commonly fed to a heated joint during hand or automated point soldering | flux is commonly contained inside the wire core |
| **bar solder** | solid bulk alloy | melted in a solder pot for processes such as wave or selective soldering | flux is normally applied separately before solder contact |

The main distinction for SMT is timing.

With solder paste:

- the joining material is deposited **before** the component is placed;
- the paste remains on the PCB while the board travels through placement;
- the alloy does not become a molten solder joint until the later reflow step.

This is why paste properties that matter at room temperature - such as flow behavior and tack - can strongly affect a joint that will not form until several process steps later.

---

## 4.6.3 - Rheology Controls How the Paste Flows and Holds Its Shape

**Rheology** describes how a material flows and deforms when forces are applied.

Solder paste is a **non-Newtonian** material, which means its apparent viscosity depends on the way the material is being deformed.

Two related ideas are useful:

- **shear thinning** - the apparent viscosity decreases as the applied shear increases, so the paste becomes easier to move during printing;
- **thixotropy** - the material response also depends on shear history and time; structure breaks down while the paste is being worked and rebuilds during rest, helping the printed deposit recover enough body to hold its shape.

Shear thinning and thixotropy are related but not identical. For an entry-level process view, the important behavior is that the paste can flow under printing shear and then recover after deposition.

That behavior is useful because the paste must satisfy two opposite-looking requirements:

> **flow when the printer needs it to flow, then stop flowing after it has been deposited.**

If the paste is too resistant to flow under the actual process conditions, possible symptoms include:

- incomplete filling of a small opening;
- poor release from the stencil;
- inconsistent deposit volume;
- paste sticking to the squeegee or stencil.

If the paste flows too easily or does not recover adequately after printing, possible symptoms include:

- slumping or spreading;
- loss of deposit definition;
- reduced spacing between neighboring deposits;
- increased bridging risk later in the process.

A single viscosity number should not be treated as a universal solder-paste requirement. Reported viscosity depends on the paste formulation, temperature, test equipment, spindle/geometry, shear condition, and test method. A meaningful comparison therefore uses the **supplier's specified test method and product limits**.

## 4.6.4 - Tack Temporarily Holds Components Before Reflow

**Tack** is the ability of the printed paste to temporarily hold a component after placement and before the solder melts.

When a component is placed:

- its terminations contact the paste deposits;
- the deposits deform under placement force;
- the paste helps keep the component in position while the board is transported toward reflow.

Tack is **not** the final mechanical strength of the solder joint. It is a temporary process property.

If paste loses tack because of age, drying, contamination, excessive exposure, or unsuitable environmental conditions, a component may be more likely to:

- shift during board movement;
- lose alignment before reflow;
- move when the board accelerates, stops, or changes direction;
- respond poorly to the later wetting forces during reflow.

The correct tack requirement depends on the paste, component, placement process, board handling, and time between printing and reflow.

> **Practical rule:** If the process depends on the paste holding a component before reflow, the paste must still be inside its qualified use window when placement occurs.

---

## 4.6.5 - Powder Type Describes the Solder-Particle Size Range

Solder paste does not contain one single particle diameter. It contains a controlled **particle-size distribution**.

Industry material specifications classify commonly used solder powders by **type**. Three useful ranges for building physical scale are:

| Powder type | Representative particle-size range | Practical meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| **Type 3** | **25-45 µm** | coarser common SMT powder |
| **Type 4** | **20-38 µm** | finer powder widely used for modern SMT printing |
| **Type 5** | **15-25 µm** | finer powder used when smaller deposits/features require it |

The type number does **not** describe the solder alloy. For example, SAC305 paste can be supplied with different powder-size types. The table gives the main size bands used for recognition; actual material classification is based on a particle-size distribution rather than every individual particle having exactly the same diameter.

Smaller particles can help when the process must create smaller paste deposits, but smaller is not automatically better.

As particle diameter decreases:

- more particles are needed for the same mass of solder;
- the total surface area of the powder increases;
- more metal surface is exposed to oxidation;
- paste formulation and process control become more demanding;
- cost can increase.

## 4.6.6 - Solder Paste Is a Time- and Temperature-Sensitive Process Material

Solder paste properties can change during storage and use.

Important influences include:

- temperature;
- time since manufacture;
- time outside controlled storage;
- time on the stencil or in an open container;
- solvent loss;
- humidity and other environmental exposure;
- repeated shear history;
- contamination;
- mixing of new and previously used paste.

Many solder pastes are stored under refrigeration. Current supplier examples commonly use refrigerated ranges near **0-10 °C** or **0-12 °C**. These values are useful for recognizing the scale of the control, but they are **not a universal solder-paste storage rule**. Some current formulations explicitly permit extended room-temperature storage, while others do not. Shelf life can also differ by product, powder type, package form, and storage condition.

The governing information is therefore the **product label, technical data sheet (TDS), and local controlled procedure**.

A second important handling principle is that refrigerated paste is commonly allowed to reach the intended use temperature **while the package is still sealed** before opening.

Why?

Opening a cold container in warmer, humid air can allow condensation to form on or near the material. Forced heating can also disturb the material or create nonuniform temperature.

> **Practical rule:** Do not open cold paste just because production is waiting. Follow the product-specific conditioning instruction.

The required equilibration time is product- and package-dependent. Current supplier examples range from about an hour for some small syringes to several hours for larger jars or cartridges, so one fixed "2-hour" or "4-hour" rule should not be memorized as universal.

## 4.6.7 - Shelf Life, Stencil Life, and Open/Tack Time Are Different Clocks

Several time limits may appear on solder-paste documentation.

They do not mean the same thing.

| Term | Basic meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **shelf life** | allowable storage period for the product under specified conditions | aged material may no longer meet the supplier's intended properties |
| **stencil life** | period the paste can remain in the printing process/on the stencil while maintaining required performance under stated conditions | prolonged use can change print behavior through exposure, drying, contamination, and repeated shear |
| **open/working time (supplier-defined)** | an allowable use interval associated with opening, exposure, or a particular process state | suppliers do not always use this term in exactly the same way, so the TDS definition controls |
| **tack time / tack life** | period over which printed paste maintains the required component-holding behavior | excessive delay between printing, placement, and reflow can reduce process margin |

These values are often measured in **months** for sealed shelf life and **hours** for stencil or tack life, but the actual number is product-specific. Do not assume that the phrase **open time** has one identical definition across all suppliers.

For example, current product data sheets can specify an **8-hour or longer stencil life** for one paste while other materials use different limits. That does not create an 8-hour rule for all solder pastes.

> **Practical rule:** Treat every time limit as a material specification tied to stated conditions, not as a universal electronics-manufacturing constant.

## 4.6.8 - Separation, Drying, and Contamination Can Change Paste Behavior

A solder paste that looks different from its normal controlled condition deserves attention before it is placed into production.

Possible warning signs include:

- visible flux separation beyond what the supplier's preparation instructions allow;
- a dry surface skin or crust;
- paste that no longer rolls or flows normally in the printer;
- unexpected thickening or thinning;
- foreign material or debris in the paste;
- material from the stencil being returned to a container of unused paste without an approved procedure;
- an expired or missing lot/date label;
- unknown time-temperature history.

Not every visible change proves the paste is unusable. Some products in jars are intended to be gently re-homogenized before use according to supplier instructions, while some cartridges or syringes should not be mixed in the same way.

The correct response is therefore evidence-based:

> **Observe the material -> check the label/TDS and exposure history -> compare with the controlled process -> hold or replace the material if status cannot be verified.**

Solder paste is also a chemical process material. Follow the product safety data sheet (SDS) and the site's requirements for handling, personal protective equipment, hygiene, and waste disposal.

Avoid adding used paste back into a container of fresh unused paste unless a qualified site procedure explicitly allows it. Used material can carry altered chemistry, contamination, and a different shear/exposure history.

## 4.6.9 - During Reflow, the Paste Changes from Process Material to Solder Joint

Before reflow, solder paste is a suspension of solid metal particles in a flux vehicle.

During reflow, several things occur in a controlled thermal sequence:

1. the paste and assembly heat up;
2. solvents and other volatile constituents are driven off or transformed;
3. flux chemistry becomes active and supports oxide removal/wetting;
4. the solder-alloy particles reach the molten state and coalesce;
5. molten solder wets the PCB land and component termination;
6. the joint solidifies during cooling;
7. flux residue may remain or may require cleaning, depending on the flux chemistry and product process.

This sequence connects directly to Section 4.4:

> **The paste delivers the solder alloy and flux to the joint site; reflow provides the thermal cycle that allows wetting and metallurgical joining to occur.**

Solder paste therefore cannot be evaluated only by asking whether it prints well. A useful material must support the complete sequence from deposition through placement and reflow.

The detailed reflow profile is developed later in Sections 4.14 and 4.15.

## 4.6.10 - What Would You Check Next?

When solder-paste behavior appears abnormal, start by checking the material status before immediately changing machine settings.

| Observation | Possible concern | Reasonable next check |
|---|---|---|
| paste is still cold when production is ready to start | material may not have completed the required conditioning/equilibration step | verify container temperature/conditioning time against the product TDS before opening or use |
| paste appears unusually thick or does not roll normally | temperature, age, shear history, drying, or material condition may have changed | check material temperature, lot/expiration, time out of storage, and supplier handling instructions |
| paste spreads after printing | rheology may be unsuitable for the actual conditions or material may be too warm/aged/altered | verify paste identity and condition before adjusting print settings |
| components do not remain stable after placement | tack may be reduced or the print/placement condition may be abnormal | verify paste age/exposure, deposit condition, placement setup, and delay to reflow |
| visible liquid separation, crusting, or foreign debris is present | material homogeneity or cleanliness may be questionable | hold the paste and compare with the supplier's allowed preparation/acceptance guidance |
| paste lot or expiration status cannot be verified | material traceability is incomplete | hold material and restore traceability before production use |
| process requires very small paste deposits | current powder type may be too coarse for the intended printing geometry | verify the selected powder type and then evaluate stencil/aperture requirements in Section 4.7 |

The troubleshooting habit is the same one used earlier in the chapter:

> **material condition first -> process evidence next -> adjustment only after a likely cause is identified.**

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- solder paste combines **solder-alloy powder** with a **flux vehicle**;
- the powder becomes the metallic solder joint, while the flux vehicle provides chemistry, flow control, and temporary tack;
- solder paste is different from solder wire and bar solder because it is deposited before component placement and reflow;
- useful paste rheology allows the material to flow under shear and then recover enough structure to retain the printed deposit;
- **tack** temporarily holds components before reflow and is not the final joint strength;
- powder type describes a particle-size distribution; representative Type 3, Type 4, and Type 5 ranges are **25-45 µm**, **20-38 µm**, and **15-25 µm**;
- finer powder can support smaller deposits but brings additional oxidation and process-control demands;
- storage, conditioning, shelf life, stencil life, and tack/open time are **product-specific controls** rather than universal constants;
- visible separation, drying, contamination, or unknown time-temperature history should trigger verification before use;
- during reflow, the paste changes from a printable process material into the fluxed molten solder that wets the joint surfaces and then solidifies.

Section 4.7 next develops the physical tool that controls where this material is deposited and approximately how much is deposited: the **stencil and paste-printing process**.
