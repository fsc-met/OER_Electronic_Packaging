# 4.5 - ESD Control and Moisture-Sensitive Device Handling Basics

Section 4.4 explained what must happen physically for solder to wet a surface and form a metallurgical joint. Before a component ever reaches placement or reflow, however, poor handling can damage the part or make it unsafe to process.

Two important handling risks are easy to overlook because they may leave no obvious mark:

- **electrostatic discharge (ESD)** can electrically damage a sensitive device;
- **absorbed moisture** can damage some nonhermetic packages when the package is heated during reflow.

A useful manufacturing rule is:

> **Protect the component before the process. If ESD or moisture status is unknown, stop and verify before continuing.**

This section introduces the handling behaviors an entry-level engineering technologist should recognize before working through the detailed SMT process.

---

## 4.5.1 - Handling Is Part of the Assembly Process

A component can be correct by part number and still arrive at placement in an unusable or questionable condition.

Examples include:

- an ESD-sensitive device handled outside a protected area;
- a reel removed from its dry package with no record of when it was opened;
- a moisture-barrier bag that is torn or has an uncertain seal;
- a wrist-strap monitor that alarms while exposed devices are being handled;
- a humidity indicator showing an unacceptable condition according to the applicable card and procedure.

None of these situations is fixed by simply loading the component into the placement machine.

For this section, the practical goal is:

> **Recognize when protected handling is required, preserve the known status of the material, and do not continue when that status cannot be verified.**

---

## 4.5.2 - ESD Can Damage a Device Without a Visible Spark

**Electrostatic discharge (ESD)** is a rapid transfer of electrostatic charge between objects at different electrical potentials.

Static charge can be generated when materials contact and separate, when people walk or move, when plastic materials are handled, or when an isolated conductive object becomes charged.

People can generate **several thousand volts** of electrostatic potential during ordinary movement. Electronic devices, however, can have ESD withstand levels in the **hundreds of volts or lower**, depending on the device and test model.

That leads to an important practical rule:

> **Not feeling a shock does not mean the device was safe from ESD.**

Possible ESD outcomes include:

- immediate catastrophic failure;
- altered electrical behavior;
- damage that is not detected immediately and later contributes to field failure.

The last case is especially difficult because the part may pass an initial functional test after the ESD event.

For an entry-level technician or engineer, the important question is not:

> "Did I see or feel a spark?"

The better question is:

> **"Was the ESDS item kept inside the required ESD controls while it was exposed?"**

---

## 4.5.3 - An ESD Protected Area Controls Potential Differences

An **ESD protected area (EPA)** is a defined area in which ESD-control materials, equipment, and procedures are used to reduce the risk of electrostatic damage to **ESD-sensitive (ESDS)** items.

The basic concept is **equipotential control**:

> **Keep personnel, conductive/dissipative work surfaces, tools, fixtures, and other controlled items at the same electrical potential by proper bonding and grounding.**

A typical workstation may include:

- an ESD-protective work surface;
- a common point ground;
- a wrist strap or another approved personnel-grounding method;
- ESD-safe tools and fixtures;
- approved ESD packaging or material-handling containers;
- an ionizer where required for process-essential insulators or isolated conductors;
- verification devices such as wrist-strap testers or continuous monitors.

An ionizer is useful when charge cannot be removed by grounding alone. It is **not** a substitute for grounding personnel and conductive items that can be grounded.

## 4.5.4 - Good ESD Handling Is a Behavior, Not Just a Wrist Strap

Wearing a wrist strap does not by itself create an effective ESD-control process.

Before handling exposed ESDS items, typical good practice is to:

1. enter the designated EPA;
2. verify the required personnel-grounding system according to the site's procedure;
3. place material only on the approved ESD-protective work surface or in approved containers;
4. keep unnecessary ordinary plastic, foam, tape, and other charge-generating materials away from exposed ESDS items;
5. use approved tools, fixtures, trays, and packaging;
6. handle boards and components only as required by the work instruction, avoiding unnecessary contact with terminals and solderable surfaces;
7. respond to a monitor alarm or failed grounding check by stopping exposed-device handling and correcting the condition.

Packaging requirements also depend on location.

Inside an EPA, material-handling containers are commonly selected to be **low charging** and conductive or dissipative as required by the ESD-control plan. When ESDS material leaves the EPA, the packaging may also need **discharge shielding**.

> **Practical rule:** An ordinary plastic bag or convenient piece of foam is not a substitute for approved ESD packaging.

---

## 4.5.5 - Moisture Sensitivity Is a Different Handling Problem

ESD and moisture sensitivity are different failure mechanisms.

Some nonhermetic surface-mount packages contain polymeric materials that absorb moisture from ambient air. During storage at ordinary room conditions, this moisture may diffuse into the package without any visible change.

The problem appears when the package is heated rapidly during assembly.

Many surface-mount reflow processes expose package bodies to temperatures **above 200 °C**. If too much moisture is trapped inside a package, heating can create vapor pressure and internal stress.

Possible damage includes:

- internal delamination;
- package cracking;
- separation at internal interfaces;
- wire-bond or die-related damage;
- severe package bulging or cracking sometimes called **popcorning**.

The part may look normal before reflow.

The purpose of moisture-sensitive-device handling is therefore not simply to keep the outside of the part dry. It is to control how much moisture the package absorbs **before high-temperature reflow exposure**.

---

## 4.5.6 - Moisture Sensitivity Level Sets the Basic Exposure Scale

The **moisture sensitivity level (MSL)** is a classification used to identify how much ambient exposure a moisture/reflow-sensitive surface-mount device can tolerate before reflow under specified conditions.

The allowable exposed time is commonly called **floor life**.

> **Floor life is controlled exposure time before reflow, not machine run time.**

Selected MSL examples are useful for building scale:

| Representative MSL | Representative floor-life classification | Reference condition |
|---|---:|---|
| **MSL 1** | unlimited | up to 30 °C / 85% RH |
| **MSL 3** | 168 hours | up to 30 °C / 60% RH |
| **MSL 4** | 72 hours | up to 30 °C / 60% RH |
| **MSL 5a** | 24 hours | up to 30 °C / 60% RH |
| **MSL 6** | use within the time stated on the label after the required drying/bake condition | per label / applicable procedure |

These are **classification anchors**, not permission to ignore the material label or local procedure.

For example, under the applicable MSL 3 floor conditions:

- 6 days of continuous exposure is $6 \times 24 = 144$ hours;
- 8 days is $8 \times 24 = 192$ hours.

A continuously exposed MSL 3 reel would therefore be below the 168-hour classification point at 144 hours but beyond it at 192 hours.

That simple arithmetic still does **not** prove the reel is acceptable to use. Prior exposure, dry-storage history, actual environmental conditions, the device label, and the controlled manufacturing procedure must also be known.

> **Practical rule:** Do not restart the floor-life clock just because a reel was placed back in an ordinary bag or cabinet.

---

## 4.5.7 - Learn to Recognize the Dry-Pack System

A moisture-sensitive shipment commonly uses a **moisture-barrier bag (MBB)** and associated controls.

Important items to recognize are:

| Item | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Moisture-barrier bag (MBB)** | slows moisture entry during controlled storage and shipment |
| **Desiccant** | absorbs moisture inside the sealed bag |
| **Humidity indicator card (HIC)** | indicates the humidity condition reached inside the package environment according to the card's marked response points |
| **Moisture-sensitivity caution label** | identifies handling information such as MSL and floor-life-related instructions |
| **Exposure record / traveler / electronic tracking** | preserves the history of when the material was opened, stored, and returned to controlled storage |

The **HIC is not a direct measurement of moisture inside every component**. It is evidence about humidity exposure inside the bag and must be interpreted according to the card, label, and applicable procedure.

Different HIC technologies can use different color systems. Do not rely on a memorized color rule without checking the actual card.

---

## 4.5.8 - Opening the Bag Starts a Controlled Handling Process

Once an MBB is opened, the material should enter a controlled handling sequence rather than becoming ordinary open stock.

A practical workflow is:

> **verify label/status -> open in the correct handling area -> record exposure -> use within allowed floor life or place in approved dry storage -> hold and verify if status becomes uncertain**

Possible controlled actions include:

- completing placement and reflow within the allowed floor life;
- placing material into qualified dry storage when permitted;
- correctly resealing/repacking material according to the approved procedure;
- following a specified drying or bake process when required.

A **bake** is not a universal "put it in an oven" repair.

Bake conditions can depend on:

- package thickness and construction;
- MSL;
- how long the floor life was exceeded;
- the drying temperature and humidity;
- component/manufacturer limits;
- whether the reel, tray, tape, label, or other carrier can tolerate the bake condition.

> **Do not invent a bake temperature or time. Follow the applicable component information and controlled J-STD-033-based procedure.**

## 4.5.9 - ESD Protection and Moisture Control Must Work Together

A component can require both ESD protection and moisture control at the same time.

These controls solve different problems:

| Control question | ESD handling | Moisture-sensitive handling |
|---|---|---|
| **What is the hazard?** | damaging electrostatic discharge or field/charged-device event | absorbed moisture followed by high-temperature reflow exposure |
| **When is the part vulnerable?** | whenever an exposed ESDS item is handled or transported without required controls | after controlled dry packaging/storage is lost and ambient exposure accumulates before reflow |
| **Typical controls** | EPA, grounding, work surface, approved packaging, ionization where needed | MSL label, MBB, desiccant, HIC, exposure tracking, dry storage, approved bake/dry process |
| **Can damage be invisible?** | yes | yes |
| **What if status is unknown?** | stop exposed handling and restore/verify ESD controls | hold the material and verify exposure/dryness status before reflow |

An MBB does not eliminate the need for ESD-safe handling after it is opened. Likewise, an ESD-safe container does not automatically provide the moisture protection required for an MSL-controlled device.

---

## 4.5.10 - What Would You Check Next?

When handling status becomes uncertain, the first action should usually be to preserve the material and verify evidence rather than keep production moving.

| Situation | What it may mean | Reasonable next action |
|---|---|---|
| wrist-strap tester fails or continuous monitor alarms | personnel grounding may no longer be controlled | stop exposed ESDS handling; secure the material and correct/verify the grounding system |
| component reel is outside the EPA in ordinary packaging | ESD protection may be inadequate | place material on hold and verify the approved packaging/handling path before exposing the parts |
| MSL label is present but bag-open time is unknown | remaining floor life cannot be established confidently | hold the material and reconstruct the exposure record or follow the approved recovery procedure |
| MBB is torn, punctured, or seal integrity is questionable | dry-pack condition may have been compromised | inspect the HIC and material status according to procedure; do not assume the original floor-life status remains valid |
| HIC shows an unacceptable condition according to the card/procedure | bag humidity exposure may have exceeded the allowed condition | hold material and follow the controlled drying/bake/repack procedure |
| MSL 3 reel has been continuously exposed for 192 hours at qualified floor conditions | the 168-hour classification point has been exceeded | do not reflow based only on visual appearance; follow the approved recovery procedure |
| exposure history cannot be verified | status is unknown | **stop, identify, segregate, and escalate** rather than guessing |

The same troubleshooting habit used earlier in Chapter 4 applies:

> **Check the physical material, label, status record, and control evidence before changing or bypassing the process.**

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- ESD and moisture sensitivity are **handling risks that occur before or during assembly**, not just inspection problems after the board is built;
- an ESDS item can be damaged without a visible or felt spark;
- an EPA uses grounding, equipotential bonding, ESD-protective surfaces, approved packaging, and ionization where needed;
- a wrist strap is one part of an ESD-control system, not the entire system;
- some nonhermetic packages absorb moisture from ambient air and can be damaged when rapidly heated during reflow;
- MSL identifies the required moisture-handling level, and **floor life** is the allowed exposure period under specified conditions;
- MSL 3 provides a useful scale example of **168 hours at up to 30 °C / 60% RH**;
- an MBB, desiccant, HIC, caution label, and exposure record are different parts of the moisture-control system;
- baking/drying is a controlled procedure, not a universal temperature-and-time recipe;
- ESD protection and moisture control must both be maintained when a device requires both;
- if handling status is unknown, the correct first response is to **hold and verify**, not to continue and hope the part is acceptable.

Section 4.6 next introduces the process material that begins the SMT attachment flow: **solder paste**.

### Numerical and unit review

Public numerical content is intentionally limited to useful handling scale.

Retained representative MSL examples:

- MSL 1: unlimited floor life at up to 30 °C / 85% RH;
- MSL 3: 168 h at up to 30 °C / 60% RH;
- MSL 4: 72 h at up to 30 °C / 60% RH;
- MSL 5a: 24 h at up to 30 °C / 60% RH;
- MSL 6: bake/dry as required and reflow within time stated on the label.

The MSL 3 arithmetic example is verified:

- $6 \times 24 = 144$ h;
- $8 \times 24 = 192$ h;
- 144 h < 168 h;
- 192 h > 168 h.

These values are explicitly presented as **classification anchors**, not substitutes for the product label, exposure history, dry-storage history, current standard, or local procedure.

No universal bake temperature/time is provided.

No universal human ESD perception voltage is provided.

The public text uses only the verified scale statement that human movement can generate **several thousand volts** while ESDS parts may have withstand ratings in the **hundreds of volts or lower**.

The statement that bulk reflow can expose moisture-sensitive package bodies above 200 °C is consistent with J-STD-033D's physical rationale and the solder-temperature scale established in Section 4.4.
