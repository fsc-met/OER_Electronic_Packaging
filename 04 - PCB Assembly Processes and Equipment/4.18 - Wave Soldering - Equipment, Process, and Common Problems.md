# 4.18 - Wave Soldering: Equipment, Process, and Common Problems

Section 4.17 ended with through-hole components correctly inserted, oriented, seated, and retained. The next manufacturing problem is to solder many of those protruding leads consistently.

**Wave soldering** is a conveyor-based process in which the solder side of a PCB passes across a controlled wave of molten solder. The board is fluxed and preheated before wave contact, then leaves the wave so excess solder can drain and the joints can cool.

The basic process is:

> **flux -> preheat -> wave contact -> drain/separate -> cool**

Wave soldering is especially useful when many through-hole joints must be soldered in one pass. Some mixed-technology assemblies also use it when board layout, fixtures, and bottom-side components are compatible with wave exposure.

> **A good wave process is not just "hot solder touching the board." Flux, preheat, contact time, wave behavior, board angle, and drainage must work together.**

---

## 4.18.1 - Recognizing the Main Wave-Soldering Hardware

A wave-soldering machine moves the assembly through several physical modules.

| Hardware | What it does | What the operator should recognize |
|---|---|---|
| **Fluxer** | applies flux to the solder side and into through-hole gaps | coverage must be appropriate for the flux/process |
| **Preheater** | heats the board and activates/prepares the flux | board temperature depends on flux, board mass, and conveyor speed |
| **Conveyor** | carries and supports the board through the machine | speed and board angle affect heating, contact, and drainage |
| **Solder pot** | holds molten solder at controlled temperature | solder temperature, level, alloy condition, and dross matter |
| **Pump and wave nozzle** | pumps molten solder upward to form the standing wave | wave height and stability affect wetting and drainage |
| **Pallet / support fixture** | supports or masks selected board areas when needed | must not block intended solder access or allow excessive board sag |
| **Cooling / exit area** | allows solder joints to solidify after separation | assembly should leave the machine without disturbing liquid joints |

Some machines also use nitrogen around the wave to reduce oxidation and improve the process window. Nitrogen is optional; it does not replace correct fluxing, preheat, wave contact, or drainage.

## 4.18.2 - Step 1: Flux the Solder Side

Flux prepares the metal surfaces for soldering by helping remove oxide films and protecting the surfaces during heating.

In wave soldering, flux must reach the areas that the solder must later wet:

- bottom-side pads;
- protruding component leads;
- the lead-to-barrel gap at plated through-holes.

Spray fluxing is common in modern equipment because the deposited amount can be controlled. Foam fluxing may still be encountered on older or specialized equipment.

The correct flux amount is product- and chemistry-dependent. Too little can leave oxide or incomplete coverage. Too much can leave excessive residue or create its own process/reliability problems.

A useful rule is:

> **Do not set flux by appearance alone. Use the flux supplier's process guidance and verify that coverage reaches the joints that must solder.**

---

## 4.18.3 - Step 2: Preheat the Assembly

Preheat performs several jobs before the board reaches the solder wave:

- evaporates or drives off the flux solvent as intended;
- brings the flux into its useful activation range;
- reduces the temperature jump when the board reaches molten solder;
- adds heat to the PCB, leads, and PTH barrels so wetting and hole filling can occur quickly.

The required board temperature depends on flux chemistry, PCB thickness, copper distribution, pallet use, component mass, and conveyor speed.

One current wave-flux datasheet uses **85-120°C top-side preheat** for a 62-mil test board, while other fluxes/processes call for different temperatures and times. Treat **about 90-120°C** only as a representative teaching scale, not a universal setpoint.

The important manufacturing check is:

> **Measure the product temperature required by the approved process; do not assume a heater setpoint equals PCB temperature.**

Too little preheat can leave the assembly thermally cold or the flux insufficiently prepared. Too much preheat can consume flux activity too early or overheat temperature-sensitive components.

---

## 4.18.4 - Step 3: Contact the Solder Wave

Inside the solder pot, a pump forces molten solder through a nozzle and creates a standing wave.

As the solder side of the PCB contacts the wave:

1. molten solder contacts the bottom pads and protruding leads;
2. fluxed metal surfaces begin to wet;
3. heat moves into the lead, pad, and plated barrel;
4. molten solder is drawn into the lead-to-barrel gap;
5. the hole fills as long as the surfaces remain solderable and hot enough for the solder to stay liquid;
6. the board continues toward the exit side of the wave.

For lead-free SAC alloys, **about 255-265°C** is a common supplier/equipment starting range for wave-solder pot temperature. The actual approved setting depends on the alloy, flux, product, machine, component temperature limits, and required process window.

## 4.18.5 - Contact Time Depends on Contact Length and Conveyor Speed

The PCB does not remain in the wave indefinitely. The important quantity is the **effective contact time** or **dwell time**.

A useful first-order relationship is:

$$
t_c = \frac{L_c}{v}
$$

where:

- $t_c$ = effective solder-wave contact time;
- $L_c$ = effective contact length along the board-travel direction;
- $v$ = conveyor speed.

The units must be consistent.

### Example

Suppose the effective contact length is measured as:

$$
L_c = 50\ \text{mm}
$$

and the conveyor speed is:

$$
v = 1.0\ \text{m/min}
$$

Convert conveyor speed:

$$
1.0\ \text{m/min}
=
\frac{1000\ \text{mm}}{60\ \text{s}}
\approx 16.7\ \text{mm/s}
$$

Then:

$$
t_c
=
\frac{50\ \text{mm}}{16.7\ \text{mm/s}}
\approx 3.0\ \text{s}
$$

A contact time of roughly **2-4 s** is a useful introductory scale, not a universal requirement. Current supplier examples range from about **3-3.5 s** for one SAC305 wave process to **4-6 s** for another lead-free flux/alloy/test-board combination. The approved recipe must come from the actual product, flux, alloy, and equipment.

Do not change conveyor speed only because one number "looks wrong." Conveyor speed also changes preheat time and interacts with wave length, board angle, board thermal mass, and drainage.

---

## 4.18.6 - Step 4: Drain and Separate Cleanly

A wave-soldered joint is not finished at the instant the board reaches the crest of the wave. The board must also leave the liquid solder cleanly.

At the exit:

- the board continues upward along the conveyor angle;
- excess solder should flow back toward the wave;
- liquid bridges between adjacent pins should break;
- enough solder should remain on the lead, pad, and in the PTH;
- the joint then cools and solidifies.

Poor separation can leave:

- solder bridges;
- icicles or spikes;
- excess solder;
- solder retained between closely spaced leads.

Conveyor angle, conveyor speed, wave shape/height, lead geometry, board flatness, flux activity, oxidation, and board orientation can all affect drainage.

Many conveyor-wave systems use a modest upward transport angle. **About 4-8° is a representative equipment range, with 7° common on some systems**, but nozzle geometry, product, and machine design determine the approved setting.

---

## 4.18.7 - The Process Variables Are Coupled

The main process variables should not be adjusted independently without considering what else changes.

| Variable | If too low / insufficient | If too high / excessive | What to verify |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Flux application** | poor oxide removal; poor wetting; skips | residue, contamination, or process instability | supplier target, coverage, penetration |
| **Preheat** | cold joints/PTH structure; incomplete flux preparation | flux can be consumed too early; component thermal risk | measured board temperature, not heater setting alone |
| **Solder-pot temperature** | slower wetting; possible poor fill | more thermal stress, oxidation/dross, copper dissolution | alloy supplier recommendation and product limits |
| **Conveyor speed** | longer residence in preheat and wave regions; can increase solder retention | shorter residence; can reduce heating or hole fill | actual product temperature and measured contact time |
| **Wave height / pump condition** | insufficient board/joint contact; possible skips | excessive board engagement, flooding, or disturbed drainage | stable wave, solder level, and correct board engagement |
| **Conveyor angle / separation geometry** | poor drainage on some setups | may reduce useful contact or create another drainage condition | equipment/nozzle design and defect pattern |

For SAC305, **about 255-265°C pot temperature** and **about 1.0-1.5 m/min conveyor speed** are representative supplier/equipment starting scales. Effective contact time is product- and wave-geometry-dependent; supplier examples commonly fall in the several-second range. These are starting scales, not universal recipes.

The correct process window belongs to the actual combination of:

> **board + components + flux + solder alloy + fixture + machine**

---

## 4.18.8 - Pallets, Supports, and Board Flatness Matter

A PCB that sags or moves relative to the wave does not receive the same solder contact everywhere.

Pallets and support fixtures may be used to:

- support a large or thin PCB;
- reduce sag during heating;
- protect selected areas from solder exposure;
- hold the board at a repeatable position;
- provide a controlled opening around the joints that must reach the wave.

A poorly designed or poorly seated pallet can create its own problems by blocking flux, preheat, or solder access.

Another geometric problem is **shadowing**. A tall component, dense connector, pallet wall, or other obstruction can reduce or disturb wave access to a joint located behind it relative to board travel. Board orientation relative to the wave therefore matters.

When shadowing is suspected, inspect the defect pattern:

- Does the problem occur downstream of one tall part?
- Does rotating the board or changing the pallet opening change the result?
- Are only joints in one geometric region affected?

Do not compensate for a geometric access problem by changing temperature first.

## 4.18.9 - Common Wave-Soldering Problems

Wave-soldering defects should be treated as observations, not one-cause diagnoses.

| Observation | What it looks like | Likely mechanisms to investigate first |
|---|---|---|
| **Bridging** | unwanted solder connection between adjacent pins/pads | poor drainage, excessive contact/wave condition, board orientation, lead geometry, oxidation/flux condition |
| **Insufficient hole fill** | solder does not rise adequately through the PTH | poor solderability, insufficient flux/preheat, high thermal mass, short contact, lead-hole geometry |
| **Icicles / excess solder** | pointed solder projection or too much retained solder | poor separation, wave instability, speed/angle interaction, oxidation, lead geometry |
| **Poor wetting** | solder contacts the area but does not spread/bond normally to the intended metal surface | oxide/contamination, weak/dried flux, inadequate thermal condition, poor solderability |
| **Skip** | an intended joint or local area is missed or receives little useful solder contact | local flux coverage, low/unstable wave, board sag, obstruction/shadowing, contamination |
| **Shadowing** | repeated weak/unsoldered region behind an obstruction relative to wave travel | board orientation, tall components, pallet opening, support geometry, wave access |

A dull-looking lead-free solder surface by itself is not enough evidence to declare poor wetting. Look at the actual wetting geometry and the applicable inspection requirements.

## 4.18.10 - Troubleshoot the Pattern, Not Just One Joint

Consider a first production board with two different observations:

- several trailing pins on a connector are bridged;
- a nearby transformer has weak or incomplete hole filling.

A poor response is to change one global setting immediately.

For example:

- speeding up the conveyor may help some drainage conditions but can reduce wave contact and preheat;
- slowing the conveyor may help a cold, high-mass PTH but can worsen excess-solder retention;
- raising the pot temperature may improve wetting but also increases thermal exposure and oxidation.

A better troubleshooting sequence is:

1. **Confirm the observations and their locations.**
2. **Check the defect pattern relative to wave direction, tall parts, and pallet openings.**
3. **Verify flux coverage and measured preheat condition.**
4. **Verify wave height/stability, board support, and effective contact time.**
5. **Check solderability and PTH/lead geometry if the process evidence looks normal.**
6. **Change the smallest logical process variable.**
7. **Run another board and verify both the original defect and any side effects.**

This prevents a correction for one joint type from creating a new problem somewhere else.

---

## 4.18.11 - A Practical Wave-Solder First-Board Check

Before releasing the first board from a wave-solder setup, a technician or technologist should be able to confirm the following.

### Before the board reaches the wave

- correct PCB and component revision;
- THT components correctly inserted, oriented, seated, and retained;
- flux chemistry and fluxer setup match the approved process;
- preheat is operating and the product temperature is reasonable for the approved flux/product;
- conveyor width/support/pallet is set correctly;
- solder pot is at the approved alloy temperature and level;
- wave height and pump behavior are stable.

### After wave contact

- board exits the wave without obvious flooding or unstable contact;
- solder separates cleanly from the trailing edge and pin fields;
- no repeated bridge/icicle pattern is visible;
- representative high-thermal-mass PTHs do not show an obvious incomplete-fill pattern;
- no local shadowed region is being missed;
- the assembly cools without handling that disturbs still-liquid joints.

Formal workmanship acceptance—including the required degree of PTH fill—must follow the applicable product requirements and standards. This section is teaching **process recognition and first troubleshooting actions**, not certification of solder-joint acceptability.

---

## 4.18.12 - Section Takeaway

Wave soldering is a coupled thermal, chemical, fluid, and mechanical process.

The most important practical points are:

- recognize the fluxer, preheater, conveyor, solder pot, pump/wave, support fixture, and cooling/exit areas;
- remember the physical sequence: **flux -> preheat -> wave contact -> drain/separate -> cool**;
- understand that flux prepares the surfaces and preheat prepares both the flux and the assembly;
- connect conveyor speed and effective wave contact length to dwell time;
- recognize that pot temperature, wave height, board angle, and drainage interact;
- use pallets/supports when needed without blocking the intended solder path;
- recognize bridging, insufficient hole fill, icicles/excess solder, poor wetting, skips, and shadowing as observations that require evidence-based troubleshooting;
- change one logical process variable at a time and verify the result.

Wave soldering treats many joints together as one board moves through one shared process. **Section 4.19 moves to selective and hand soldering, where heat and solder can be applied more locally to particular joints or areas.**

### Equation and numerical review

The only equation is the first-order contact-time relationship:

$$
t_c = \frac{L_c}{v}
$$

Worked example:

- $L_c = 50$ mm;
- $v = 1.0$ m/min = 16.7 mm/s;
- $t_c \approx 3.0$ s.

Arithmetic checked:

$$
50 / 16.6667 \approx 3.0
$$

The example is explicitly illustrative because actual joint contact length varies with wave height, board angle, board bending, lead protrusion, nozzle geometry, and board layout.
