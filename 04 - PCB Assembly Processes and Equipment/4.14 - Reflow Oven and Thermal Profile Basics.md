# 4.14 - Reflow Oven and Thermal Profile Basics

Section 4.13 ended with components placed into solder paste. At that point, the parts are only **positioned** on the PCB. The solder paste is still a mixture of metal powder and flux, and the joints have not yet been formed.

The next step is **reflow soldering**:

> **controlled heating -> solder melts and wets -> metallurgical joint forms -> controlled cooling -> solder solidifies**

A reflow oven is therefore not simply a hot box. It is a controlled thermal machine that moves the PCB assembly through a planned temperature history. For a beginning technologist, the most important questions are:

- What hardware moves and heats the board?
- What does a reflow temperature-versus-time profile mean?
- Which parts of the profile matter physically?
- Why can different locations on the same board experience different temperatures?

This section develops those ideas before Section 4.15 introduces the hardware and workflow used to **measure and verify** the actual board profile.

---

## 4.14.1 - What Reflow Soldering Must Accomplish

During reflow, the oven must provide enough heat, for enough time, to let the solder paste complete several physical steps:

1. **Warm the PCB, components, pads, and paste in a controlled way.**
2. **Activate the flux and allow volatile materials to leave the paste.**
3. **Raise the solder above its liquidus region so it becomes molten.**
4. **Allow molten solder to wet the component termination and PCB land and form the metallurgical interface.**
5. **Cool the assembly so the solder solidifies without excessive thermal stress.**

Surface tension can also help a slightly displaced small component move toward a lower-energy aligned position while the solder is molten. This self-alignment effect is useful, but it is not a substitute for correct printing and placement.

The thermal process must therefore balance two competing needs:

> **enough heat and time to produce complete wetting and joint formation, but not so much heat or time that the PCB, components, flux, or solder joint are damaged.**

---

## 4.14.2 - Major Hardware of a Conveyorized Reflow Oven

The most common production reflow system is a **conveyorized forced-convection oven**. Machine designs vary, but several functional elements are easy to recognize.

| Oven element | Basic function | What to recognize |
|---|---|---|
| **Conveyor / rail system** | Moves the assembly through the oven at a controlled speed | Edge rails, mesh belt, optional center support, board direction |
| **Heating zones** | Add heat in controlled stages | Multiple independently controlled zones, usually with top and bottom heating |
| **Recirculation fans and nozzles** | Move hot process gas over the assembly | Forced convection above and below the board |
| **Cooling section** | Removes heat after reflow | One or more controlled cooling zones |
| **Exhaust / flux-management system** | Removes process vapors and limits residue buildup | Exhaust ducts, traps/filters, maintenance points |
| **Process atmosphere system** | Controls the gas surrounding the assembly | Air in many lines; nitrogen on processes that benefit from reduced oxidation |

Current production ovens may have roughly **6 to 12 heated zones, or more**, depending on machine length, throughput, and process-control needs. The exact number is not important by itself. More zones give the process engineer more places to shape the board's heating history.

A conveyor may support the PCB on a **mesh belt**, on **edge rails/chains**, or with a hybrid arrangement. Edge transport is common when bottom-side clearance is needed. Large or flexible boards may also use center support to reduce sag during heating.

## 4.14.3 - Oven Zones and Thermal-Profile Regions Are Not the Same Thing

A modern oven may have many physical heating zones, but the **board thermal profile** is usually discussed in a smaller number of functional regions.

That distinction matters:

- a **machine zone** is a physical section of the oven with a setpoint and airflow condition;
- a **profile region** describes what is happening to the assembly as its temperature changes with time.

A useful introductory profile has four functional regions:

1. **Ramp / preheat**
2. **Soak or thermal-equalization region, when used**
3. **Reflow / peak region**
4. **Cooling**

Some solder-paste processes use a clear soak region. Others use a more continuous **ramp-to-peak** profile with little or no flat soak. The solder-paste supplier's process window and the actual product determine which shape is appropriate.

The profile is a plot of **product temperature versus time**. It is not a plot of oven-zone setpoints.

> **Oven setpoint tells the machine what to do; the thermal profile describes what the PCB assembly actually experiences.**

Section 4.15 will show how that actual product profile is measured and verified.

## 4.14.4 - Reading the Basic Temperature-versus-Time Profile

A simple lead-free reflow profile can be interpreted by following the assembly from left to right.

### Ramp / preheat

The assembly starts near room temperature and warms gradually.

Main purposes:

- reduce abrupt thermal shock;
- begin flux activation;
- drive off some volatile materials in a controlled way;
- allow large and small parts to begin moving toward a more similar thermal state.

A ramp that is too aggressive can increase thermal stress and may promote paste spatter or solder-ball formation. A process that heats too slowly or spends too long at elevated temperature can consume useful flux activity before solder melting.

### Soak / thermal equalization

A soak region, when the paste/process uses one, slows the rate of temperature increase while different parts of the assembly move closer together thermally.

This region can:

- continue flux activity;
- allow more volatile material to leave the paste;
- reduce the temperature difference between low- and high-thermal-mass regions before reflow.

A long soak is not automatically better. Excessive time at elevated temperature can deplete flux activity and increase oxidation or other heat exposure.

### Reflow / peak

The solder becomes molten as the joint temperature rises above the process liquidus reference. During this region:

- solder wets the termination and land;
- surface tension acts on the molten solder/component system;
- the solder/base-metal interface develops the intermetallic layer needed for a metallurgical joint.

Two quantities are especially important:

- **peak temperature** - the highest temperature reached at the measured location;
- **time above liquidus (TAL)** - the total time that location remains above the defined liquidus reference.

### Cooling

After reflow, the assembly must cool below the solidification range so the joint becomes mechanically stable.

Cooling affects:

- how quickly the molten solder solidifies;
- solder microstructure;
- thermal stress in components, joints, and the PCB.

Cooling should therefore be controlled rather than treated as an unimportant exit step.

## 4.14.5 - Representative Lead-Free Profile Numbers

Exact limits depend on the **solder paste, alloy, component temperature limits, PCB construction, and product requirements**. A process engineer should therefore use the applicable material and product specifications rather than treat one textbook profile as universal.

Still, representative numbers are useful for understanding engineering scale. For a common SAC305 lead-free process, current solder-paste guidance often falls in the following general range:

| Profile quantity | Representative scale for SAC305 processing | Physical meaning |
|---|---:|---|
| Average heating ramp | roughly **0.7-2.0 °C/s** in many ramp-style profiles | How quickly the assembly temperature rises |
| Soak, if used | often roughly **140-180 °C** for **tens of seconds to about 2 min** | Flux activity and thermal equalization before reflow |
| Liquidus reference | about **217 °C** | Common reference used for SAC305 TAL calculations |
| Time above liquidus | often about **45-90 s** | Time available for molten-solder wetting and joint formation |
| Peak temperature | often about **235-245 °C** for many SAC305 paste processes | Maximum local product temperature during reflow |
| Cooling slope | commonly on the order of **1-6 °C/s** | Controlled solidification and removal of heat |

These are **representative process scales, not acceptance limits for every product**. Supplier recommendations differ. A paste that uses a ramp-to-peak profile may specify a different soak strategy, TAL, peak window, or cooling range.

### Why the liquidus reference matters

For a common SAC305 process, TAL is commonly referenced to approximately

$$
T_L \approx 217\ ^\circ\text{C}
$$

If a measured joint location rises above that reference at time $t_1$ and falls below it at time $t_2$, then

$$
\text{TAL} = t_2 - t_1
$$

For example, if that location is above the liquidus reference from 185 s to 245 s,

$$
\text{TAL} = 245 - 185 = 60\ \text{s}
$$

A 60 s TAL is within many current SAC305 paste-process windows, but the actual paste specification still controls the decision.

## 4.14.6 - Conveyor Speed Controls Residence Time

In an inline oven, the PCB moves continuously. Conveyor speed therefore controls how long the board spends under the heating and cooling hardware.

For a zone or heated length $L$ and conveyor speed $v$,

$$
t = \frac{L}{v}
$$

The units must be consistent.

### Simple transport example

Suppose one oven zone is 300 mm long and the conveyor is moving at

$$
v = 900\ \text{mm/min}
$$

Then the nominal time spent traveling through that physical zone is

$$
t = \frac{300\ \text{mm}}{900\ \text{mm/min}}
  = 0.333\ \text{min}
  \approx 20\ \text{s}
$$

If the conveyor is slowed, the board spends longer in **every** downstream zone. If it is sped up, residence time decreases.

This is why conveyor speed is one of the most important reflow controls. However, a 20 s physical residence time does **not** mean the PCB instantly reaches that zone's setpoint. The product temperature responds according to heat transfer and thermal mass.

A useful first-order idea is:

> **zone temperatures determine the thermal environment; conveyor speed determines exposure time; airflow determines how effectively heat is transferred to the product.**

## 4.14.7 - Different Parts of the Same Board Heat Differently

A reflow oven heats the entire assembly, but the assembly is not thermally uniform.

A small chip resistor may respond quickly because it has little thermal mass. A large connector, metal shield, power component, or heavy copper region may respond more slowly. Large copper planes can spread heat into surrounding board material, increase the effective thermal mass seen by a local joint, and change the local temperature response.

Important sources of temperature difference include:

- component size and mass;
- package material and exposed metal;
- local copper area and internal planes;
- PCB thickness and local board construction;
- component density and shielding of airflow;
- top-versus-bottom heating balance;
- local convection conditions.

This creates a practical reflow challenge:

> **the coldest joint must receive enough thermal exposure to solder correctly while the hottest temperature-sensitive location must stay within its allowable limit.**

### Applied example

Consider one board containing:

- several 0402 passive components;
- one large metal-shielded connector;
- a power package attached to a large copper plane.

The small passives will usually respond to oven-air changes faster than the high-mass connector or copper-coupled power region. A recipe chosen only from the small-component behavior could leave the heavy region too cold. A recipe extended until the heavy region reaches the required temperature could push a small or heat-sensitive location too hot.

The correct process therefore depends on the **thermal window of the whole assembly**, not on one convenient location.

## 4.14.8 - Air, Nitrogen, and Exhaust Awareness

### Air versus nitrogen

Many SMT assemblies can be reflowed successfully in air. Some processes use **nitrogen** to reduce oxygen concentration around the hot soldering surfaces.

Lower oxygen can:

- reduce re-oxidation during heating;
- improve wetting margin in difficult applications;
- provide a wider process window for some fine-pitch, small-deposit, or oxidation-sensitive assemblies.

Nitrogen does **not** automatically fix poor printing, bad placement, contamination, or an incorrect thermal profile. It also adds equipment and operating cost. Atmosphere choice is therefore a process decision rather than a universal requirement.

### Exhaust and flux management

Flux solvents and other volatile materials leave the paste during heating. The oven must manage those vapors so they do not accumulate on heaters, ducts, fans, and cooling hardware.

Poor flux/exhaust management can contribute to:

- residue buildup;
- restricted airflow;
- contamination of oven surfaces;
- reduced thermal repeatability over time.

Exhaust flow must be appropriate for the machine and process. In a nitrogen oven, excessive exhaust can also increase nitrogen consumption and disturb atmosphere control.

## 4.14.9 - Recognizing Profile Conditions That Need Attention

At this stage, the goal is not to diagnose every solder defect. Section 4.16 will integrate print, placement, and reflow defects. Here, the goal is to connect the **shape of the thermal process** to likely physical consequences.

| Profile condition | Basic physical concern |
|---|---|
| Ramp much too fast | Thermal shock, large temperature gradients, paste spatter or solder-ball risk |
| Extended heating before reflow | Flux depletion, oxidation, unnecessary component/board heat exposure |
| Peak/TAL too low or too short | Incomplete melting/wetting or insufficient joint formation |
| Peak/TAL excessive | Excessive intermetallic growth and unnecessary thermal stress/damage risk |
| Large temperature difference across the PCB | Some locations may be under-heated while others approach their maximum allowable temperature |
| Cooling too abrupt | Increased thermal-stress risk |
| Cooling too slow | Different solder microstructure and longer high-temperature exposure |

These relationships are reasons to **measure the product profile** rather than assuming that a reasonable-looking oven recipe must be correct.

---

## 4.14.10 - What to Recognize at the Reflow Oven

When observing a production reflow process, a beginning technologist should be able to identify and ask about the following:

- **Board transport:** Is the PCB stable on the rails/belt, and is center support used when needed?
- **Heating zones:** How many controlled zones are present, and are top/bottom heat and airflow available?
- **Conveyor speed:** What speed is the recipe using, and how does that affect residence time?
- **Cooling:** Is cooling controlled after the peak region?
- **Atmosphere:** Is the process using air or nitrogen, and why?
- **Exhaust/flux management:** Are process vapors being removed and residue buildup controlled?
- **Profile requirements:** What peak, TAL, ramp, soak, and cooling limits apply to this product/material system?
- **Thermal differences:** Which parts of the assembly are likely to heat quickly, and which are likely to lag?

The final question cannot be answered from oven setpoints alone. It requires measurement of the actual assembly, which is the focus of the next section.

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- reflow soldering uses a controlled thermal history to activate flux, melt solder, complete wetting/joint formation, and solidify the joint;
- production reflow ovens combine a conveyor, multiple heating zones, forced convection, cooling, exhaust/flux management, and air or nitrogen atmosphere control;
- physical oven zones are not the same thing as the ramp, soak, reflow/peak, and cooling regions used to describe a product thermal profile;
- peak temperature and time above liquidus are key reflow quantities, but their allowed ranges depend on the paste, alloy, components, PCB, and product requirements;
- conveyor speed controls residence time, while zone temperatures and airflow determine the heating environment and heat-transfer intensity;
- different components and board regions can follow different thermal histories in the same oven;
- a reasonable oven recipe does not prove that the actual PCB profile is acceptable.

Section 4.15 introduces the practical workflow for **measuring and verifying the actual reflow profile** on the PCB assembly.
