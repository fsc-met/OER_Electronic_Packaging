# 4.23 - NPI and Manufacturing Job Package Basics

Section 4.22 completed the major physical assembly, cleaning, and protection processes. Before those processes can be run correctly for a real product, the factory needs a controlled set of information that tells people and equipment **what product to build, which revision applies, how the job should be processed, and what must be checked before the build continues**.

That transition is part of **New Product Introduction (NPI)**.

NPI is the coordinated work used to move a new or revised product from released design information into a controlled manufacturing process. The exact workflow varies among companies, but the practical objective is the same:

> **Turn the released product definition into a buildable, verifiable manufacturing job without losing configuration control.**

This section focuses on one central question:

> **What information must the factory have to set up the processes we already learned?**

A company may call the controlled information for one build a **job package**, **work package**, **shop packet**, digital traveler, or another local name. This book uses **manufacturing job package** as a general term.

---

## 4.23.1 - NPI Connects Design Release to Manufacturing Readiness

A released design tells the organization what the approved product is supposed to be. Production still has to convert that released definition into usable manufacturing information, tooling, programs, instructions, and checks.

Typical NPI/manufacturing-readiness activities include:

1. identify the released product part number and revision;
2. review the applicable manufacturing data;
3. resolve missing, conflicting, or ambiguous information;
4. prepare product-specific tooling, machine programs, and work instructions;
5. confirm material, PCB/panel, stencil, fixture, and equipment readiness;
6. define the required setup/inspection/test checks;
7. perform an initial, prototype, pilot, or first-board build as required;
8. correct problems through the controlled process;
9. approve the job for the next authorized production stage.

These steps are not one universal corporate procedure. A prototype shop may use a compact workflow, while a high-volume or regulated product may require a much more formal approval process.

The important distinction is:

> **Design release defines the approved product. Manufacturing readiness confirms that the factory can build and verify that approved product with the intended process.**

---

## 4.23.2 - A Manufacturing Job Package Is a Controlled Information Set

A job package is not necessarily one file. It is the controlled information set needed to prepare, execute, verify, and identify a manufacturing job.

A useful way to organize that information is by the question it answers.

| Information group | Representative items | Main question answered |
|---|---|---|
| **Product definition** | BOM, approved manufacturer/alternate information, assembly drawing, placement/PnP data, PCB/panel identity | **What are we building?** |
| **Process definition** | stencil/paste information, work instructions, traveler/router, machine programs/recipes, special handling notes | **How should this job be built?** |
| **Verification definition** | setup/first-board checks, inspection-plan information, test-plan awareness, required signoffs/hold points | **What must be checked before the job continues?** |
| **Configuration identity** | product part number/revision, engineering-change status, controlled document/program identifiers | **Which approved version applies?** |

Not every factory uses the same documents or names. Some information may be carried in an integrated digital manufacturing-data system rather than separate files. The requirement is that the applicable information be complete enough, internally consistent, controlled, traceable to the authorized configuration, and usable by the people and equipment that perform the work.

## 4.23.3 - Recognize What the Major Product-Definition Files Do

Several documents can describe the same PCBA from different viewpoints. They should not be treated as interchangeable.

| Item | Main purpose | Practical question |
|---|---|---|
| **BOM** | identifies the required parts and controlled alternates/sources as applicable | **Which parts belong in this assembly?** |
| **Approved manufacturer / alternate information** | identifies which physical component configurations are approved for the internal requirement | **Which actual parts are allowed?** |
| **Assembly drawing** | gives a human-readable view of component locations, polarity/orientation, mechanical items, and special notes | **What should the assembled board look like?** |
| **Placement/PnP data** | gives machine-importable component location/orientation information | **Where and how should placement equipment place each component?** |
| **PCB/panel identity** | identifies the correct bare board/panel and revision | **Are we loading the correct physical board?** |
| **Stencil/paste information** | identifies the applicable stencil/tooling and solder-paste/process requirement | **Are the printing materials and tooling correct for this job?** |

Section 4.12 already developed the details of placement data, including reference designator, X/Y position, rotation, board side, origin, and convention checking. Section 4.23 uses placement data only as one controlled part of the larger job package.

A generic description such as `10 kΩ resistor` is usually not enough by itself to control production. The job package must connect the internal assembly requirement to the approved physical component configuration. The exact company terms may include an **Approved Manufacturer List (AML)**, **Approved Vendor List (AVL)**, controlled alternates in the BOM/ERP system, or other equivalent controls.

> **Do not substitute a visually similar part simply because it appears to fit. The approved configuration must come from controlled product information.**

Detailed reel, tray, lot/date-code, barcode, feeder, and line-side identity checks belong to Section 4.24.

## 4.23.4 - Work Instructions, Travelers, and Machine Programs Serve Different Functions

These three items are also easy to confuse.

| Controlled item | Main job |
|---|---|
| **Work instruction** | tells a person how to perform or verify a defined task |
| **Traveler / router** | controls or records the required route, operation sequence, status, signoffs, or hold points |
| **Machine program / recipe** | contains or references equipment-specific information used to run a manufacturing operation |

Examples of machine programs/recipes include:

- solder-paste printer product setup;
- pick-and-place program;
- reflow recipe;
- selective-solder program;
- inspection program.

A machine program is normally derived from and linked to the released product/process requirements, but it is equipment specific. Two factories can build the same approved PCBA using different machines and therefore use different program names or recipe identifiers.

> **Product revision and machine-program revision must be traceably linked, but they do not have to use the same revision label.**

---

## 4.23.5 - Revision Consistency Is a Production Requirement

Chapter 3 introduced release-package consistency from the design side. On the factory floor, the consequence is direct:

> **Do not set up or run a job until the applicable manufacturing information points to one controlled, approved product configuration.**

Consider a simplified example:

- product: `Controller Assembly A-100`;
- released product revision: `Rev D`;
- BOM: current for `Rev D`;
- assembly drawing: current for `Rev D`;
- placement data: regenerated for `Rev D`;
- placement machine program: still based on the previous `Rev C` export.

The machine program may load correctly. The feeder setup may look normal. The first board may even look reasonable.

The job is still **not ready to continue**.

The correct response is to stop at the appropriate hold point, identify the stale information, update or regenerate the affected controlled output, verify the corrected setup, and document the approved status. Do not solve a configuration-control problem with an undocumented shop-floor workaround.

If the controlled product definition itself must change, the change must follow the organization's applicable engineering/configuration-control process before the revised definition is used for production. Terms such as **engineering change order (ECO)** and **engineering change notice (ECN)** are common, but company usage varies.

The general sequence is:

> **change the controlled source -> update affected manufacturing outputs -> recheck the affected setup -> document the approved status**

## 4.23.6 - Prototype, Pilot, First-Board, and Process Approval Are Different Ideas

NPI often uses staged builds before routine production. The exact names and formality vary, so focus on the purpose.

| Term | Basic purpose at this chapter level |
|---|---|
| **Prototype build** | learn about the product and prove basic design/manufacturing feasibility, often with extra manual work or temporary tooling |
| **Pilot / staged build** | use more production-like materials, tooling, programs, instructions, and sequence to learn whether the intended manufacturing method is ready |
| **First-board / first-article awareness** | perform extra verification on the first board or small initial set from a new setup, revision, or approved change before the broader run proceeds |
| **Process approval / qualification awareness** | provide the required documented evidence that a process, material, recipe, or approved change is suitable for its intended production use under the applicable requirements |

A successful prototype does not automatically prove that the intended production process is ready. Likewise, a local first-board check should not automatically be treated as equivalent to a formal regulated-industry First Article Inspection requirement.

## 4.23.7 - A Practical Job-Package Readiness Check

Before a new or revised PCBA job proceeds to setup or first-board verification, a technician or engineer should be able to answer the following questions from controlled information.

| Readiness question | Evidence to check |
|---|---|
| **What exact product are we building?** | assembly part number and released revision |
| **Which parts are approved?** | current BOM and approved manufacturer/alternate information |
| **What should the assembly look like?** | assembly drawing, orientation/polarity information, special notes |
| **Where should components be placed?** | current placement/PnP data and approved placement program |
| **What process route and instructions apply?** | traveler/router, work instructions, process notes |
| **What tooling/material setup applies?** | PCB/panel identity, stencil/tooling identity, paste/process information |
| **Which machine programs/recipes apply?** | approved printer, placement, reflow, selective-solder, inspection, or other applicable identifiers |
| **What must be checked before continuing?** | setup/first-board checks, inspection/test-plan awareness, signoffs/hold points |
| **Are any changes or exceptions still open?** | engineering-change/deviation status and required approvals |

If one of these answers cannot be established, the correct action is not to guess. Hold the job at the appropriate point and resolve the missing or conflicting information through the controlled process.

> **Production-readiness principle:** The factory should be able to identify **what to build, how to build it, what to verify, and which approved configuration applies** before normal production begins.

---

## 4.23.8 - Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- **NPI** is the controlled transition from released design information to a manufacturable job;
- a manufacturing job package is a controlled **information set**, not one universal file;
- the BOM, approved-part information, assembly drawing, placement data, PCB/panel identity, and stencil/paste information answer different manufacturing questions;
- work instructions, travelers/routers, and machine programs/recipes serve different functions;
- manufacturing artifacts must be traceably linked to the correct approved product configuration;
- prototype, pilot, first-board/first-article, and process qualification/approval are related but different ideas;
- stale or conflicting information should stop the job rather than be resolved by undocumented guesswork.

Section 4.24 next moves from the **information package** to the **physical material at the line**: kitting, component identity, quantity, feeder location, solder-paste status, moisture status, and bare-PCB verification.

### Important technical clarifications

1. **Manufacturing job package is a generic book term, not one mandated industry file.**
   Companies may use job package, shop packet, work package, digital traveler, MES job, or other terminology.

2. **AML/AVL terminology is not universal.**
   Approved manufacturer control and approved supplier/vendor control can be separate functions. Public text therefore uses `approved manufacturer/alternate information` as the broad concept.

3. **Placement data are not retaught here.**
   Section 4.12 owns coordinate fields, origins, rotation conventions, board side, and fiducial alignment.

4. **Product revision is not necessarily machine-program revision.**
   Different controlled manufacturing artifacts can use different identifiers while remaining traceably linked to the same approved product configuration.

5. **First-board/first-article language is context dependent.**
   The public section explicitly avoids equating an internal setup check with a formal regulated-industry FAI requirement.

6. **Prototype success does not equal production-process qualification.**
   Prototype builds can use temporary/manual methods and therefore do not by themselves prove production readiness.

7. **No universal numerical values belong here.**
   This is a documentation/readiness section; process numbers remain in the applicable process sections.
