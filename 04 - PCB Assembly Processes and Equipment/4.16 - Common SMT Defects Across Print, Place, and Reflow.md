# 4.16 - Common SMT Defects Across Print, Place, and Reflow

Sections 4.6 through 4.15 developed the three major SMT process stages: **solder-paste printing, component placement, and reflow soldering**. After reflow, a defect may be visible at one solder joint, but its cause may have started several process steps earlier.

A solder bridge, open, tombstone, or shifted component is therefore not automatically a "reflow problem." The same visible result can come from different combinations of land pattern, paste deposition, placement, material condition, and thermal history.

> **A defect is an observation. Root cause must be supported by evidence.**

This section combines the earlier process knowledge into a practical troubleshooting method:

> **Observation -> evidence -> likely mechanism -> check the upstream process -> next action**

The goal is not to memorize one cause for every defect. The goal is to know what evidence to collect before changing the process.

---

## 4.16.1 - One Defect Can Have Several Upstream Sources

The final solder joint is the result of several earlier conditions acting together. A useful first step is to separate the possible source categories.

| Source category | Typical questions to ask |
|---|---|
| **Design / land pattern** | Are pad size, spacing, symmetry, solder-mask openings, and copper balance reasonable? |
| **Paste printing** | Was the paste deposit centered, complete, and reasonably balanced in area, height, and volume? |
| **Placement** | Was the correct component placed at the correct location and orientation, with suitable seating and alignment? |
| **Component / PCB condition** | Are terminations and pads solderable, clean, undamaged, and within material-handling requirements? |
| **Reflow process** | Did the important locations experience the required ramp, peak, time above liquidus, and cooling behavior? |
| **Handling / material status** | Was paste, PCB, and component storage/handling consistent with the approved process? |

These categories overlap. For example, tombstoning can involve unequal paste deposits, placement offset, copper asymmetry, and uneven heating at the same time.

## 4.16.2 - Start with Evidence, Not a Guess

When a defect is found, use the same sequence every time:

1. **Describe the observation.**
   - What is physically wrong?
   - Is the defect visible before reflow, after reflow, or only during inspection/test?
2. **Collect evidence.**
   - solder-paste inspection (SPI) or print records;
   - pre-reflow placement condition;
   - machine alarms or setup history;
   - measured reflow profile;
   - post-reflow visual/AOI evidence;
   - X-ray evidence when the joint is hidden.
3. **Identify the likely physical mechanism.**
   - too little solder volume;
   - unwanted solder connection;
   - unequal wetting forces;
   - poor wetting;
   - component not contacting the pad;
   - thermal or material condition outside the intended process.
4. **Check the upstream process that could create that mechanism.**
5. **Make the smallest logical correction and verify the result.**

A poor troubleshooting habit is:

> **"The defect appeared after reflow, so change the oven."**

A better habit is:

> **"What evidence tells us whether the problem started in design, print, placement, material condition, or reflow?"**

## 4.16.3 - Insufficient Solder and Opens

**Insufficient solder** means the joint has less solder than needed to form the intended connection. An **open** means electrical continuity is missing. The two can be related, but they are not identical.

Typical observations include:

- a very small or incomplete solder fillet;
- one termination that is not joined to the pad;
- a lead or termination visibly lifted away from the solder;
- an electrical open found during test;
- a hidden BGA connection that failed to coalesce even though solder is present.

Possible upstream sources include:

- incomplete aperture fill or poor paste release;
- clogged, undersized, or poorly releasing stencil apertures;
- insufficient paste volume;
- print misalignment that leaves part of the pad uncovered;
- bent or non-coplanar leads;
- poor component seating or placement offset;
- oxidized or contaminated surfaces that do not wet;
- insufficient thermal exposure for the specific paste/joint location;
- package/board warpage that separates contacting surfaces during reflow.

The first check should match the evidence. If SPI already showed low paste volume at the failed pad, investigate printing before changing the reflow recipe. If paste volume was acceptable but the lead was lifted before reflow, investigate component coplanarity and placement. If the joint was properly printed and seated but did not wet, material condition and thermal history become more important.

> **An open circuit does not prove that the oven was too cold.**

## 4.16.4 - Solder Bridging

A **solder bridge** is an unintended solder connection between conductors that should remain electrically separate. It can create an electrical short.

Common upstream contributors include:

- excessive paste volume;
- paste printed between adjacent pads;
- paste slumping before reflow;
- stencil-to-pad misregistration;
- poor gasketing or stencil underside contamination;
- component placement offset that pushes paste toward a neighboring pad;
- insufficient pad spacing or problematic solder-mask/land geometry;
- solder movement during reflow that allows already-excess material to connect adjacent conductors.

Useful evidence includes the SPI image/volume data, the pre-reflow component position, stencil/aperture condition, and the exact location of the final bridge.

If paste was already connecting two pads before placement, the strongest evidence points upstream toward the print/stencil process. Changing peak temperature first would not address that physical condition.

---

## 4.16.5 - Solder Balls and Spatter

**Solder balls** are small spheres of solder outside the intended joint area. They may appear on solder mask, beside a component, or between closely spaced conductors.

Possible sources include:

- excessive or stray paste deposited outside the intended pad area;
- paste squeeze-out caused by excessive placement force;
- paste slumping or smearing;
- rapid heating or volatile outgassing that expels small amounts of solder;
- contamination or poor surface condition;
- solder-paste condition, storage, or handling problems.

Do not assume that every solder ball is created by the same reflow-profile error. First determine **where the solder came from**. If the print image shows paste on solder mask before placement, the evidence is different from a board that printed cleanly but produced spatter during heating.

Loose solder balls also matter because a mobile conductive sphere can violate electrical spacing or later move into an unwanted location.

---

## 4.16.6 - Tombstoning

A **tombstone** occurs when one end of a small chip component lifts from its pad during reflow while the other end remains soldered.

The immediate mechanism is usually an **imbalance in wetting forces**. One termination wets and pulls before the opposite termination develops a balancing force.

Several process stages can create that imbalance:

| Possible source | Example mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Design / copper geometry** | one pad is connected to much more copper and heats differently from the other |
| **Paste printing** | one pad receives more or less paste than the other |
| **Placement** | component is offset, rotated, or seated unevenly |
| **Component / pad condition** | one termination or pad wets differently because of oxidation, contamination, or finish condition |
| **Reflow** | the two ends reach melting/wetting conditions at different times |

This is why tombstoning is a useful troubleshooting example: it connects **design, print, place, materials, and reflow** in one defect.

If repeated tombstones occur at the same reference designator, compare the two pad deposits, placement centering, local copper geometry, and local thermal response before making an oven adjustment.

## 4.16.7 - Skew, Shift, Lifted Leads, and Misplaced Components

Not every placement-related problem first appears after reflow.

### Skew or shift

A component may be rotated or displaced from its intended pad position.

Possible sources include:

- incorrect placement coordinates or rotation;
- feeder or vision-recognition error;
- board/fiducial alignment problem;
- placement offset;
- uneven paste tack or paste volume;
- unbalanced wetting forces during reflow.

A useful distinction is **when the shift became visible**:

- wrong **before reflow** -> investigate placement, alignment, setup, and paste condition first;
- correct **before reflow but shifted after reflow** -> investigate wetting-force imbalance, paste symmetry, pad/copper geometry, and thermal behavior.

Small placement offsets can sometimes improve through solder self-alignment during reflow, but self-alignment should not be used to justify an uncontrolled placement process.

### Lifted leads or poor contact

A lead can remain above the pad because of:

- bent leads or component coplanarity problems;
- insufficient or uneven placement seating;
- board deflection during placement;
- paste geometry that does not create reliable contact;
- warpage or movement during reflow.

A lifted lead may produce an open even when the local paste volume is adequate.

---

## 4.16.8 - Non-Wetting and Poor Wetting

**Wetting** occurs when molten solder spreads over and bonds to the intended metal surface. Poor wetting means that this metallurgical contact did not develop adequately.

Two related terms are useful:

- **non-wetting** - solder contacts the surface but does not form adequate wetting/bonding, leaving exposed base metal;
- **dewetting** - solder initially wets and then recedes, leaving irregular solder areas after the surface has been contacted.

Possible sources include:

- oxidized or contaminated pads/terminations;
- poor component or PCB solderability;
- unsuitable or degraded surface finish;
- insufficient flux activity or paste-condition problems;
- inadequate thermal activation for the materials being used;
- excessive thermal exposure that depletes flux before wetting is completed;
- atmosphere or handling conditions that increase oxidation.

Do not diagnose wetting quality only from whether the joint looks shiny. Modern lead-free joints can naturally appear duller or grainier than traditional tin-lead joints. Judge the **wetting geometry, coverage, continuity, and applicable acceptance criteria** instead of using brightness alone.

> **Poor wetting is often a material-and-surface problem, a flux/process problem, or both. It is not automatically solved by increasing peak temperature.**

---

## 4.16.9 - Hidden BGA Joint Defects

Ball grid array (BGA) and other bottom-terminated packages hide their solder joints underneath the component body. A normal-looking package from the top does not prove that all hidden joints are acceptable.

Hidden-joint defects can include:

- bridges between adjacent solder balls;
- missing or insufficient connections;
- voiding;
- package/board warpage effects;
- **head-in-pillow (HiP)**, where the package solder ball and the reflowed paste deposit do not fully coalesce, creating an open or weak connection.

Direct visual inspection cannot see the underside of a BGA. X-ray inspection can provide internal structural evidence such as solder-ball shape, bridges, voids, and some open/HiP conditions, but no single inspection method detects every possible defect. Detailed AOI, X-ray, and electrical-test strategy is developed later in the book.

## 4.16.10 - A Practical First-Check Matrix

The table below is not a substitute for a product-specific troubleshooting plan. It is a first-pass guide for deciding what evidence to collect.

| Defect observation | High-value first checks |
|---|---|
| **Insufficient solder / open** | SPI volume/release, lead or termination contact, placement seating, solderability, measured profile |
| **Bridge** | paste volume and registration, stencil underside/aperture, placement offset, pad/mask spacing |
| **Solder balls** | stray/excess paste, placement squeeze-out, paste condition/contamination, heating/outgassing behavior |
| **Tombstone** | paste balance on both pads, placement centering, pad/copper symmetry, local thermal balance |
| **Skew / shift** | pre-reflow position, fiducial/alignment evidence, paste tack/volume, post-reflow wetting imbalance |
| **Non-wetting / poor wetting** | pad/termination condition, material storage/handling, paste/flux condition, measured thermal history |
| **BGA hidden open / HiP** | print volume, package/board warpage evidence, reflow profile, X-ray/internal evidence, electrical test |

A pattern matters. One isolated random defect and the same defect repeating at the same component location may require different investigations.

- **same location repeatedly** -> suspect a systematic design, stencil/aperture, library, feeder/component, or local thermal condition;
- **random locations across the board** -> investigate broader material, equipment, process, or handling variation;
- **many defect types appearing together after a process change** -> look for a common upstream change before tuning each symptom independently.

This pattern check does not prove root cause, but it helps decide where to collect the next evidence.

## 4.16.11 - Worked Troubleshooting Example: A Repeating Tombstone

Suppose the same small capacitor repeatedly tombstones after reflow.

### Observation

One end of the capacitor is lifted after reflow.

### Evidence

- SPI shows one pad consistently receives less paste than the opposite pad.
- Pre-reflow placement is centered and the correct component is used.
- The measured reflow profile is within the approved window at the relevant board locations.

### Likely mechanism

The two ends do not develop balanced wetting forces because the solder deposits are unequal.

### Check the upstream process

Investigate:

- stencil aperture condition and cleanliness;
- paste release from the two apertures;
- stencil-to-pad alignment;
- board support/gasketing under that location;
- pad/copper geometry if the print process is stable.

### Next action

Correct the print-related cause, print another board, verify the two deposits with SPI, and then confirm the post-reflow result.

Changing the oven first would be a weak response because the available evidence already points upstream. It could also disturb joints that were already processing correctly.

Now consider a different case: the two paste deposits are balanced and placement is centered, but profiling shows a strong temperature difference between the two pads because one pad is tied into a much larger copper region. The same visible tombstone now points toward a **thermal/design imbalance**, so the next action would be different.

> **The observation can stay the same while the correct next action changes with the evidence.**

---

## Section Takeaway

After this section, you should be able to:

- recognize common SMT defects such as insufficient solder, opens, bridges, solder balls, tombstoning, skew/shift, lifted leads, and poor wetting;
- explain why a post-reflow defect can originate in design, printing, placement, material condition, or reflow;
- distinguish a visible defect from its possible root causes;
- use SPI, pre-reflow placement evidence, measured profile data, and post-reflow inspection evidence to narrow the source;
- recognize that hidden BGA joints require internal inspection awareness;
- use the troubleshooting pattern **observation -> evidence -> likely mechanism -> upstream check -> next action**;
- avoid changing the reflow recipe when the evidence points to an earlier process stage.

With the major SMT process now connected from **print -> place -> reflow -> defect evidence**, Section 4.17 moves to the next assembly technology: **through-hole components and insertion**, where component leads or pins pass through plated holes before soldering.

## Authoring and QA Notes

### Section purpose

Integrate the three major SMT stages after students have learned printing, placement, reflow, and profiling. The section should teach defect recognition and evidence-based upstream troubleshooting at an entry-level Engineering Technology level. It must not become a standards-acceptability chapter, an advanced failure-analysis chapter, or a detailed AOI/X-ray/test chapter.

### Primary-instructional-material teaching inventory

Retained from the MET406 Assembly Technologies material:

- solder-paste printing defects: insufficient paste/opens, excess paste/bridging, slumping, incomplete release, and print misalignment;
- placement defects: misalignment, missing/dropped component, tombstoning, lifted leads/coplanarity;
- reflow defects: poor/cold wetting behavior, non-wetting/dewetting, solder balling/spattering, head-in-pillow, tombstoning, excessive thermal exposure awareness, and cracked/open-joint awareness;
- physical wetting concept and contact-angle awareness from the soldering-fundamentals material;
- post-reflow visual/AOI awareness for bridges, solder volume, tombstones, misalignment, and lifted leads;
- X-ray awareness for hidden joints such as BGA/QFN and defects such as voids, bridges, and head-in-pillow;
- recurring idea that printing, placement, and reflow conditions interact.

Required outline items explicitly covered:

- insufficient solder;
- bridging;
- solder balls;
- tombstoning;
- skew/shift;
- opens;
- non-wetting/poor wetting;
- lifted or misplaced components;
- BGA/hidden-joint defect awareness;
- multiple possible sources: design/land pattern, paste printing, placement, component/board condition, reflow profile, and handling/material status;
- recurring troubleshooting model: observation -> evidence -> likely mechanism -> upstream process -> next action;
- explicit warning not to blame every solder defect on reflow.

Corrected or qualified:

- the primary material's simple "dull, grainy = cold joint" cue is not used as a standalone diagnosis. Lead-free solder can naturally appear duller/grainier; wetting geometry, continuity, product criteria, and process evidence are more reliable;
- head-in-pillow is treated as a hidden BGA/open-joint condition involving separation/warpage and failure to coalesce, not as a defect that can always be confirmed by ordinary top-side visual inspection;
- solder balls are treated as potentially originating from print, placement, paste/material condition, contamination, or heating/outgassing rather than a single reflow cause;
- tombstoning is treated as an imbalance of wetting forces with design, paste, placement, material, and thermal contributors rather than a single ramp-rate problem;
- opens are distinguished from insufficient solder because an open can also result from lifted leads, poor wetting, warpage, or head-in-pillow even when solder volume is present;
- the lecture statement that a fixed percentage of SMT defects originate in printing is not repeated because that percentage depends strongly on product, equipment, measurement method, and production environment and is not needed for the section's teaching goal.

Deferred to later content:

- detailed AOI programming and false-call management;
- X-ray equipment physics and 2D/3D/CT method selection;
- electrical test strategy;
- detailed workmanship acceptance criteria and product-class decisions;
- SPC/process capability analysis and defect-rate statistics;
- destructive failure analysis and metallography;
- rework/repair procedure;
- THT/wave/selective solder defect troubleshooting, introduced after the relevant processes are taught.

### Technical verification basis

- Primary instructional baseline: MET406 Chapter 4 - Assembly Technologies, solder-paste printing defect pages, placement defect pages, soldering fundamentals, reflow defect pages, and inspection-method awareness.
- Clyde F. Coombs Jr., *Printed Circuits Handbook*, 6th ed.: definitions and causes of insufficient solder, bridging, solder balls, non-wetting/dewetting, inspection/process-indicator concepts, and the need to distinguish defects from upstream process conditions.
- Glenn R. Blackwell, *The Electronic Packaging Handbook*: SMT defect causes across paste volume, placement, reflow, tombstoning/drawbridging, solder balls, visual inspection limitations, and hidden-joint inspection.
- Current Indium technical guidance: tombstoning as unequal wetting-force behavior with stencil/printing, placement, board design, and reflow contributors; head-in-pillow as a BGA/warpage/coalescence problem; solder balling/poor wetting/open-joint troubleshooting across paste, surface, and reflow variables.
- Current AIM lead-free assembly guidance: lead-free solder joints can naturally appear duller/grainier, so brightness alone is not a reliable defect criterion.
