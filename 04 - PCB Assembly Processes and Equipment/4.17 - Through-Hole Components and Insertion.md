# 4.17 - Through-Hole Components and Insertion

Surface-mount technology places component terminations on pads at the PCB surface. **Through-hole technology (THT)** uses component leads or pins that pass through **plated through-holes (PTHs)** in the PCB.

That physical difference changes the assembly task. Before soldering can begin, the component must be identified, oriented, mechanically fitted to the board, and held in the intended position.

THT is still common where the component geometry, mechanical loading, power level, connector style, or production method makes a lead-through-hole connection practical. Typical examples include connectors, transformers, relays, large electrolytic capacitors, LEDs, and some power devices.

> **The first THT manufacturing problem is not soldering. It is getting the correct component into the correct holes, in the correct orientation, at the correct seating condition, without damaging the part or PCB.**

This section focuses on that physical insertion step. Section 4.18 will continue with wave soldering.

---

## 4.17.1 - What Makes a Component Through-Hole?

A through-hole component has one or more conductive leads, pins, or terminals that enter holes in the PCB. For a conventional soldered THT connection, the lead passes through a plated hole and is later joined to the plated barrel and surface pad by solder.

The PCB hole is therefore doing more than providing empty space. It must:

- accept the component lead without forcing or damaging it;
- place the lead where the design intends;
- provide a plated conductive barrel for the electrical interconnection;
- leave enough process clearance for the selected assembly method;
- maintain the annular copper and structural integrity established by the PCB design.

Earlier PCB and DFM sections already introduced finished-hole size, lead fit, annular ring, tolerance, and manufacturability. Here, those ideas become an **assembly check** rather than a new hole-design lesson.

If a lead does not enter the intended hole normally, the operator should not solve the problem by forcing it through. The next check is whether the correct component, hole pattern, lead condition, and board revision are being used.

## 4.17.2 - Common THT Component Forms

Several component forms appear repeatedly in through-hole assembly.

| Component form | Physical recognition | Typical assembly concern |
|---|---|---|
| **Axial leaded** | leads exit opposite ends of the body | leads may need forming to match the hole spacing |
| **Radial leaded** | leads exit from the same side of the body | polarity, lead spacing, and seating are often important |
| **DIP / multi-pin package** | two or more organized rows of pins | all pins must align with the hole pattern without bending |
| **Connector** | multiple pins in a rigid housing | orientation, full seating, pin straightness, and mechanical support |
| **Transformer / relay / large odd-form part** | larger body with several leads or tabs | mass, orientation, support, and insertion access |
| **Power device** | thicker leads or tabs; may include a heatsink interface | lead geometry, thermal/mechanical support, and orientation |

The terms **axial** and **radial** describe the lead arrangement, not the electrical function of the part. A resistor is often axial. An electrolytic capacitor or LED is often radial. Connectors and transformers are commonly treated as **odd-form** components because their shapes and pin patterns do not fit one simple axial/radial category.

THT should not be described as automatically “better” or “stronger” than SMT in every application. Its practical advantage is that the lead passes through the PCB and can provide a useful mechanical load path for parts such as connectors and large components when the product is designed for it.

## 4.17.3 - Lead Forming and Preparation

Before insertion, some THT leads must be prepared so the lead geometry matches the PCB footprint.

**Lead forming** may include:

- bending axial leads to the required span;
- adjusting lead spacing for a radial component;
- straightening slightly displaced pins when permitted by the component/process instruction;
- cutting leads to a controlled length at the appropriate stage of the process.

Lead forming should change the **lead geometry**, not damage the component package. Avoid sharp bends directly at the package body or seal unless the component manufacturer's instructions specifically permit them. Also avoid nicking, scraping, or cracking the lead finish because damaged surfaces can later create solderability or reliability problems.

A good preparation step asks:

> **Does the lead pattern naturally match the board holes without forcing either the component or PCB?**

If the answer is no, stop and determine whether the part, footprint, lead-forming setup, or board is incorrect.

## 4.17.4 - Manual and Automated Insertion

THT insertion can be manual or automated. The correct method depends on production volume, component form, available equipment, and how much variation the product can tolerate.

### Manual insertion

Manual insertion is common for:

- prototypes and low-volume production;
- large connectors and transformers;
- irregular or low-usage odd-form components;
- products where automation would not be economical.

The operator identifies the component, verifies orientation, aligns the leads with the hole pattern, inserts the part, and confirms seating before the board moves to the next operation.

Manual insertion is flexible, but it also creates opportunities for variation in orientation, seating height, lead protrusion, and handling.

### Automated insertion

Automated insertion is practical for standardized, repeatable component formats such as taped axial and radial components. Modern through-hole insertion equipment can sequence parts, prepare leads, position the PCB, insert the component with controlled motion/force, and provide programmed clinch options.

Automation improves consistency and throughput, but it still depends on correct:

- component supply and identification;
- program/board data;
- insertion tooling;
- lead geometry;
- hole pattern and board support.

A machine cannot compensate for the wrong part or an incompatible footprint.

## 4.17.5 - Polarity and Orientation Must Be Correct Before Soldering

Some THT components can be inserted in more than one physical direction even though only one orientation is electrically correct.

Examples include:

- electrolytic capacitors;
- diodes;
- LEDs;
- polarized connectors;
- relays or modules with keyed pin assignments;
- transformers with primary/secondary or pin-1 orientation requirements.

Other parts, such as many ordinary resistors, are electrically nonpolar, although their orientation may still be controlled for readability, process consistency, or downstream access.

Before insertion, verify polarity/orientation using the approved assembly documentation and the component's physical marking or keying feature. Silkscreen can help, but it should not be treated as the only authority when the drawing, BOM, work instruction, or product data define the orientation.

> **Soldering permanently connects what was inserted. It does not correct a reversed component.**

---

## 4.17.6 - Seating and Stand-Off

After insertion, the component should sit at the intended **seating condition**.

Some parts are designed to sit close to the board. Others intentionally use a **stand-off** between the component body and PCB. The correct condition depends on the package, thermal/mechanical design, cleaning/access needs, and product documentation.

A useful inspection is to look for:

- all intended leads fully entering their holes;
- the component body level or positioned as specified;
- no lead being forced sideways by a mismatched hole;
- no unintended tilt caused by one bent lead;
- required standoffs, spacers, shoulders, or connector features seated correctly;
- heavy parts supported as required rather than hanging from stressed leads alone.

Do not assume that “flush to the PCB” is always correct. A forced flush condition can stress leads or package seals, while an excessive stand-off can create its own mechanical and assembly problems.

## 4.17.7 - Clinching, Retention, and Lead Cutting

A newly inserted component may need temporary retention so it does not lift, shift, or fall out before soldering.

One common method is **lead clinching**, in which the protruding lead is bent after insertion. Clinching is a retention method, not a requirement for every THT component.

The amount and direction of clinching must follow the product and process documentation because excessive bending can:

- place unnecessary stress on the lead or component body;
- load the plated hole or pad;
- interfere with solder flow around the lead;
- reduce electrical clearance to nearby conductors;
- make later removal or rework more difficult.

Other retention methods can include formed retention tabs, connector latches, fixtures, board supports, or dedicated hardware.

**Lead cutting/trimming** is also process-dependent. Some automated inserters cut and form leads as part of insertion. Other processes trim leads before or after soldering. The goal is not a universal protrusion dimension; the goal is a lead condition that meets the applicable drawing/workmanship requirement and does not interfere with the next manufacturing step.

## 4.17.8 - Connectors and Transformers Need Extra Mechanical Awareness

Connectors and transformers are useful examples because they show why THT insertion is more than simply “putting leads into holes.”

### Connector example

A multi-pin connector may require checks for:

- correct connector family and keying;
- pin straightness;
- all pins entering their corresponding holes;
- housing fully seated to the specified datum or board surface;
- board locks, pegs, latches, or mounting hardware engaged when provided;
- no connector body rocking because one pin is bent or blocked.

Trying to force a misaligned multi-pin connector into the PCB can damage both the pins and plated holes.

### Transformer or large-component example

A transformer or similar heavy part may require:

- correct pin pattern and orientation;
- adequate support during insertion;
- controlled seating so the body does not load the leads unevenly;
- retention features, hardware, or fixtures when specified;
- awareness that its large thermal mass will matter during the later soldering process.

The insertion task should leave the part mechanically stable and correctly positioned before wave or selective soldering begins.

---

## 4.17.9 - Press-Fit Is a Different Through-Hole Interconnection

Not every pin inserted into a plated hole is intended to be soldered.

A **press-fit** connection uses a specially designed pin—commonly a **compliant pin**—that is pressed into a specified plated through-hole. The compliant section deforms during insertion and maintains contact force against the plated barrel, creating a mechanical and electrical connection without a solder joint.

Press-fit is common in some connector, backplane, automotive, and high-pin-count applications.

The important distinction is:

| Soldered THT connection | Press-fit connection |
|---|---|
| lead/pin enters a plated hole and is later soldered | compliant pin is mechanically pressed into a specified plated hole |
| solder forms the final electrical/mechanical interconnection | contact force between the pin and plated barrel forms the interconnection |
| wave, selective, or hand soldering may follow | no soldering step is required for the press-fit joint |
| ordinary THT hole/lead requirements apply to the soldering process | pin design, finished-hole size/tolerance, plating, tooling, insertion force, and seating are part of one controlled press-fit system |

A press-fit pin should therefore never be treated as an ordinary THT pin that is simply pushed harder into the board. The connector/pin manufacturer and PCB requirements define the required hole and tooling conditions.

## 4.17.10 - A Practical Insertion Check Before Soldering

Consider a board containing four THT parts:

- one axial resistor;
- one polarized electrolytic capacitor;
- one multi-pin connector;
- one transformer.

A useful pre-solder check is:

| Part | What should be verified before the board leaves insertion? |
|---|---|
| Axial resistor | correct value; leads formed to the hole span; body positioned as specified; leads not forced |
| Electrolytic capacitor | correct value; polarity correct; leads in correct holes; seating/stand-off correct |
| Connector | correct part and keying; every pin entered its hole; housing fully seated; retention features engaged as required |
| Transformer | correct orientation/pin pattern; body supported; seating stable; no lead or PCB damage |

Then check the assembly as a whole:

- no missing THT components;
- no visibly bent pins that missed their holes;
- no reversed polarized parts;
- no component forced into a mismatched pattern;
- no unintended component tilt;
- retention/clinch only where specified;
- lead condition suitable for the next soldering operation.

This is the THT equivalent of the first-board verification used earlier for SMT placement: catch the physical setup problem **before** heat and solder make it harder to correct.

---

## 4.17.11 - Section Takeaway

Through-hole assembly begins with a physical fit between the **component lead/pin** and the **plated PCB hole**.

The most important practical points are:

- recognize common axial, radial, connector, transformer, and other odd-form THT parts;
- verify the correct part, hole pattern, polarity, and orientation before insertion;
- form leads only as needed and avoid forcing a mismatched lead pattern into the board;
- distinguish manual insertion from automated axial/radial or odd-form insertion;
- establish the specified seating or stand-off condition;
- use clinching, retention, and lead trimming only as required by the process;
- recognize press-fit as a controlled solderless through-hole interconnection, not ordinary THT soldering.

Once the components are correctly inserted and retained, the next manufacturing problem is how to solder many of those protruding leads consistently. **Section 4.18 moves to wave soldering equipment, the flux -> preheat -> wave-contact -> separation -> cooling sequence, and the common process problems that can occur there.**
