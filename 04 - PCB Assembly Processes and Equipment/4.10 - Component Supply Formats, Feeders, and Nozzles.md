# 4.10 - Component Supply Formats, Feeders, and Nozzles

Section 4.9 ended with a printed PCB that has acceptable solder-paste deposits and is ready for component placement. Before a placement head can move any component to the board, however, the machine must first receive that component in a controlled way.

Three physical elements work together:

> **component supply format -> feeder / presentation mechanism -> pickup tool**

The **supply format** stores and protects the components. The **feeder** or presentation device brings the next component to a known pickup location. The **nozzle** then grips the component, most commonly by vacuum, so the placement head can move it. Not every supply method uses the same kind of feeder, but every automated pickup needs a predictable part position and orientation.

A placement program can contain the correct X/Y coordinates and rotation and still fail if the component is not presented correctly. For a beginning technologist, the important question is therefore:

> **Can the machine repeatedly find the correct part, at the expected orientation and pickup location, and hold it securely with the correct pickup tool?**

---

## 4.10.1 - Supply Format, Feeder, and Nozzle Are Different Things

These terms are related, but they should not be used interchangeably.

| Item | Main job | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **component supply format** | stores, protects, and organizes components before pickup | tape-and-reel, matrix tray, tube/stick, bulk container |
| **feeder / presentation device** | advances or presents the next component at a repeatable pickup location | electric tape feeder, tray handler, stick feeder, vibratory feeder |
| **nozzle / pickup tool** | physically holds the component while the placement head moves it | round vacuum nozzle, rectangular nozzle, custom nozzle, special gripper |

A reel by itself is not a feeder. It is a package of carrier tape containing components. The feeder accepts that tape, advances it, exposes the next component, and establishes the pickup position.

Likewise, the nozzle does not determine where the component belongs on the PCB. It is the physical interface that picks up and carries the part. Placement coordinates, vision, board alignment, and machine motion are introduced in Sections 4.11 and 4.12.

---

## 4.10.2 - Common Component Supply Formats

Components can reach a placement machine in several forms. The correct choice depends on package size and shape, component fragility, production volume, supplier packaging, and the equipment available on the line.

| Supply format | What it looks like | Common use | What must remain controlled |
|---|---|---|---|
| **tape-and-reel** | components sit in repeated pockets in a continuous carrier tape covered by a peelable top tape | very common for chip resistors/capacitors, small ICs, and many other SMT packages | tape width, pocket pitch, pocket geometry, component orientation, cover-tape removal |
| **matrix tray** | components sit individually in a grid of pockets | larger, fine-pitch, fragile, or odd-shaped packages; BGAs/QFPs and other packages may be supplied this way | tray orientation, pocket coordinates, component orientation, tray flatness and support |
| **tube / stick** | components form a row inside a shaped tube or stick | compatible ICs, connectors, and other packages that can slide in a controlled profile | tube profile, part orientation, smooth movement, prevention of jams |
| **bulk / vibratory supply** | loose components are stored together and a feeder orients them before pickup | selected robust components that can tolerate bulk handling | part orientation, separation, damage prevention, reliable presentation |

These are common possibilities, not fixed package rules. The same package family may be available in more than one supply format. For example, a BGA or QFN may arrive in tape-and-reel or in a tray depending on the package, quantity, supplier, and manufacturing plan.

## 4.10.3 - Tape-and-Reel: The Most Common High-Volume Supply Format

A tape-and-reel package contains a continuous **carrier tape** wound on a reel. Each component sits in a pocket. A **cover tape** keeps the part inside the pocket during shipping and handling. Regular sprocket holes along the carrier tape allow a feeder to advance the tape by a controlled distance.

Two dimensions are especially useful to recognize:

- **tape width, $W$** - the overall width of the carrier tape;
- **component pitch, $P_1$** - the center-to-center distance between successive component pockets.

These are different dimensions. An **8 mm tape** does not mean that each component is spaced 8 mm apart.

For a representative small-component feeder, **8 mm carrier tape** is common. Current feeder examples use **2 mm or 4 mm feed pitch** with 8 mm tape. Larger packages use wider carrier tape and may use larger pocket spacing; nominal tape widths such as **12, 16, 24, 32, 44, and 56 mm** are also encountered, and some placement platforms support wider formats. These are recognition-scale examples, not a universal compatibility list. The actual tape width and pitch must match the component packaging and feeder.

### Simple indexing example

Suppose an 8 mm carrier tape holds a component every **4 mm** along the tape. If the feeder presents one component per placement cycle, the next pocket center must advance approximately 4 mm to the pickup position after each successful pick.

After 10 indexed pockets, the tape has advanced approximately:

$$
10(4\text{ mm}) = 40\text{ mm}
$$

The calculation is simple, but the physical idea is important: **repeatable pickup depends on repeatable indexing**. A feeder that advances to the wrong position can cause off-center pickup, missed pickup, or an attempt to pick from an empty region between pockets.

---

## 4.10.4 - What a Tape Feeder Actually Does

A tape feeder converts a reel of packaged parts into a repeated pickup sequence. A typical cycle is:

1. The carrier tape enters the feeder.
2. The feeder advances the tape by the required pitch.
3. The cover tape is peeled away from the component pocket.
4. The next component is exposed at a known pickup position.
5. The placement head moves a nozzle to that position.
6. Vacuum is applied and the component is picked up.
7. The feeder advances the next pocket for a later cycle.

The exact mechanism varies. Modern feeders may use electric drives and software-controlled pickup-position correction; older or simpler designs may use different mechanical arrangements. The important requirement is not one feeder architecture. It is **repeatable component presentation**.

Common feeder sensing or status functions can include:

- component-presence detection;
- tape-index or pitch monitoring;
- cover-tape status;
- tape-end or low-reel warning;
- tape-tension or break detection;
- feeder identification and pickup-position correction on some systems.

Not every feeder has every sensor. A sensor also does not guarantee a correct pickup if the wrong tape, wrong feeder setup, damaged pocket, or incorrect pickup position is present.

## 4.10.5 - How Non-Tape Formats Establish a Pickup Position

A placement machine does not always receive parts from indexed carrier tape. Trays, tubes/sticks, and bulk systems establish the next pickup position in different ways. The common requirement is still the same: **one correctly oriented component must arrive at a predictable location without damage**.

### Matrix trays

A matrix tray holds components in a regular grid. The machine or tray handler uses a defined tray origin and pocket spacing, then steps through the occupied pocket locations.

Useful checks include:

- correct tray orientation;
- correct row/column or pocket definition;
- component orientation inside the pockets;
- tray support and flatness;
- no empty pocket where a component is expected.

A tray does not automatically mean a large component, and a large component does not automatically require a tray. The deciding issue is whether the selected supply method protects and presents the actual part reliably.

### Tubes and sticks

A tube or stick constrains a row of components in a shaped channel. Gravity, vibration, or another controlled feed mechanism moves the next component to a stop or presentation point.

Useful checks include:

- tube cross-section matches the component;
- all parts face the intended direction;
- the tube is not bowed, damaged, or blocked;
- the next component moves freely into presentation position;
- the component cannot rotate into an unintended pickup attitude.

### Bulk and vibratory feeders

Bulk supply removes individual pockets or trays. A vibratory or other orientation mechanism must separate the parts, establish the required attitude, and present one component at a time. This works only for parts that can tolerate the handling and can be oriented reliably; it is not a universal low-cost substitute for tape, trays, or tubes.

## 4.10.6 - The Pickup Location Is a Physical Reference

The placement head cannot pick a component from an approximate area. It needs a known pickup position.

For a tape feeder, the pickup position is related to:

- feeder mounting location;
- carrier-tape width;
- pocket pitch and index position;
- pocket geometry;
- cover-tape peel condition;
- the programmed or learned pickup coordinates.

For a tray, the pickup position is related to the tray origin and pocket grid. For a tube/stick feeder, it is related to the component stop or presentation point.

A modern machine may measure pickup offset and automatically apply a correction. That capability improves repeatability, but it does not make physical setup unimportant. Large or changing pickup errors are still evidence that the feeder, tape, pocket, tray, or presentation mechanism should be checked.

> **Practical distinction:** The feeder is responsible for presenting the part where the machine expects to find it. Vision can correct normal pickup variation, but it should not be used to hide an unstable presentation mechanism.

---

## 4.10.7 - Vacuum Nozzles: The Interface Between Machine and Component

Most SMT placement heads use a **vacuum nozzle** to hold the component. The nozzle contacts a suitable surface on the package, vacuum is applied, and the pressure difference creates a holding force while the head accelerates, rotates, and transports the part.

The best nozzle is not simply the largest nozzle that fits inside the package outline. It must match the **available pickup surface** and the mechanical behavior of the component. In first-order terms, holding capability increases with useful sealed area and pressure difference, but real pickup also depends on leakage, component mass, acceleration, nozzle condition, and surface geometry.

A compatible nozzle should provide:

- enough contact area for stable holding;
- a good vacuum seal on the available package surface;
- a pickup point that does not interfere with leads, solder balls, openings, or fragile features;
- adequate support against tilt or rotation;
- clearance from neighboring package features;
- a geometry that the machine can center and inspect reliably.

Common nozzle-tip forms include round, oval, rectangular, and component-specific shapes. Odd-shaped or difficult components may require a custom nozzle or, on some machines, a mechanical gripper.

Nozzle condition also matters. Contamination, a chipped tip, a blocked vacuum path, or a bent/damaged nozzle can produce unstable pickup even when the selected nozzle type is nominally correct.

## 4.10.8 - Vacuum Pickup Is Verified, Not Assumed

Placement equipment commonly monitors the pickup condition using vacuum pressure, airflow, optical sensing, or a combination of methods. The machine may check whether:

- a component was present before pickup;
- vacuum changed as expected after pickup;
- the component remained on the nozzle during transport;
- the component was released after placement.

The exact vacuum level or sensing threshold is **machine-, nozzle-, and component-dependent**. A small nozzle with a tiny opening, an uneven pickup surface, and a large custom nozzle do not produce the same flow and pressure behavior. Some systems monitor pressure, some use flow-related information, and others combine sensing methods. Therefore, one universal kPa value is not a useful acceptance rule for all placement machines.

A successful vacuum reading also does not prove that the component is centered correctly. That is why placement systems combine pickup sensing with component recognition and centering, which will be developed in Sections 4.11 and 4.12.

## 4.10.9 - Matching the Supply Method and Pickup Tool to the Component

The component package creates physical constraints for both the feeder and the nozzle.

| Component example | Common supply possibilities | Pickup / presentation concern |
|---|---|---|
| **small chip resistor or capacitor** | commonly tape-and-reel | small pocket and short pitch require accurate indexing; nozzle must contact the small top surface consistently |
| **QFN / small leadless IC** | tape-and-reel or tray depending on package and quantity | pocket orientation matters; nozzle should contact the body without depending on exposed terminals |
| **BGA / CSP** | tape-and-reel or tray | protect solder balls; use a stable top-side pickup surface and correct orientation |
| **QFP / leaded IC** | tape-and-reel, tray, or other controlled format | leads are fragile; feeder/tray must preserve orientation and nozzle should contact the package body, not the leads |
| **connector or odd-shaped part** | tray, tube/stick, tape, or special presentation depending on design | center of mass, flat pickup area, height, and irregular geometry may require a special nozzle or gripper |

The table shows common possibilities, not mandatory packaging rules. Supplier packaging, machine capability, product quantity, and the validated manufacturing process determine the actual choice.

A useful engineering question is:

> **Where can the machine hold this part without losing it, damaging it, or confusing its orientation?**

That question links the component drawing, supply format, feeder, pickup point, nozzle, and later vision setup.

---

## 4.10.10 - What Can Go Wrong and What Should You Check First?

A placement problem should not automatically be blamed on the machine's X/Y accuracy. Many apparent placement problems begin at component presentation or pickup.

| Observation | Useful first checks | Avoid this premature conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| **repeated miss-pick from one tape feeder** | component present, correct pocket/index position, cover-tape peel, feeder pickup position, damaged pocket | the placement head has lost global accuracy |
| **component is picked but drops during travel** | nozzle condition, seal/contact area, vacuum path, component pickup surface, part damage | more vacuum is always the correct fix |
| **component is consistently skewed on the nozzle** | pickup position, pocket freedom, nozzle centering/contact area, component orientation in supply | the PCB coordinates are wrong |
| **tray pickup misses one row or column** | tray origin, pocket pitch, tray orientation, pocket occupancy, tray support | every component definition is wrong |
| **tube/stick feed stops** | jam, tube bow/damage, component orientation, gravity/vibration feed condition | the nozzle is the root cause |

These are deliberately **presentation-side first checks**. Section 4.13 develops placement troubleshooting more fully. The goal here is to recognize whether the evidence points first to **component supply, feeder presentation, or pickup-tool compatibility** rather than immediately blaming global machine accuracy.

---

## 4.10.11 - First-Setup Component-Presentation Checklist

Before relying on the placement program, a beginning technologist should be able to verify the physical supply side:

1. **Correct component and supply format?** Is the intended physical part being supplied in the expected reel, tray, tube/stick, or bulk presentation?
2. **Correct feeder or presentation device?** Is it compatible with the tape width/pitch, tray, tube profile, or bulk system being used?
3. **Correct orientation?** Are pin-1/polarity and other orientation-sensitive features presented consistently?
4. **Repeatable pickup location?** Does the next component arrive where the machine expects it?
5. **Cover tape / pocket / presentation path acceptable?** Is anything blocking, dragging, lifting, or damaging the component before pickup?
6. **Correct nozzle or pickup tool?** Does it contact a suitable surface without interfering with fragile or functional package features?
7. **Nozzle condition acceptable?** Is the tip clean, undamaged, and free of blockage?
8. **Pickup verified?** Does the machine confirm component presence and successful retention during a test pick or first-board run?

Detailed barcode interlocking, material traceability, line-side replenishment, and formal job-package verification are treated later in the chapter. Detailed placement optimization also belongs beyond this introductory section.

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- component **supply format**, **feeder**, and **nozzle** are different parts of the placement system;
- tape-and-reel is common, but trays, tubes/sticks, and selected bulk systems are also used;
- a tape feeder must advance the correct pocket, remove the cover tape, and present the component at a repeatable pickup location;
- tape width and component pitch are different dimensions;
- tray, tube/stick, and bulk systems establish the pickup position in different ways;
- a vacuum nozzle must match the component's usable pickup surface, not merely its overall package outline;
- pickup sensing verifies that a part is being held, but the exact vacuum threshold is machine- and component-dependent;
- repeated pickup errors should lead to checks of the supply format, feeder presentation, pickup location, nozzle, and component condition before assuming a general machine-positioning problem.

Section 4.11 follows the component into the placement machine and explains the basic **pick -> inspect/center -> move -> rotate -> place** sequence.
