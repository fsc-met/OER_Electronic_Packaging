# Figure 4.14.3 Suggested Content - Different Thermal Responses on the Same PCB During Reflow

## Status

**Suggested-content specification - Rev1 QA-reviewed candidate**

This file defines the instructional content and figure-generation constraints for Figure 4.14.3 in Section 4.14, **Reflow Oven and Thermal Profile Basics**.

The figure has one instructional role: show **why two locations on the same PCB can follow different temperature histories during the same reflow-oven pass**.

## Instructional Purpose

Help first-time Engineering Technology readers connect **physical thermal mass and local board construction** to the temperature-versus-time behavior introduced in Figure 4.14.2.

The figure should answer:

> **Why can a small component and a large/copper-coupled region on the same PCB experience different temperature histories even though they pass through the same oven recipe?**

The figure should prepare readers for the key practical idea that a reflow process must satisfy the thermal needs of the **whole assembly**, not just one convenient location.

---

## Central Teaching Message

> **One oven recipe does not produce one identical temperature curve everywhere on the PCB. Low-thermal-mass locations respond quickly, while high-thermal-mass or copper-coupled locations can lag during heating and retain heat longer during cooling.**

Supporting messages:

- component mass and package materials influence local thermal response;
- copper distribution, internal planes, PCB thickness/construction, component density, and airflow exposure also influence local response;
- the "cold" location may struggle to receive enough reflow exposure while a "hot" or fast-response location approaches its allowable temperature limit;
- the curves shown are conceptual thermal responses, not measured data from a specific board;
- the figure must not introduce thermocouple hardware or measurement-location procedure; Section 4.15 owns that workflow.

## Overall Layout

Use a **two-part teaching composition**:

### Left side - One PCB, two thermal locations

Show one simplified populated PCB with two clearly marked conceptual locations:

- **Location A - low thermal mass**
- **Location B - high thermal mass / copper-coupled**

Recommended examples:

- Location A: a small chip resistor/capacitor near a relatively light copper region;
- Location B: a larger connector, metal-shielded component, power package, or larger package associated with a broad copper plane/thermal-spreading region.

### Right side - Two conceptual product-temperature curves

Show two labeled temperature-versus-time curves for the **same oven pass**:

- `Location A - faster response`
- `Location B - slower / lagging response`

Both curves should follow the same broad reflow sequence but differ in timing and peak response.

### Bottom or top bridging message

Use one concise statement connecting the panels:

`Same board • same oven recipe • different local thermal response`

Optional small note:

`Conceptual curves; not to scale`

## Required Objects and Labels

### Required PCB objects

- one generic populated PCB;
- several small passive components;
- one clearly larger/high-mass component or connector;
- one visible broad copper-rich region or conceptual internal-plane cue associated with Location B;
- optional local shield/large exposed-metal feature if used to reinforce mass;
- board outline and a few traces/pads to make it clearly a PCB assembly.

Required labels:

- `Location A`
- `Low thermal mass`
- `Small passive`
- `Location B`
- `High thermal mass / copper-coupled`

If a broad copper region is shown, label it concisely as:

- `Large copper area / plane influence`

Do not imply that copper always makes a location hotter. The teaching point is that copper changes local heat spreading and effective thermal response.

### Required curve objects

- temperature vertical axis;
- time horizontal axis;
- two smooth curves with different line styles as well as different colors;
- direct labels on or near each curve;
- one clear heating-lag indication;
- one clear cooling-retention indication.

Required curve labels:

- `A - faster response`
- `B - slower response`

Optional process markers:

- `Heating`
- `Peak / reflow region`
- `Cooling`

Keep these secondary. Figure 4.14.2 already teaches the detailed profile regions.

### Required conceptual callouts

Use concise arrows/notes equivalent to:

- `heats faster`
- `lags during heating`
- `retains heat longer during cooling`

A short outcome note may say:

`Process must heat the cold location enough without overheating the hottest sensitive location.`

Keep it compact.

## Required Technical Relationships

1. Both locations must belong to the **same PCB** and the **same oven pass**.
2. Location A must represent a physically plausible lower-thermal-mass / faster-response region.
3. Location B must represent a physically plausible higher-thermal-mass and/or copper-coupled slower-response region.
4. During the heating portion, curve A should rise faster than curve B.
5. Near peak, curve A may reach its peak earlier and/or slightly higher than curve B in the conceptual example.
6. During cooling, the high-thermal-mass curve B should fall more slowly and may remain warmer after curve A has cooled substantially.
7. If the curves cross during cooling, the crossing must be smooth and physically plausible; do not make the crossing the central teaching point.
8. The curves must not be labeled as universal measured values. Use `conceptual` or `not to scale` wording.
9. The figure must not imply that component mass is the **only** cause of thermal difference. Copper distribution, board construction, component density, shielding, and local convection should be acknowledged in a concise note or spec-driven label.
10. Do not imply that a large copper plane always increases or always decreases peak temperature; it changes local heat spreading/effective thermal response and can contribute to lag depending on the complete assembly.
11. Do not show thermocouples, tape, soldered sensors, a profiler, data logger, or measurement-location procedure; those belong to Section 4.15.
12. Do not show formal process-window limits, acceptance bands, or pass/fail scoring.
13. Do not diagnose specific solder defects from the curves; Section 4.16 owns integrated defect troubleshooting.
14. Do not duplicate the full liquidus/TAL annotation from Figure 4.14.2. A faint liquidus reference may be included only if necessary to show why one location can have less above-liquidus exposure, but it should remain secondary.

## Spatial / Geometry Requirements

### PCB panel

- Keep the PCB in a simple top or restrained isometric view.
- Place Location A and Location B far enough apart that their callout leaders do not cross.
- The small passive at A must be visibly smaller than the high-mass object at B.
- If a copper-rich region is shown, use a hatched/shaded board area or simplified plane cue rather than exposing unrealistic solid copper on the surface.
- Keep board features generic; no vendor/product likeness.
- Do not place a thermal sensor icon at either location.

### Curve panel

- Use the same time axis for both curves.
- Use one common temperature axis.
- Keep curve A and B distinguishable by both line style and color.
- Label curves directly to reduce legend lookup.
- Avoid exaggerated differences that suggest one location is hundreds of degrees different from the other.
- Show a modest, instructional separation consistent with thermal lag.
- Keep callout arrows away from curve intersections.
- Maintain approximately **6-8% outer margin**.

### Cross-panel relationship

- Use matching `A` and `B` markers on the PCB and curve panel.
- Match marker shape/line style as well as color so the relationship works in grayscale.
- A light connector arrow from each board marker to its curve label may be used if it does not clutter the figure.

## Technical-Verification Basis

### Current Section 4.14 basis

Section 4.14 Rev3 explains that one assembly is thermally nonuniform because of differences in:

- component size and mass;
- package material and exposed metal;
- local copper area and internal planes;
- PCB thickness and local construction;
- component density and shielding of airflow;
- top-versus-bottom heating balance;
- local convection conditions.

The section's applied example contrasts small 0402 passives with a large metal-shielded connector and a power package coupled to a large copper plane.

### Approved outline basis

The approved Section 4.14 outline explicitly requires **board/component thermal differences** as part of the basic reflow-profile introduction, before Section 4.15 introduces measurement hardware and location selection.

### Primary instructional basis

MET406 Chapter 4 reflow material states that:

- thermal gradients across the PCB should be minimized;
- uneven heating can contribute to board/process problems;
- airflow uniformity affects local hot/cold spots and TAL;
- the product thermal profile depends on controlled heating and cooling;
- large/thin boards and different component/board structures respond differently during heating.

The current OER keeps that teaching purpose but avoids overclaiming one universal curve difference.

### Approved reference hierarchy

Technical relationships were checked against:

- Clyde F. Coombs Jr., *Printed Circuits Handbook*, which emphasizes balancing sufficient reflow of larger/high-thermal-mass locations against thermal damage to smaller devices or the substrate;
- Glenn R. Blackwell, *The Electronic Packaging Handbook*, which states that the reflow profile depends on the mix/layout of low- and high-thermal-mass components and that the product profile is not equal to the oven's setpoints;
- Rao R. Tummala, *Fundamentals of Microsystems Packaging*, for board-assembly/reflow thermal-profile context.

### Current equipment/process verification

Current production forced-convection oven information confirms that controlled convection and multiple heating/cooling zones are used to manage product thermal uniformity. No current manufacturer source is used to prescribe the exact conceptual curve separation in this figure; the two curves are intentionally **qualitative and product-dependent**.
