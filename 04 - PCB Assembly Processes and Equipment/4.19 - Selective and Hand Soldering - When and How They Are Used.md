# 4.19 - Selective and Hand Soldering: When and How They Are Used

Section 4.18 showed how wave soldering handles many through-hole joints with one shared conveyor process. That approach is efficient, but not every board can expose a large part of its underside to a solder wave.

Two localized alternatives are important:

- **selective soldering**, which uses programmed equipment to apply flux, heat, and a small solder wave only where needed;
- **hand soldering**, which uses a temperature-controlled iron and solder wire at individual joints.

The practical question is not simply which method is "better." It is:

> **How much of the board should be exposed to solder and heat, how repeatable must the process be, and how much access is available around the joint?**

---

## 4.19.1 - Why Localized Soldering Methods Are Needed

Wave soldering is a strong choice when many THT joints can be exposed to one common bottom-side process. Selective or hand soldering becomes more attractive when only a limited number of joints need soldering or when other board areas should not be exposed to the full wave.

Localized soldering is especially useful when:

- bottom-side SMT components near the THT joints;
- only a few THT connectors, terminals, transformers, or odd-form parts;
- joints with different thermal masses;
- heat-sensitive nearby components;
- high-mix or lower-volume production;
- prototypes, modifications, or repair work.

Selective and hand soldering both localize the process, but they do so differently.

| Method | How solder reaches the joint | Best fit | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wave soldering** | board passes across a full-width or broad solder wave | many compatible THT joints | large board area is exposed to one global process |
| **Selective soldering** | programmed mini-wave/nozzle reaches selected joints or groups | mixed SMT/THT boards and repeatable local production | nozzle access, programming, and cycle time |
| **Hand soldering** | operator heats one joint with an iron and feeds solder wire | prototypes, low quantity, special joints, repair/modification | operator skill, repeatability, and local heat control |

Section 4.20 will use these differences to plan mixed-technology assembly routes. Here the focus is what the two localized methods physically do.

---

## 4.19.2 - Selective Soldering Is a Programmed Local Process

**Selective soldering** uses a small controlled solder source to solder specific THT joints without passing the entire board underside across a broad solder wave.

A typical sequence is:

> **localized flux -> preheat -> mini-wave contact -> programmed withdrawal/drainage**

Depending on the machine, the PCB may move over the nozzle, the solder pot/nozzle may move under the PCB, or both motions may be coordinated.

Compared with wave soldering:

- solder contact is local rather than board-wide;
- flux can be deposited only near the intended joints;
- contact time and motion can be changed for different joints or groups;
- nearby bottom-side SMT can often remain outside the solder-contact region;
- throughput is usually lower because the machine must visit selected locations or programmed joint groups.

Selective soldering does **not** mean that tooling disappears. It often avoids a broad masking pallet used to protect large bottom-side areas during wave soldering, but the PCB still needs repeatable support/alignment and a clear nozzle-access opening.

---

## 4.19.3 - Recognizing the Selective-Soldering Hardware and Sequence

A selective-soldering system commonly includes:

| Hardware / function | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Board support / fixture** | holds the PCB flat and repeatable while leaving target joints accessible |
| **Flux applicator** | deposits controlled flux at selected THT locations; drop-jet and small spray methods are common |
| **Preheater** | prepares the flux and adds heat to the assembly before solder contact |
| **Motion system** | positions the PCB, nozzle, or solder pot in X-Y-Z as required |
| **Solder pot and pump** | supplies molten solder to the local nozzle |
| **Mini-wave nozzle** | creates a small solder fountain for one joint or a local group |
| **Vision / fiducial alignment** | corrects board-position offsets on machines that use camera alignment |
| **Nitrogen around the nozzle** | commonly used on many systems to reduce oxidation and support a stable solder surface |

Preheat may be broad, zoned, or localized depending on the machine. The key point is that **solder contact is selective even when the preheat system treats a larger part of the board**.

## 4.19.4 - The Nozzle Must Be Able to Reach the Joint

Selective soldering depends first on **physical access**.

The nozzle and its solder fountain need enough room to approach the target without contacting:

- nearby SMT components;
- tall THT bodies or leads;
- pallet walls or support hardware;
- board-edge hardware;
- other features that block the approach or exit path.

This creates a practical **keep-out area** around each programmed solder location.

Nozzle selection also affects access and heat transfer. Small round nozzles can reach isolated pins, while larger round, oval, slot, or custom nozzles can solder groups of pins more efficiently.

**About 3-8 mm** is a useful recognition scale for small round selective-soldering nozzles. Current commercial systems can provide both smaller and larger interchangeable nozzles, so treat this range as a **representative scale**, not a design limit.

A useful engineering check is:

> **Can the nozzle reach the joint, establish controlled solder contact, and leave the joint without colliding with nearby hardware or dragging solder into another feature?**

## 4.19.5 - Selective-Soldering Parameters Are Local and Coupled

Selective soldering allows parameters to change by joint or by joint group. That flexibility is useful, but it also means that one setting should not be adjusted without considering the others.

Important controls include:

- flux amount and deposition location;
- product preheat;
- nozzle size and shape;
- solder-pot temperature;
- pump speed / wave height;
- X-Y location;
- Z position or penetration into the mini-wave;
- dwell or travel time;
- approach speed;
- withdrawal speed and exit path.

Representative process scales are useful for developing judgment.

| Parameter | Representative introductory scale | Important qualification |
|---|---|---|
| **Small round nozzle** | about **3-8 mm** diameter | equipment may offer smaller or larger interchangeable nozzles |
| **Lead-free solder-pot temperature** | about **260-280°C** | alloy, nozzle size, joint thermal mass, flux, and machine strongly affect the approved value |
| **Dwell/contact time** | about **1-3 s** for many small-joint examples | heavy copper, thick boards, nozzle size, and process method can require different times |
| **Nozzle / board Z relationship** | a **small controlled Z offset / immersion**; instructional examples may be on the order of 1-2 mm | use the machine/nozzle coordinate system and validated recipe rather than a universal gap number |

These are **starting scales**, not recipes.

Small nozzles contain less moving solder and can cool rapidly when they contact a high-thermal-mass joint. Some selective processes therefore use solder-pot temperatures higher than ordinary wave-solder settings so the local joint can receive enough heat within the available contact time.

A better rule is:

> **Verify the actual joint and product response. Do not assume that a higher temperature, longer dwell, or higher wave is automatically better.**

---

## 4.19.6 - Programmed Withdrawal Controls Drainage

Selective soldering still has the same physical problem seen in wave soldering: liquid solder must separate cleanly from the joint.

A selective machine can control separation by changing:

- vertical withdrawal speed;
- X-Y motion during exit;
- drag direction;
- pump speed / wave height near the end of the joint;
- nozzle size;
- dwell time.

The programmed path may be as simple as:

> **approach -> contact/dwell -> withdraw**

or it may include a small lateral movement that makes solder peel away in a preferred direction.

The exact path is product- and machine-dependent. The purpose of the programmed exit is to control:

- where the liquid solder neck breaks;
- how much solder remains;
- whether adjacent pins bridge;
- whether an icicle or solder tail forms.

---

## 4.19.7 - Common Selective-Soldering Problems and First Checks

Many selective-soldering problems look similar to wave-soldering problems, but the local program adds new evidence to check.

| Observation | First evidence to check |
|---|---|
| **Insufficient hole fill / weak wetting** | flux location/amount, product preheat, solderability, nozzle size, dwell, wave height, thermal mass |
| **Bridge between nearby pins** | nozzle width, wave height, exit path, withdrawal speed, lead spacing, flux/oxidation condition |
| **Icicle / solder tail** | withdrawal speed/path, dwell, solder volume, nozzle condition, oxidation |
| **Skipped joint** | X-Y alignment, fiducial correction, flux deposition, nozzle contact, board flatness |
| **Solder contacts nearby SMT** | keep-out/access, nozzle choice, Z position, board/fixture alignment |
| **Inconsistent result from board to board** | fixture seating, board warp, wave height, flux delivery, nozzle condition, program alignment |

Do not treat the defect name as the diagnosis. A bridge, for example, may involve solder volume, geometry, oxidation, or exit motion.

The practical troubleshooting sequence is:

1. confirm the exact joint and defect pattern;
2. check board seating, flatness, and alignment;
3. verify localized flux and preheat;
4. verify nozzle size, nozzle condition, location, wave stability, and Z relationship;
5. verify dwell/travel and withdrawal path;
6. change the smallest logical variable;
7. run another board and verify the result.

## 4.19.8 - Where Hand Soldering Fits

**Hand soldering** uses a heated tip to transfer heat into a joint while solder wire and flux provide the solder and surface preparation.

It remains important for:

- prototypes and laboratory builds;
- very low-volume production;
- engineering modifications;
- wires, terminals, connectors, or odd-form parts that are difficult to automate;
- touch-up or repair where the approved process allows it.

Hand soldering is flexible because the operator can work on one joint at a time, but that flexibility also creates variation. Joint quality depends on:

- tip condition and size;
- station temperature control;
- heat-transfer contact;
- flux condition;
- solder wire;
- joint thermal mass;
- operator timing and motion.

Detailed component removal, pad repair, and rework control belong later in the book. Here the goal is basic solder-joint formation with an iron.

---

## 4.19.9 - Recognizing the Basic Hand-Soldering Tools

A basic hand-soldering setup includes:

| Tool / material | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Temperature-controlled soldering station** | maintains and recovers the selected tip temperature |
| **Soldering tip** | transfers heat into the pad and lead |
| **Flux-cored solder wire** | supplies solder and internal flux to the heated joint |
| **Additional compatible flux** | improves surface preparation when the process requires it |
| **Tip cleaner** | removes residue without damaging the tip plating |
| **PCB holder / fixture** | keeps the work stable and reduces movement while the joint cools |
| **Tweezers / small hand tools** | position small parts when needed |

Tip shape matters.

A narrow conical tip can reach a tight location, but a small contact area transfers heat slowly. A chisel-shaped tip often transfers heat more efficiently to a THT pad or larger lead because its contact face can provide more thermal contact area.

> **Use a tip that fits the joint. Do not compensate for a tip that is too small by simply turning the temperature much higher.**

The working tip should remain clean and tinned. A thin solder coating improves thermal contact and helps protect the tip surface.

---

## 4.19.10 - Heat the Joint, Then Feed Solder to the Joint

For a basic THT hand-soldering joint:

1. clean and tin the tip;
2. place the tip so it contacts **both the pad and the component lead**;
3. allow the joint surfaces to heat;
4. feed solder wire to the heated joint near the tip;
5. let the solder melt and wet the pad, lead, and PTH region;
6. remove the solder wire;
7. remove the iron;
8. allow the joint to cool without movement.

A thin tinned solder film on the tip is useful because it improves thermal contact. However, the operator should not melt the working solder supply on the iron and **paint it onto a cold joint**.

The solder wire should melt because the **joint is hot enough**, not because the wire touches only the iron tip.

## 4.19.11 - Temperature and Time Are Process Variables, Not Goals

The station setpoint must support efficient heat transfer, but higher temperature is not a substitute for correct tip size and thermal contact.

Representative introductory **starting scales** are:

- **SnPb hand soldering:** about **320-350°C** tip setpoint;
- **lead-free hand soldering:** about **350-370°C** tip setpoint;
- **ordinary THT heating/contact:** often only a few seconds; **2-5 s** is a useful training scale.

These values are not universal setpoints or acceptance limits.

Current tool and solder-wire suppliers publish different recommendations because:

- active-tip stations can recover heat quickly and may solder successfully at lower setpoints;
- some flux-cored lead-free wires specify higher tip temperatures;
- large ground planes and heavy terminals remove heat much faster than small isolated pads;
- tip geometry changes the contact area and heat-transfer rate.

Use the **lowest practical temperature and shortest practical heating time that produce reliable wetting for the approved materials and joint**.

If a joint does not wet quickly, check:

- tip size and condition;
- whether the tip actually contacts both lead and pad;
- surface cleanliness and solderability;
- flux;
- joint thermal mass;
- station power and thermal recovery.

If the joint still does not wet, stop and check the process rather than continuing to heat it.

---

## 4.19.12 - Common Hand-Soldering Mistakes

| Poor practice | Why it causes trouble | Better practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Heating only the lead** | pad may remain too cold for wetting | contact pad and lead together |
| **Melting solder on the tip and painting the joint** | solder can melt before the joint surfaces are hot enough | heat the joint, then feed wire to the joint |
| **Using a tiny tip on a large joint** | poor heat transfer encourages long dwell or excessive setpoint | use a tip with adequate contact area |
| **Dirty or oxidized tip** | poor heat transfer and poor solder wetting on the tip | clean and re-tin the tip |
| **Too much solder** | hides geometry and can create bridges or excess solder | feed only the amount needed for the joint |
| **Holding heat too long** | increases risk of pad, laminate, component, and interface damage | remove heat after wetting is achieved |
| **Moving the joint while solder is liquid** | can disturb the solidifying connection | support the assembly and allow it to cool undisturbed |
| **Skipping needed flux** | oxide/contamination can prevent wetting | use the approved compatible flux when needed |

A solder joint should be evaluated by the required geometry and wetting condition, not by a simple "shiny = good" rule. Lead-free joints can have a different surface appearance from older SnPb joints.

Formal workmanship acceptance must follow the applicable product requirements and standards. This section is not a soldering-certification course.

---

## 4.19.13 - Choosing Between Selective and Hand Soldering

Selective and hand soldering both localize heat and solder, but they serve different production needs.

Use **selective soldering** when:

- the joint locations repeat from board to board;
- automated local soldering improves consistency;
- nearby bottom-side SMT makes broad wave exposure undesirable;
- multiple joints or connectors justify machine programming;
- nozzle access and keep-out are available.

Use **hand soldering** when:

- only a few joints are involved;
- the build is a prototype or very low quantity;
- an odd-form component or wire is difficult to automate;
- an approved modification or repair requires direct manual access.

Do not decide from volume alone. Joint access, thermal mass, nearby components, required repeatability, tooling/fixture needs, inspection access, and product requirements all matter.

---

## 4.19.14 - Section Takeaway

Selective soldering and hand soldering solve the same general problem—forming a solder joint only where it is needed—but at different levels of automation.

The most important practical points are:

- selective soldering uses **localized flux, preheat, a programmed mini-wave/nozzle, and controlled withdrawal**;
- selective soldering depends on nozzle access, fixture/alignment, keep-out space, and a validated program;
- nozzle size, pot temperature, wave height, Z position, dwell/travel, and withdrawal path interact;
- hand soldering uses a **temperature-controlled station, suitable tip, flux, solder wire, and correct joint heating**;
- the iron should transfer heat into both the pad and lead before solder wire is fed to the joint;
- representative temperature and time values are starting scales, not universal rules;
- too little heat gives poor wetting, while too much heat or time increases the risk of PCB/component damage;
- appearance is not a root-cause diagnosis, and formal workmanship acceptance requires the applicable requirements/standards.

Section 4.20 now uses wave, selective, reflow, and limited hand soldering as process options when planning a complete **mixed SMT/THT and double-sided assembly route**.

### Important primary-source corrections / qualifications

1. **Selective soldering is not always one joint at a time.**  
   A mini-wave can solder one joint, a connector row, or a local group using point, drag, slot, or multi-nozzle methods. Public text therefore uses `selected joints or groups`.

2. **Selective soldering does not literally eliminate all fixtures/pallets.**  
   It often avoids the broad wave-solder masking pallet used to shield bottom-side SMT, but it still needs board support/tooling and clear nozzle access.

3. **Preheat is not necessarily localized to one joint.**  
   Current selective systems can use broad IR/convection preheating even though flux and solder contact are selective.

4. **Nozzle-size ranges are machine-dependent.**  
   The primary 3-8 mm round-nozzle scale is retained as a recognition value. Current commercial equipment can provide a broader set; one current Pillarhouse platform lists roughly 2.5-12 mm standard nozzle tips and smaller optional micro-nozzles.

5. **Lead-free selective pot temperature is strongly process-dependent.**  
   The primary 260-280°C teaching scale is retained as representative. Current selective-solder literature and machine guidance show that small nozzles can require higher temperatures because the small solder mass loses heat rapidly when it contacts a high-thermal-mass joint. Public prose therefore does not turn 260-280°C into a required window.

6. **Dwell time is not universally 1-3 s.**  
   The primary range is retained as an introductory scale. Current ITW technical guidance describes local joint heating on a roughly 2-3 s scale for some small-nozzle work, while heavier boards/joints can require longer.

7. **Board-to-nozzle distance is machine/nozzle specific.**  
   The primary ~1-2 mm teaching item is reduced publicly to a millimeter-scale Z-offset/immersion concept rather than presented as a universal value.

8. **Nitrogen is common but not universal.**  
   Many selective systems inert the nozzle area to reduce oxidation. Public prose says `commonly used on many systems`, not `required for all selective soldering`.

9. **Hand-soldering temperature cannot be one universal number.**  
   The primary 320-350°C SnPb and 350-370°C lead-free scales are retained as representative introductory values. Current supplier recommendations differ substantially with station thermal recovery, tip geometry, wire/flux chemistry, and joint mass.

10. **The primary statement `solder should melt on the joint, not the iron tip` is clarified.**  
    A thin tinned solder film on the tip is desirable for thermal contact. The important rule is that solder wire should be fed to the heated joint rather than melting a large blob on the tip and painting it onto a cold joint.

11. **Detailed rework/desoldering is deferred.**  
    The primary material contains wick, vacuum, hot-air, and low-melt-alloy rework methods. Section 4.19 mentions repair/rework only as a hand-solder use case because detailed repair/rework belongs in Chapter 5.

- using the lowest practical tip temperature;
- 350°C or below for many lead-free joints on high-performance active-tip equipment;

- not feeding the wire directly to the soldering-iron tip;
- supplier-specific tip-temperature recommendations that differ between SnPb and lead-free alloys.
