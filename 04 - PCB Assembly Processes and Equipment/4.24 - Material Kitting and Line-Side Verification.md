# 4.24 - Material Kitting and Line-Side Verification

Section 4.23 established the controlled manufacturing job package. The next step is to make sure the **physical material at the line** matches that approved information.

A machine can have the correct program and still build the wrong assembly if the wrong reel is loaded, the correct part is assigned to the wrong feeder location, the wrong PCB revision reaches the line, or solder paste is issued with unknown status.

This is the purpose of **material kitting and line-side verification**.

> **Correct material, correct identity, correct condition, correct location, correct job.**

---

## 4.24.1 - A Manufacturing Kit Must Match the Active Job

A **manufacturing kit** is the set of physical materials prepared for a specific build or production stage. Depending on the product and factory, it may include:

- bare PCBs or panels;
- components supplied in reels, trays, tubes/sticks, or other approved formats;
- solder paste and other process materials;
- labels, travelers, or electronic records used to identify the job and material status.

Kitting is not only a parts-collection task. Before material enters the process, the factory needs to establish five things:

| Verification dimension | Practical question |
|---|---|
| **identity** | Is this the exact approved board, component, or process material? |
| **quantity** | Is enough usable material available for the planned build? |
| **condition / status** | Is the material still within its required handling, storage, moisture, or use condition? |
| **location** | Is the material assigned to the correct feeder, station, or job location? |
| **job** | Does the material belong to the active product and revision? |

A correct part in the wrong feeder, a PCB at the wrong revision, or solder paste with unknown controlled-use status are all material-control problems.

---

## 4.24.2 - Keep the Part-Identity Chain Clear

Three identities are especially important for electronic components.

### Internal part number

The **internal part number** is the organization's controlled identifier for the required item. For example:

`RES-10K-0603-1%`

It represents the requirement used by the product definition and manufacturing system.

### Manufacturer part number

The **manufacturer part number (MPN)** identifies the specific manufacturer's component. One internal part number may map to one or more approved MPNs when the released product information allows that choice.

A part that has the same resistance, capacitance, package size, or appearance is not automatically an approved substitute.

### Physical container identity

The actual component reaches production in a physical container such as a reel, tray, tube/stick, bag, or box. Its label may include:

- manufacturer and MPN;
- internal part number where applied by the factory;
- quantity;
- lot/batch or date code;
- barcode or 2D code;
- moisture or handling information where applicable.

The key relationship is:

> **approved job requirement -> approved physical part -> labeled container -> correct line-side location**

### Barcode and 2D-code verification

Factories may use barcode or 2D-code scanning to reduce manual identification errors. Where this capability is used, the setup can compare the scanned container identity with the active job, approved part information, feeder ID, and feeder location. The exact data fields and interlocks are factory- and equipment-dependent.

The important limitation is:

> **A scan verifies identity against controlled data; it does not create approval.**

If the database, label, alternate-part authorization, or feeder map is wrong, an automated scan can still confirm bad information. Scanning supports configuration control; it does not replace it.

## 4.24.3 - Quantity Verification Is Part of Production Readiness

For a component used several times on each assembly, the basic placement quantity is:

$$
N_{\text{required}} = N_{\text{build}}\,n_{\text{per assembly}}
$$

where:

- $N_{\text{required}}$ = minimum number of successful component placements required;
- $N_{\text{build}}$ = number of assemblies in the planned build;
- $n_{\text{per assembly}}$ = number of that component used on each assembly.

### Example 4.24.1 - Required Placement Quantity

A controller board uses four identical 0603 resistors. The planned build is 120 boards.

$$
N_{\text{required}} = 120 \times 4 = 480
$$

At least **480 successful placements** are required.

The quantity issued to the line may need to be greater than 480 because of feeder setup, leader/trailer requirements, machine rejects, sampling, or an approved attrition allowance. That extra allowance is **process- and company-dependent** and should not be treated as one universal percentage.

> **Practical rule:** Calculate the build requirement first. Apply any approved setup or attrition allowance separately so the reason for the extra quantity remains visible.

---

## 4.24.4 - Feeder Location Is Part of Material Identity

Section 4.10 showed that reels, trays, and tubes are presentation formats and that a feeder or presentation mechanism brings each part to a known pickup location. At line setup, material control adds another requirement: the correct physical material must be associated with the feeder location expected by the active job.

### Example 4.24.2 - Material and Feeder Verification

Consider an illustrative placement setup. The active job requires:

- internal part number: `RES-10K-0603-1%`;
- approved MPNs: `MPN-A` or `MPN-B`;
- assigned feeder location: `F12`.

The operator scans a reel labeled `MPN-B` and loads it into feeder location `F12`. The controlled part mapping confirms that `MPN-B` is an approved source for the required internal part number. The labels `F12`, `MPN-A`, and `MPN-B` are illustrative; actual factories use their own feeder identifiers and part-number structures.

This setup is consistent.

Now suppose the same approved reel is loaded into `F13`, while a different approved component is loaded into `F12`.

Both reels may be valid materials for the product, but the setup is wrong because the **part-to-feeder relationship no longer matches the active machine job**.

The appropriate action is to hold the affected setup, correct the feeder/material assignment, and verify the corrected mapping before normal placement begins.

> **A correct reel in the wrong feeder location is still the wrong setup.**

---

## 4.24.5 - Lot, Date Code, and Alternate-Part Status Must Be Understood

Component labels commonly include a **lot code**, **batch code**, **date code**, or similar production identifier. These identifiers can support:

- material segregation;
- customer or product restrictions;
- problem investigation;
- controlled records and traceability;
- identification of suspect material when required.

A date code by itself is not a universal expiration limit. Usability depends on the applicable component requirements, storage history, condition/solderability, customer restrictions, and the organization's controlled rules.

The same principle applies to alternate parts. If the required component is unavailable, a visually similar or electrically similar part should not be substituted simply because it fits the footprint.

Differences can include:

- tolerance or rating;
- temperature coefficient;
- termination finish;
- package dimensions;
- qualification status;
- customer or sourcing restrictions.

> **Production shortage is not engineering authorization. Use only an approved alternate or an authorized change.**

---

## 4.24.6 - Material Condition Matters as Much as Part Number

A container can have the correct part number and still be unsuitable for immediate use if its required condition or handling status is unknown.

Three material groups deserve special attention.

### Solder paste

Section 4.6 showed that solder paste is a product-specific process material. At line issue, verify as applicable:

- paste product/type;
- solder alloy and flux system;
- lot/batch;
- expiration or use-by status where specified;
- required storage condition;
- conditioning/use status after removal from controlled storage;
- container status.

Different solder-paste products can have different storage and working-life requirements. Use the current product data sheet and approved work instruction rather than a generic shelf-life rule.

### Moisture-sensitive components

Section 4.5 developed moisture-sensitive-device handling in detail. At the kitting level, the required decision is simpler: verify that the applicable moisture/reflow-sensitive status is known before the material is issued to a reflow process.

Depending on the controlled process, useful evidence can include:

- moisture-sensitivity classification or label;
- sealed/dry-pack condition;
- exposure-status record after opening;
- dry-storage or bake status when required.

Do not recalculate floor life from memory at the line. Use the controlled exposure record and applicable handling procedure.

### Bare PCBs

Bare PCBs are also controlled material. Verify as applicable:

- PCB part number and revision, from the applicable marking/label/controlled record;
- fabrication lot/batch where required;
- panel configuration;
- quantity;
- packaging/storage condition;
- cleanliness and obvious physical damage;
- moisture/storage status when controlled by the board and process requirements.

Printed boards can be degraded by contamination, physical damage, solderability loss, or moisture uptake. Their handling and storage controls depend on the board construction, finish, packaging, storage history, and applicable requirements.

> **Do not assign the component MSL/floor-life table to a bare PCB or invent one universal board bake schedule. Follow the applicable printed-board handling and storage requirement.**

---

## 4.24.7 - Verification Continues During Replenishment and Return-to-Stock

Material control does not end after the first feeder is loaded.

During a production run, the line may require:

- a new reel or tray;
- feeder replacement;
- tray change;
- a reel splice or replenishment;
- a new solder-paste container;
- removal of material when a job stops or changes.

Each event is another opportunity for the wrong material or unknown-status material to enter the process.

When unused or partially used material leaves the line, the information needed for the next manufacturing decision should remain with it, as applicable:

- material identity;
- remaining quantity;
- lot/date code;
- moisture/exposure status;
- expiration/use-by status;
- product or process restrictions.

If identity or required status is lost, the material should not quietly return to unrestricted stock. It should be held for the applicable verification or disposition process. Some process materials, including opened solder paste, may have product-specific return/reuse restrictions even when the label remains intact.

## 4.24.8 - A Practical Line-Side Verification Checklist

Before material is released into normal production, a technician or engineer should be able to answer the following questions from controlled information and physical evidence.

| Check | Question |
|---|---|
| **active job** | Is the correct product and revision active? |
| **bare PCB** | Are the correct PCB/panel part number, revision, and applicable lot present? |
| **component identity** | Does each reel/tray/tube map to the required internal part number and an approved MPN/alternate? |
| **quantity** | Is enough usable material available for the build and any approved setup/attrition allowance? |
| **lot/date code** | Does the material meet any applicable product/customer restriction? |
| **feeder assignment** | Is each component loaded into the feeder/location expected by the active job? |
| **barcode/2D check** | Where scanning is used, do the physical container, feeder, and job records agree? |
| **solder paste** | Is the correct paste product/alloy/flux system and lot present, with acceptable controlled-use status? |
| **moisture status** | Is the required moisture/exposure status known for applicable components? |
| **return control** | Can partial material leave the line with the identity/status needed for the approved return, reuse, or disposition decision? |

If one of these answers cannot be established, the correct action is to **hold the affected material or setup and resolve the mismatch before continuing**.

---

## 4.24.9 - Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- kitting is a manufacturing-control activity, not merely a warehouse task;
- internal part number, manufacturer part number, and physical container identity are related but different;
- material verification covers identity, quantity, condition/status, location, and the active job;
- barcode/2D scanning can reduce manual loading errors, but it verifies against controlled data rather than creating approval;
- the basic build quantity comes from the assembly quantity and the number of placements per assembly, while setup/attrition allowance is process dependent;
- a correct component in the wrong feeder location is still an incorrect setup;
- lot/date code supports traceability and restrictions but is not automatically an expiration limit;
- approved alternates must come from controlled product information;
- solder paste, moisture-sensitive components, and bare PCBs require status checks in addition to part-number checks;
- material identity/status must be preserved during replenishment and return-to-stock.

Section 4.25 brings the job package, materials, tooling, machine programs, feeder/nozzle setup, board orientation, and first-board checks together into a practical **changeover and production-readiness** workflow.

### Important technical clarifications

1. **Internal PN and MPN are not the same identifier.** One internal requirement may map to one or more approved physical manufacturer parts depending on the controlled product definition.
2. **A barcode/2D scan does not create engineering approval.** It only compares encoded identity with controlled data and rules.
3. **Feeder location is part of the active machine setup.** A valid component loaded into the wrong feeder location can produce a wrong assembly.
4. **Lot/date code is not a universal expiration rule.** Restrictions depend on product, customer, supplier, storage, and controlled manufacturing requirements.
5. **Quantity allowance is not universal.** The public calculation separates minimum successful placements from approved setup/attrition allowance.
6. **Solder-paste storage/use status is product specific.** Section 4.6 owns the detailed handling explanation.
7. **Moisture status is a condition check, not a re-teaching of MSL.** Section 4.5 owns floor-life and dry-handling details.
8. **Bare PCB moisture handling is not the same as component MSL control.** Current IPC-1602A explicitly treats printed-board handling/storage separately and does not define component-style MSL/floor life for boards.
9. **Return-to-stock requirements are process dependent.** The section teaches preservation of identity/status rather than a universal warehouse procedure.
