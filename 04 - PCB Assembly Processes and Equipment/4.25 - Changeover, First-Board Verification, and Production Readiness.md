# 4.25 - Changeover, First-Board Verification, and Production Readiness

Sections 4.23 and 4.24 established two parts of manufacturing readiness: the **controlled job package** and the **correct physical materials at the line**. A production changeover brings those pieces together with the machines, tooling, recipes, board orientation, and first-board evidence.

A changeover is not complete just because each machine powers up without an alarm. The line can be mechanically ready and still be configured for the wrong product, revision, board side, stencil, feeder arrangement, or thermal recipe.

> **A changeover is complete only when the line is configured for the same approved product and the first-board evidence confirms that setup before normal production is authorized.**

This section uses **first board** as a general manufacturing term for the first assembly, or the defined small initial set when the local process requires more than one unit, used to verify a new setup, product revision, or approved change. The exact company procedure, sample size, and terminology vary.

---

## 4.25.1 - A Changeover Is More Than Loading a New Program

A **changeover** prepares equipment and line-side resources for a different product, product revision, board side, process route, or approved setup. Some changeovers affect only part of a line. Others change nearly every station.

Typical changeover items include:

| Area | What may change | What can go wrong if the previous setup remains |
|---|---|---|
| **product / configuration** | job ID, product revision, assembly side, traveler/route | the line runs the wrong configuration |
| **PCB handling** | board/panel, conveyor width, stops, clamps, support tooling, orientation | the wrong board or side enters the process, or the board is handled incorrectly |
| **printing** | stencil, solder paste, squeegee/tooling, printer program/recipe | the printed deposit pattern or process does not match the new board |
| **placement** | placement program, feeder map, reels/trays/tubes, nozzles, board support, fiducial references | wrong/missing/misoriented components or repeated pickup/placement problems |
| **reflow** | oven recipe, conveyor speed, zone setpoints, atmosphere where applicable | the board experiences the wrong thermal process |
| **verification** | first-board checks and applicable inspection program/plan | the setup is not checked against the correct product requirements |

Not every item changes on every product. The practical task is to identify **what is different for this job** and verify those changes without carrying old setup information into the new run.

Many factories use some form of previous-job removal or **line-clearance** check before the new job is released to the equipment. The exact procedure varies, but the purpose is simple: previous boards, materials, labels, tooling, or programs should be removed from the active setup or positively segregated so they cannot become part of the new job accidentally.

> **Changeover rule:** Do not ask only whether each machine is ready. Ask whether the entire line is ready for the **same product, revision, board side, materials, tooling, programs, and process route**.

---

## 4.25.2 - Start with One Product, Revision, Board, and Assembly Side

Before checking detailed machine settings, establish the active configuration.

Verify from controlled information:

- product or assembly part number;
- released product revision;
- PCB/panel part number and revision;
- intended assembly side or process pass;
- required process route;
- applicable approved changes, deviations, or special instructions.

Then compare the controlled information with the physical board at the line.

A board can be the correct part number but still be wrong for the current operation if:

- the revision is wrong;
- the top/bottom side is confused;
- the panel enters the conveyor in the wrong orientation;
- the board is from the wrong fabrication lot when a controlled restriction applies;
- the support tooling or board stop is set for a different panel geometry.

The detailed coordinate, origin, rotation, and fiducial concepts were developed in Section 4.12. At changeover, the important question is more practical:

> **Is the physical board entering the line in the orientation and side expected by the active job?**

Useful evidence can include board/panel markings, controlled labels, fiducial locations, tooling features, machine board maps, or barcode/2D identification where the factory uses them.

---

## 4.25.3 - Verify the Whole Line as One Configuration

A production line contains several machines that may each hold product-specific information. Those programs and recipes do not have to use the same revision label, but they must be traceably linked to the same approved product configuration.

A practical changeover review looks across the line rather than checking each station in isolation.

| Station / resource | Product-specific items to verify | Basic readiness evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **solder-paste printer** | correct stencil/tooling, paste material/status, board support, printer program/recipe | stencil and board agree; support/clamping and alignment are reasonable; qualified starting recipe is loaded |
| **SPI, when used** | correct inspection program / board orientation | expected pads/deposits are recognized and the program corresponds to the current product |
| **pick-and-place** | correct placement program, component sources, feeder locations, nozzles/tools, board support, fiducial references | job map, materials/feeders, tools, board handling, and vision references agree |
| **reflow oven** | correct product recipe, conveyor setup, applicable atmosphere/control settings | intended recipe is loaded, the oven reaches the required ready/stable condition, and the profile evidence is applicable to this product/pass |
| **downstream verification** | applicable first-board inspection/test readiness | required checks can be performed on the correct product configuration |

Sections 4.8, 4.13, and 4.15 already developed the detailed printer, placement, and reflow setup logic. Here the important idea is **configuration agreement across the line**.

A common mistake is to treat a familiar machine recipe as automatically valid for the new job. Similar-looking boards can differ in stencil design, component thermal mass, panel orientation, feeder mapping, or product-specific limits. Use the controlled job information and verified process evidence rather than similarity alone.

## 4.25.4 - Board Orientation and Assembly Side Are Setup Variables

Board orientation is easy to underestimate because a panel may physically fit into the conveyor in more than one direction.

Before the first board enters the line, confirm as applicable:

- **top or bottom side** being assembled;
- **panel leading edge** and conveyor direction;
- board/panel orientation expected by the printer and placement programs;
- fiducials or tooling features expected by the machine;
- underside components and keep-out areas that affect support pins or fixtures;
- orientation of polarized or keyed assemblies when a physical cue is used.

For a double-sided PCBA, the process pass matters. The second side may use a different stencil, placement program, support arrangement, and reflow condition from the first side.

> **A correct board loaded in the wrong orientation or for the wrong side is still the wrong setup.**

Do not correct an orientation mismatch by entering unexplained offsets or rotations simply to make the machine continue. Confirm that the physical board, job side, program convention, and tooling all agree.

---

## 4.25.5 - First Print and SPI Provide the Earliest Process Evidence

The first printed board is the earliest opportunity to confirm that the new printing setup is producing the intended paste pattern.

Before components are placed, verify the print using the applicable method. Depending on the process, evidence can include:

- deposits appear on the intended pads;
- no obvious missing, smeared, bridged, or severely offset paste pattern;
- printer alignment and board support appear stable;
- the correct stencil and paste are actually in use;
- **SPI**, when used, measures the expected deposit features against the product-specific inspection program.

Section 4.9 showed that 3D SPI can report measurements such as paste **volume, height, area, and X/Y offset**. The limits are product- and process-specific. Do not memorize one percentage or offset tolerance as a universal first-board requirement.

If the first print is abnormal, the best time to react is **before placement**. Hold the affected board/setup and check the evidence related to the observed pattern: stencil identity/condition, board support, registration, paste condition, printer recipe, or cleaning state as appropriate.

> **First-print rule:** Catch a printing setup problem while the board still contains only paste, not after components have been placed and reflowed.

One good first print does not prove long-term process stability. It confirms that the startup evidence is reasonable enough for the job to proceed to the next controlled stage.

---

## 4.25.6 - First Placement Check Should Occur Before Reflow

After the first board is placed, but before soldering permanently joins the components to the PCB, inspect the placement evidence required by the local process.

A basic first-placement check can ask:

- Are key components **present**?
- Are polarized or orientation-sensitive components facing the correct direction?
- Are components reasonably centered on the intended paste/pads?
- Do many components show the same board-level shift or rotation?
- Are there wrong-part, pickup, drop, or feeder alarms in the machine history?
- Does the physical board look consistent with the assembly drawing and placement map?

Section 4.13 developed the detailed feeder/nozzle/setup troubleshooting chain. Section 4.25 uses those checks as a **release gate**.

If the placement evidence is abnormal, hold the board before reflow when practical. A component can still be repositioned or the setup can be corrected under the approved local procedure more easily than after the joint has been soldered.

> **First-placement rule:** Verify identity, orientation, presence, and gross position before heat makes the assembly harder to correct.

---

## 4.25.7 - Reflow Readiness Requires Both the Recipe and Applicable Profile Evidence

The reflow oven has a **recipe**: zone setpoints, conveyor speed, airflow or related settings, and atmosphere controls where applicable. The PCBA experiences an actual **thermal profile** that depends on the product as well as the machine settings.

These are related but not identical.

| Evidence | What it tells you | What it does **not** prove by itself |
|---|---|---|
| **oven recipe** | which commanded settings the oven will use | that the actual product temperatures meet the required process window |
| **measured profile** | how selected locations on a representative assembly respond through time and temperature | that every future board will remain correct without appropriate process control |
| **post-reflow inspection** | whether the assembled board shows the required visible/available physical evidence after soldering | why a defect occurred or whether every hidden joint is acceptable |

Before the first board is reflowed, confirm that the intended recipe is loaded, the oven has reached the required ready/stable condition defined by the equipment or work instruction, and the **profile-verification evidence is applicable** to the product, board side/pass, paste/alloy, oven, and approved setup.

A new or significantly changed product, different oven, changed board/component thermal mass, major recipe change, or other defined trigger may require profiling or re-profiling according to the local process. Section 4.15 showed the measurement workflow:

> **measure -> compare -> adjust -> re-measure**

Do not assume that every first production board must carry thermocouples. Many factories establish or verify the profile with a representative profiling board and then use the approved recipe for production. The required profiling trigger and frequency are product- and company-specific.

After reflow, the first assembled board should receive the required first-board inspection or review before the broader run is released. Depending on the product and process, that startup evidence may include visual inspection, AOI, X-ray for hidden-joint awareness, or another defined check. If automated inspection is used, its product/program identity must also match the active job. Detailed workmanship, inspection-method selection, and acceptance criteria belong to Chapter 5.

## 4.25.8 - First-Board Verification Is a Gate, Not a Ceremony

The first board is useful because it connects the **controlled setup** to a **physical result**.

A practical integrated sequence is:

1. confirm the correct product/job and released revision;
2. verify the correct PCB/panel, side, and orientation;
3. verify components, solder paste, and other applicable materials;
4. verify stencil, fixtures, support tooling, and other product-specific tooling;
5. load and verify the applicable machine programs/recipes;
6. verify feeders, nozzles/tools, board handling, and alignment references;
7. print the first board and review visual/SPI evidence as applicable;
8. place the first board and review component identity/orientation/position evidence;
9. verify the applicable reflow recipe/profile evidence and review the post-reflow board as required;
10. release normal production only after the required checks pass and the authorized local procedure permits continuation.

This sequence is a **generic manufacturing-readiness logic**, not one manufacturer's operating procedure. A factory may combine steps, use more than one initial board, or require additional signoffs for high-reliability or regulated products. Passing the first-board gate confirms startup readiness; it does **not** by itself establish long-term process capability or statistical stability. Routine process control must continue after startup.

> **First-board rule:** Check each process at the earliest point where its setup error can be observed, and do not allow a failed gate to disappear into the next process step.

## 4.25.9 - Local Setup Check, First Board, and Production Release Are Different Scopes

Three ideas are related but should not be treated as synonyms.

| Level | Main question | Typical evidence at this chapter level |
|---|---|---|
| **local machine setup check** | Is this machine configured correctly for the active job? | correct program/recipe, tooling, board handling, material/tool assignment, basic machine recognition |
| **first-board / first-article awareness** | Does the initial physical build agree with the intended setup across the required process stages? | first print/SPI, first placement, applicable profile evidence, required initial inspection/review |
| **broader production release** | Has the organization received the required evidence and authorization to continue the planned production stage? | applicable setup checks, first-board evidence, required signoffs/hold-point closure, documentation status |

A local printer check can pass while the placement setup is wrong. A first board can look reasonable while the applicable documentation or process approval is still incomplete. Production release therefore depends on the **required evidence set**, not one machine's green status indicator.

Some organizations use the term **first article** informally for an initial unit or initial inspection. Other industries define formal First Article Inspection requirements. Do not assume that a local first-board verification is equivalent to a formal customer, regulatory, or aerospace FAI requirement.

> **Production readiness is broader than machine readiness.**

## 4.25.10 - Know the Conditions That Should Create a Hold

A startup or changeover should stop at the appropriate hold point when required evidence does not agree.

Examples include:

- product/revision or board identity cannot be confirmed;
- previous-job material or tooling remains mixed with the new setup;
- wrong or unapproved component/alternate is detected;
- feeder location or nozzle/tool does not match the active job;
- stencil/tooling identity is wrong or uncertain;
- board side/orientation does not match the program;
- printer/SPI evidence is abnormal;
- first placement shows wrong, missing, shifted, or misoriented components;
- reflow recipe identity or profile applicability cannot be established;
- first-board post-reflow evidence is abnormal;
- a required signoff, change authorization, or setup check is incomplete.

A useful reaction pattern is:

> **hold -> identify the mismatch -> correct the controlled input/setup -> repeat the affected verification -> repeat any downstream check invalidated by the change -> document/authorize -> continue**

Do not bypass an interlock, ignore a failed first-board check, or continue a run merely to see whether the next boards improve. If the status of an already processed board is uncertain, keep that board controlled until the applicable disposition process determines what happens next.

Detailed nonconformance disposition, MRB, CAPA, and quality-system reaction plans belong to Chapter 5.

## 4.25.11 - Applied Example: Every Machine Can Look Ready While the Line Is Not

A line is changing from `Controller Assembly Rev C` to `Controller Assembly Rev D`.

The team verifies:

- correct Rev D PCB panels;
- correct stencil and solder paste;
- correct printer program;
- correct placement program;
- correct feeders and approved materials;
- correct nozzles and board support.

The first print passes the required SPI review, and the first placement check looks correct.

Before reflow, the technologist notices that the oven is still displaying the recipe identifier used for the previous product, a thicker high-thermal-mass assembly.

The placement machine is ready. The printer is ready. The **line is not ready**.

The correct response is to hold the first board before reflow, verify the reflow recipe required by the active job, load the applicable approved recipe, and confirm that the required profile evidence applies. If the controlled procedure requires a profiling run because of the change, profile and verify before the production board is released through reflow.

If the first board had already been processed with the wrong or unverified recipe, hold that board for the applicable review/disposition process. A visually acceptable soldered board does not retroactively prove that the required thermal process was used.

Do not continue simply because the previous recipe produced acceptable-looking boards on another product.

This example illustrates the central idea of Section 4.25:

> **Production readiness is agreement across the complete process route, not a collection of independent machine starts.**

## 4.25.12 - Practical Startup / Changeover Checklist

Before normal production begins, an entry-level technologist should be able to work through a checklist similar to the following using the factory's controlled documents and procedures.

| Check group | Questions to answer |
|---|---|
| **job / revision** | Correct product? Released revision? Correct route/side/pass? Required change/deviation status closed or authorized? |
| **board** | Correct PCB/panel and revision? Correct orientation? Board handling/support appropriate? |
| **materials** | Correct components/approved alternates? Correct paste/process material? Required condition/status known? |
| **printing** | Correct stencil/tooling? Correct printer program/recipe? First print/SPI reasonable? |
| **placement** | Correct program? Feeders/locations/materials verified? Correct nozzles/tools? Fiducials/support reasonable? First placement reasonable? |
| **reflow** | Correct recipe? Oven at required ready/stable condition? Applicable profile evidence? Required atmosphere/setup ready? |
| **first-board result** | Required print, placement, reflow, and initial inspection evidence passed? |
| **reaction / release** | Any open mismatch or hold condition? Required authorization complete before normal production? |

The exact checklist may be electronic, paper based, built into machine software, or distributed among several controlled records. The format is less important than the evidence.

## 4.25.13 - Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- a product changeover is a **configuration change across the line**, not merely loading a new machine program;
- previous-job materials, tooling, boards, or programs must not carry into the new job accidentally;
- the product/revision, PCB, board side/orientation, materials, stencil/tooling, machine programs/recipes, feeders/nozzles, and process route must agree;
- the first print/SPI check catches printing setup problems before placement;
- the first placement check catches wrong, missing, misoriented, or grossly shifted components before reflow;
- the reflow recipe and the measured thermal profile are related but different forms of evidence;
- a first production board does not necessarily need thermocouples, but applicable profile verification must exist when required;
- local machine setup, first-board/first-article awareness, and broader production release are different scopes;
- an abnormal startup result should create a **hold -> correct -> reverify** loop rather than automatic continuation;
- normal production begins only after the required evidence is complete and the authorized local procedure allows release.

Section 4.26 uses these process, setup, material, and evidence concepts together in one **integrated factory-floor case** that asks the reader to plan a build, interpret problems, and choose the next reasonable check.

### Important technical clarifications

1. **First-board verification is not the same as process capability.** One board can reveal setup problems but does not establish long-term statistical stability.
2. **Machine program/recipe identifiers do not have to match the product revision label.** They must be controlled and traceably linked to the applicable configuration.
3. **SPI limits are product/process specific.** Do not reuse the lecture's fixed tolerance examples as universal startup gates.
4. **A reflow recipe is not the same as the product thermal profile.** Oven setpoints and conveyor speed are inputs; the measured product temperature history is the verification evidence.
5. **The first production board does not necessarily carry thermocouples.** Profiling can use a representative profiling board according to the qualified/local process.
6. **Post-reflow inspection is included only at awareness level.** Detailed workmanship/acceptance method selection and criteria belong to Chapter 5.
7. **First article is context dependent.** A local initial-board check is not automatically a formal AS9102-style FAI.
8. **Production release is organization dependent.** The book teaches the evidence relationships, not a universal signoff hierarchy.
