# 4.7 - Stencils and Paste Printing Basics

Section 4.6 introduced solder paste as a material that must flow during printing, recover after printing, and remain stable long enough for component placement. The next question is physical:

> **How does the assembly process put a controlled amount of solder paste onto thousands of PCB pads in the correct locations?**

For most SMT production, the answer is **stencil printing**.

A useful first model is:

> **supported PCB + aligned stencil + solder paste + squeegee motion -> filled apertures -> stencil separation -> paste deposits on PCB pads**

This section develops the physical printing concept. Detailed printer hardware, setup variables, recipes, and adjustment strategy are introduced in Section 4.8. Quantitative inspection of the printed deposits is introduced in Section 4.9.

---

## 4.7.1 - What Stencil Printing Does

A **stencil** is a thin sheet or foil containing precision openings called **apertures**. In SMT assembly, each aperture is positioned over a PCB land or pad where solder paste is required.

Modern SMT stencils are commonly metal foils. Stainless steel is widely used, while nickel-based and other specialized stencil constructions are also available for particular applications.

During a basic print cycle:

1. the PCB is positioned and supported beneath the stencil;
2. the stencil and PCB pattern are aligned;
3. the stencil is brought into the required printing position against the board;
4. a bead of solder paste is placed on the stencil surface;
5. a **squeegee** moves across the stencil and drives paste into the apertures;
6. the stencil separates from the PCB;
7. controlled paste deposits remain on the PCB pads.

The squeegee is therefore not painting paste directly onto every pad. It is moving a common bead of paste across a **patterned metering tool**. The stencil decides where openings exist, and the aperture geometry strongly influences how much paste can be deposited.

## 4.7.2 - Apertures, PCB Pads, and Board Support Work Together

An **aperture** is the opening cut through the stencil foil. The aperture is located relative to a PCB **land**, commonly called a **pad**, so paste is deposited where the component termination will later be placed.

The aperture does not have to be exactly the same size and shape as the pad.

Depending on the component and process, an aperture may be:

- approximately one-to-one with the pad;
- reduced to limit solder volume or bridging risk;
- enlarged where additional paste is required;
- reshaped or segmented to control how paste is distributed.

The important beginner-level idea is:

> **The PCB pad defines where the solder joint belongs; the stencil aperture meters how paste is delivered to that location.**

Aperture design is therefore part of process planning rather than a simple copy of the copper pattern.

### Board support and gasketing

The PCB must also be supported so it stays flat and stable during the print stroke.

Good support helps the board contact the stencil uniformly. This close local contact is often described as **gasketing**. If the board bows away from the stencil, paste can spread under the stencil or the deposit volume can vary from one area of the board to another.

Board support may use pins, tooling, vacuum, dedicated fixtures, or other equipment-specific methods. Section 4.8 develops the machine setup details. At this point, remember the physical purpose:

> **Support the PCB so the stencil can seal consistently against the printing surface without distorting the board.**

## 4.7.3 - Stencil Thickness and Aperture Geometry Set the Nominal Paste Volume

For a simple rectangular aperture, the theoretical aperture volume is approximately:

$$
V_{\text{aperture}} = LWT
$$

where:

- $L$ = aperture length;
- $W$ = aperture width;
- $T$ = stencil thickness.

This gives an important physical relationship:

- larger aperture area -> more available paste volume;
- thicker stencil -> more available paste volume;
- smaller aperture area -> less available paste volume;
- thinner stencil -> less available paste volume.

Many standard SMT stencil processes use foil thicknesses in the general range of about **0.10-0.15 mm (4-6 mil)**. This is a useful scale for recognition, not a universal design rule. Very small features may require thinner stencils, while larger components or mixed paste-volume requirements may use thicker or stepped regions.

A stencil thickness of 0.10 mm is about 100 µm. That means the stencil itself can be only a few times thicker than the Type 3-Type 5 solder particles introduced in Section 4.6.

The theoretical aperture volume is not automatically the actual volume left on the PCB. Some paste may remain on the aperture walls, the paste may deform after release, and the print process itself introduces variation.

## 4.7.4 - Area Ratio Helps Predict Whether Paste Will Release Cleanly

After the aperture is filled, the stencil must separate from the PCB and leave the paste behind.

The paste is pulled in two competing directions:

- adhesion to the PCB pad helps keep the deposit on the board;
- interaction with the stencil aperture walls tends to keep paste inside the aperture.

A useful geometric measure is the **area ratio**:

> **area ratio = aperture opening area / aperture wall area**

For a rectangular aperture:

$$
A_R = \frac{LW}{2T(L+W)}
$$

where $L$, $W$, and $T$ are the aperture length, width, and stencil thickness.

A higher area ratio means the opening area is large compared with the wall area. In general, that gives the paste a more favorable geometry for release.

A lower area ratio means the aperture walls are more dominant. Release becomes more difficult and more sensitive to factors such as:

- solder-paste powder size and formulation;
- aperture-wall smoothness;
- stencil coating or fabrication method;
- separation behavior;
- stencil cleanliness.

### Simple area-ratio example

Consider a square aperture:

- $L = 0.30\text{ mm}$;
- $W = 0.30\text{ mm}$;
- $T = 0.10\text{ mm}$.

Then:

$$
A_R = \frac{(0.30)(0.30)}{2(0.10)(0.30+0.30)}
= \frac{0.09}{0.12}
= 0.75
$$

The area ratio is dimensionless.

For many conventional stencil-printing processes, an area ratio around **0.66 or higher** has long been used as a practical starting guideline for reliable paste release. Treat this as a **process-design guideline, not a universal pass/fail law**. Modern paste formulations, finer powders, improved stencil-wall finishes, coatings, and optimized processes can sometimes print successfully below that value.

## 4.7.5 - Filling the Aperture Is Only Half of the Printing Process

A good print requires two physical events:

1. **aperture filling** - paste enters and fills the intended opening;
2. **aperture release** - paste detaches from the stencil walls and remains on the PCB pad during separation.

A print can fail even when the aperture initially fills.

For example, if paste stays attached to the sidewalls during separation, the deposit may become:

- too small;
- irregular in height;
- torn or distorted;
- missing on part of the pad.

This is why smooth, clean aperture walls matter. Residue, damage, or contamination can change the release behavior even when the nominal stencil dimensions have not changed.

At this stage, do not try to correct release problems by randomly changing printer settings. First identify whether the evidence points to:

- an aperture-geometry limitation;
- contamination or clogging;
- poor board support/contact;
- paste condition;
- alignment;
- or a machine/process setting that should be investigated in Section 4.8.

## 4.7.6 - Recognize a Good Print Before Adjusting the Machine

Before reflow, the paste deposit itself provides useful manufacturing evidence.

A healthy print should generally look:

- centered on the intended pad;
- separated from neighboring deposits;
- reasonably well defined rather than smeared;
- complete rather than missing a large portion of the pad;
- repeatable from pad to pad for similar features.

Three common abnormal conditions are easy to recognize at this level.

| Print condition | What you may observe before placement | Basic concern | First checks |
|---|---|---|---|
| **too little / incomplete deposit** | small, broken, irregular, or missing paste | not enough solder may be available for the intended joint | clogged aperture, incomplete fill, poor release, paste condition |
| **too much / paste connecting pads** | oversized deposit, smear, or paste between neighboring pads | bridging or solder-ball risk after reflow | aperture/stencil selection, stencil contact, paste condition |
| **misaligned deposit** | paste shifted off-center from the pad | component terminals may not meet the intended paste geometry; bridging risk can increase | stencil-to-board alignment, fiducial recognition, board position/support |

The table gives **first checks**, not final root causes. Detailed printer variables belong in Section 4.8, and measured print quality belongs in Section 4.9.

## 4.7.7 - Stencil Cleanliness Is Part of Print Repeatability

A stencil can be dimensionally correct and still print poorly if paste residue builds up.

Two locations matter:

- **inside the apertures** - dried or accumulated paste can reduce the effective opening and cause incomplete deposits;
- **on the underside of the stencil** - transferred paste can smear onto the PCB and create unwanted deposits or bridging risk.

A clean stencil therefore supports both aperture filling and good gasketing.

> **Practical rule:** When print quality changes unexpectedly, inspect the stencil condition before assuming that a machine parameter must be changed.

The exact cleaning method and cleaning frequency depend on the stencil, paste, equipment, and qualified process. Those controls are developed with the printer setup in Section 4.8.

## 4.7.8 - A Practical First-Board Check

After the first board is printed, a beginning technologist should be able to perform a simple physical check before deeper process adjustment.

Ask:

1. **Correct stencil?** Does the stencil match the product and side of the PCB being built?
2. **Board supported?** Is the PCB stable and making the expected contact with the stencil?
3. **Alignment reasonable?** Are deposits centered on their intended pads?
4. **Deposits complete?** Are similar pads receiving similar-looking deposits?
5. **No obvious excess or bridging?** Are neighboring deposits clearly separated where they should be?
6. **Stencil clean?** Are apertures and the underside free of visible buildup that can disturb the next print?
7. **Paste condition acceptable?** Does the material still match the controlled condition discussed in Section 4.6?

If one of these checks fails, preserve the evidence and investigate the likely physical cause before changing multiple settings at once.

That habit prepares the reader for the machine-level logic used in the next section:

> **observe the deposit -> identify the likely physical mechanism -> check the related setup/control -> adjust only with evidence**

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- a stencil is a thin patterned foil that meters solder paste onto PCB pads through **apertures**;
- the squeegee moves paste across the stencil so the apertures fill, after which stencil separation leaves deposits on the PCB;
- board support helps keep the PCB flat and promotes consistent stencil-to-board contact or **gasketing**;
- aperture size, shape, and stencil thickness determine the theoretical paste volume available at each location;
- many standard SMT stencils are roughly **0.10-0.15 mm (4-6 mil)** thick, although actual thickness is product- and process-dependent;
- actual deposited volume can differ from theoretical aperture volume because paste must release from the aperture walls;
- **area ratio** compares aperture opening area with aperture wall area and is a useful predictor of release difficulty;
- an area ratio near **0.66 or higher** remains a useful traditional starting guideline for many conventional processes, but it is not a universal pass/fail boundary;
- a good print is centered, separated, complete, and repeatable enough for the intended feature;
- insufficient, excessive, smeared, or misaligned paste should trigger physical checks before random machine adjustment;
- stencil cleanliness is part of process repeatability.

Section 4.8 next moves from the physical printing concept to the **solder-paste printer itself**: its major hardware, setup sequence, and the few process controls a beginning technologist should understand.
