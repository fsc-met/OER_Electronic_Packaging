# 4.8 - Solder-Paste Printer - Hardware, Setup, and Basic Process Controls

Section 4.7 explained the physical stencil-printing process: the board is supported beneath the stencil, solder paste fills the apertures during the squeegee stroke, and the stencil separates to leave paste deposits on the PCB pads.

A production solder-paste printer turns that physical process into a controlled machine cycle.

A useful beginner-level model is:

> **transport and locate the PCB -> support and clamp it -> align board and stencil -> print with a controlled squeegee stroke -> separate -> clean when required -> inspect the result**

The goal of this section is not to teach every printer menu or optimization method. It is to identify the major machine systems, understand the few controls that most directly affect the print, and develop a disciplined first-board setup habit.

---

## 4.8.1 - From a Stencil to a Production Printer

A modern automatic stencil printer is more than a frame holding a stencil.

It combines several systems that must work together:

- **conveyor or board-handling system** to bring the PCB into the machine and transfer it out;
- **board support** to keep the PCB flat during the print stroke;
- **clamping** to locate and restrain the board without distorting it;
- **vision/alignment system** to locate fiducials and register the PCB pattern to the stencil pattern;
- **stencil frame and clamping system** to hold the stencil securely;
- **squeegee head** to move one blade across the stencil with controlled speed and force;
- **solder-paste bead** in front of the active squeegee;
- **separation motion** that parts the PCB and stencil after printing;
- **understencil cleaning system** to remove residue from the stencil underside and apertures when required.

Machines differ in mechanical design, software, automation level, and optional features, but these functions are common enough to form a useful recognition model.

The important idea is that each machine system supports one physical requirement. If the board moves, bows, misaligns, or loses good stencil contact, changing a squeegee setting alone may not solve the print problem.

## 4.8.2 - Board Handling, Support, and Clamping

The PCB normally enters the printer on a conveyor or rail system. The machine then stops and locates the board in the print position.

The printer must support the PCB from below so the board remains stable while the squeegee applies force through the stencil. Support may use:

- support pins;
- tooling blocks or dedicated fixtures;
- vacuum support;
- flexible or automatic support systems.

The exact hardware varies with the printer and product.

Clamping then restrains the board laterally and/or vertically. Good clamping should hold the PCB repeatably without introducing significant bow or distortion.

These two functions are related but not identical:

- **support** resists deflection under the print load;
- **clamping** controls board position and motion.

Poor support can produce uneven stencil-to-board contact. Excessive or poorly located clamping can distort the PCB. Either condition can cause deposit-volume variation or print-position errors.

> **Practical check:** Before changing print speed or pressure, verify that the board is actually supported and clamped the way the product recipe expects.

---

## 4.8.3 - Vision Alignment Registers the Stencil to the PCB

The stencil pattern must be registered to the PCB pads before printing.

Automatic printers commonly use a camera system to locate **fiducial marks** and compare their measured positions with the programmed product coordinates. The machine then applies the required registration correction before the print stroke.

At a beginner level, think of the alignment system as correcting three basic errors:

- X-position error;
- Y-position error;
- rotational error about the vertical axis.

Some machines can also compensate for additional board-scaling or stretch effects, but that is beyond the basic setup concept here.

Alignment problems can come from more than a camera setting. A dirty fiducial, wrong product program, incorrect stencil, poor board location, warped PCB, or unstable clamping can all produce evidence that looks like a registration problem.

This is why alignment should be checked as a **system**, not as one isolated software value.

---

## 4.8.4 - The Squeegee Head Creates the Print Stroke

During printing, one squeegee blade contacts the stencil and moves across it. The blade pushes a rolling bead of solder paste ahead of the contact line while the applied force keeps the blade wiping the stencil surface.

The important physical variables are:

- **squeegee speed** - how fast the blade travels across the stencil;
- **squeegee force / pressure setting** - the machine-controlled blade loading against the stencil; printer interfaces may display this as force, pressure, or a normalized load;
- **blade angle and condition** - part of the qualified hardware/setup that affects wiping and paste rolling;
- **paste bead size and condition** - enough paste must remain available across the printable width.

Many metal squeegee systems use blade angles in the general range of about **45-60°**. Within this common geometry, a steeper blade generally favors wiping action, while a shallower blade changes the paste-roll geometry and blade loading. Treat the angle as part of the qualified hardware/setup rather than a universal troubleshooting adjustment.

A solder-paste bead on the order of roughly **10-25 mm in diameter** appears in instructional and supplier guidance. A bead that becomes too small can starve the print stroke; an unnecessarily large bead can become less stable and increases the amount of paste exposed on the stencil. The actual qualified target depends on the paste, blade, printer, printable width, and paste-management system.

The goal is not "more pressure" or "slower is always better." The goal is a stable paste roll, complete aperture filling, and a clean stencil wipe without excessive wear, scooping, or board distortion.

## 4.8.5 - Separation Is a Controlled Part of the Print Cycle

After the print stroke, the PCB and stencil must separate.

This motion matters because the paste must leave the aperture walls and remain on the PCB pads. Section 4.7 introduced the geometric side of this problem through area ratio. The printer adds the motion side.

Depending on machine design, the board may move down, the stencil may move up, or both motions may contribute. The machine controls the initial separation so that paste release is repeatable.

There is no single separation speed that is correct for every stencil and paste. Current supplier data sheets can specify values such as **5-20 mm/s** for particular materials, while other printers and processes use slower controlled ranges. Small apertures can respond differently from large apertures.

Therefore:

> **Use the qualified product recipe as the starting point. Change separation only when the deposit evidence indicates a release problem and the related physical checks support that change.**

Poorly matched separation can produce irregular or incomplete deposits even when the aperture was initially filled. The useful setting is therefore the one verified by transfer and deposit evidence for the actual stencil/paste combination.

---

## 4.8.6 - Understencil Cleaning Maintains the Printing Interface

Paste residue can collect on the stencil underside or within apertures. If it remains there, the next board may see:

- weaker gasketing;
- smearing;
- blocked or partially blocked apertures;
- inconsistent paste transfer.

Automatic printers therefore commonly include an **understencil cleaning system**. Depending on the equipment and process, the cycle may use:

- a dry wipe;
- a solvent-assisted wipe;
- vacuum;
- a combination of these methods.

Cleaning frequency is a process setting, not a universal fixed number. Representative solder-paste data sheets often suggest beginning with a cleaning interval somewhere around **every 5-25 prints** and then adjusting the frequency based on board design, paste, stencil condition, observed print quality, and the qualified process.

Cleaning every cycle is not automatically better. It increases cycle time and, when solvent is used, adds another process condition that must remain controlled. The correct frequency is the one that keeps the stencil interface stable for the actual product.

## 4.8.7 - A Beginner-Friendly Setup Sequence

A practical beginner-friendly setup sequence is intentionally simple:

> **load product/stencil -> verify board support -> align -> establish basic recipe -> print first board -> inspect -> adjust only with evidence**

### Step 1 - Load the correct product and stencil

Verify:

- correct PCB side and product program;
- correct stencil and revision;
- correct squeegee/blade configuration;
- solder paste is the correct material and in controlled condition.

### Step 2 - Verify board support and clamping

Confirm that the board is supported under the printable area and is held repeatably without visible distortion.

### Step 3 - Align the PCB and stencil

Verify fiducial recognition and basic board-to-stencil registration. Do not ignore dirty or damaged fiducials.

### Step 4 - Establish the basic recipe

Use the qualified starting values for:

- squeegee speed;
- squeegee force/pressure;
- separation motion;
- support/clamping configuration;
- cleaning method and frequency.

Do not copy settings from another product merely because the PCB looks similar. A recipe belongs to a specific combination of board, stencil, paste, tooling, and qualified process.

### Step 5 - Print the first board

Watch the physical print cycle if possible. Look for a stable paste roll, a clean stencil wipe, board movement, unusual noise, or obvious setup mismatch.

### Step 6 - Inspect the print

Use the visual checks from Section 4.7 and the production inspection method available for the process. Quantitative SPI measurements are introduced in Section 4.9.

### Step 7 - Adjust only with evidence

If the result is abnormal, identify the likely physical mechanism first. Change one related item at a time when practical, then print and re-check.

## 4.8.8 - The Five Controls to Understand First

A beginning technologist does not need to optimize every printer parameter. Start with five controls that connect directly to the physical printing process.

| Control | Physical purpose | If the setting or condition is poor |
|---|---|---|
| **squeegee speed** | move the paste bead across the stencil while allowing aperture filling | an excessively high speed for the paste/stencil can reduce fill or produce an incomplete wipe; unnecessarily low speed can reduce throughput and may fall outside the qualified paste window |
| **squeegee force / pressure** | maintain controlled blade contact and obtain a clean top-side wipe while the paste roll fills apertures | too little can leave paste on the stencil surface or contribute to incomplete filling; too much can promote scooping, wear, stencil damage, or board distortion |
| **separation motion** | release the paste from aperture walls after the print stroke | a poorly matched speed/profile can distort deposits or reduce transfer, especially on small apertures |
| **board support / clamping** | keep the PCB flat, located, and stable during printing | poor support can weaken gasketing and vary deposit volume; poor or excessive clamping can shift or distort the board |
| **understencil cleaning frequency** | control residue, smearing, and aperture blockage | too infrequent can cause buildup and print variation; unnecessary cleaning adds cycle time and must itself be controlled |

The settings interact. For example, increasing pressure to compensate for poor board support may hide the original problem while introducing a second one.

> **Engineering habit:** Correct the physical cause first. Do not use one machine parameter to compensate blindly for an unrelated setup error.

## 4.8.9 - Representative Starting Scales

The values below are provided for engineering scale, not as a recipe for every printer.

| Item | Representative scale seen in current supplier/process guidance | How to use the number |
|---|---:|---|
| **print speed** | about **25-100 mm/s** for many conventional stencil-printing paste data sheets | recognize the order of magnitude; use the qualified paste/product setting |
| **squeegee force normalized by blade length** | about **0.018-0.027 kgf/mm**, equal to roughly **0.45-0.68 kgf per 25 mm** of blade length | use only as a starting scale; the clean-wipe condition and equipment calibration matter |
| **metal blade angle** | commonly about **45-60°** | usually treated as part of the qualified blade/setup configuration |
| **paste bead diameter** | roughly **10-25 mm** in representative instructional/supplier guidance | maintain enough paste for a stable roll across the printable width; follow the paste-management recipe |
| **controlled separation speed** | strongly process-dependent; some current paste data sheets use about **5-20 mm/s**, while some printer platforms provide controlled ranges near **1-6 mm/s** or lower | use the qualified recipe and deposit evidence; different machines and aperture sets can require different values |
| **understencil wipe interval** | examples commonly begin around **every 5-25 prints** before optimization | use print evidence to establish the actual interval for the product |

These values should not be copied from one line to another without verification. Paste formulation, stencil geometry, board design, blade length, machine mechanics, production rate, and environmental conditions all affect the qualified process.

## 4.8.10 - Match the Evidence to the First Machine Check

The printer should be adjusted from evidence, not from guesswork. A visible defect points to a set of related physical checks; it does not identify one guaranteed root cause.

| Evidence after printing | First machine/setup items to check | Do not conclude yet |
|---|---|---|
| **paste remains on top of stencil after stroke** | blade condition, squeegee force, speed, paste condition | do not immediately assume the aperture design is wrong |
| **low or incomplete deposits across one region** | local board support, gasketing, clogged apertures, paste bead, print stroke | do not immediately increase pressure everywhere |
| **irregular release on small apertures** | stencil cleanliness, area ratio, separation setting, paste condition | do not treat separation speed as the only possible cause |
| **smearing or paste under stencil** | support, clamping, stencil underside cleanliness, board/stencil contact | do not compensate only by changing print speed |
| **deposit pattern shifted from pads** | fiducial recognition, board position, clamping, correct stencil/program | do not treat the problem as a paste-rheology defect |

This table provides **first checks**, not root-cause certification. Section 4.9 next adds quantitative print evidence from solder paste inspection (SPI).

---

## 4.8.11 - First-Board / Changeover Checklist

Before releasing a setup into production, a beginning technologist should be able to answer:

1. **Correct product and stencil?**
2. **Correct paste and controlled paste condition?**
3. **Board supported and clamped without obvious distortion?**
4. **Fiducials recognized and alignment reasonable?**
5. **Squeegee blade installed correctly and in usable condition?**
6. **Paste bead present and rolling consistently across the print area?**
7. **Starting recipe loaded for this product?**
8. **Stencil underside and apertures acceptably clean?**
9. **First print centered, separated, and complete enough to proceed to the defined inspection step?**
10. **If abnormal, was one physical cause checked before changing multiple settings?**

This checklist is intentionally practical. It turns the machine from a collection of settings into a controlled manufacturing process.

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- an automatic solder-paste printer combines board transport, support/clamping, vision alignment, stencil holding, squeegee motion, separation, paste management, and understencil cleaning;
- board support and clamping must stabilize the PCB without creating distortion;
- vision systems use fiducials to register the PCB and stencil before printing;
- the squeegee must move a stable paste bead while wiping the stencil clean and filling the apertures;
- squeegee speed and force are important, but they should be adjusted from evidence rather than by rule of thumb alone;
- separation motion affects paste release and must be qualified for the stencil/paste combination;
- understencil cleaning frequency is product- and process-dependent;
- representative values such as **25-100 mm/s print speed** and normalized squeegee-force ranges provide engineering scale, not universal recipes;
- a disciplined setup follows **correct tooling -> support -> alignment -> qualified recipe -> first print -> inspection -> evidence-based adjustment**;
- do not change multiple parameters at once when the physical cause has not been identified.

Section 4.9 next focuses on the printed deposit itself: **height, area, volume, offset, defect patterns, and how SPI data becomes process evidence**.

### Numerical and unit review

Current public representative values are deliberately qualified.

Squeegee-force conversion used in the public table:

- 0.018 kgf/mm × 25 mm = 0.45 kgf per 25 mm;
- 0.027 kgf/mm × 25 mm = 0.675 kgf per 25 mm;
- rounded public scale: **0.45-0.68 kgf per 25 mm**.

Equivalent force is approximately 4.4-6.6 N per 25 mm using 1 kgf = 9.80665 N. The Newton conversion is not required in the public body because printer/paste documentation commonly presents this normalized setting in kg-based force units.
