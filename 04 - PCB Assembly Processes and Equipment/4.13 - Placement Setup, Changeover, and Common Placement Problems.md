# 4.13 - Placement Setup, Changeover, and Common Placement Problems

Sections 4.10 through 4.12 introduced component supply, feeders and nozzles, machine motion, placement data, vision, and fiducial alignment. In production, those pieces must agree before the first board is allowed to run normally.

A pick-and-place machine can execute the programmed motion correctly and still build the wrong assembly if the wrong job, reel, feeder location, nozzle, board support, or component orientation is loaded.

> **Placement setup is the process of making the physical machine match the intended production job.**

This section brings the placement concepts together at the machine level. The goal is not to teach one manufacturer's setup procedure. The goal is to recognize the evidence that should agree before production and to know what to check when placement does not look right.

---

## 4.13.1 - A Good Setup Makes Several Things Agree

A placement job is ready only when several parts of the setup agree:

- **job/program** - correct product, PCB revision, assembly side, placement data, and approved alternatives;
- **materials** - correct component part/reel/tray/tube and usable material status;
- **feeders** - correct source installed at the expected feeder or machine location;
- **nozzles/tools** - suitable pickup tool available, clean, undamaged, and recognized by the machine when applicable;
- **board handling** - correct conveyor width, stops/clamps, and support arrangement;
- **vision/alignment** - expected fiducials and component-recognition data work without abnormal results;
- **first-board evidence** - component identity, orientation, position, board alignment, and machine event history are reasonable before normal production continues.

Modern production equipment may use barcode, feeder-ID, reel-ID, nozzle-ID, or software interlocks to help verify these relationships. Those systems reduce setup error, but they do not prove that the underlying job/BOM/library data are correct; first-board evidence still matters.

## 4.13.2 - Practical Setup Check Before the First Board

The exact checklist is company- and machine-specific, but an entry-level technologist should recognize the following checks.

| Setup item | Evidence to verify | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Job / product** | product name or job ID, PCB revision, assembly side | prevents running the correct machine setup with the wrong program |
| **Component sources** | reel/tray/tube ID and approved part/alternate status | prevents wrong-component placement |
| **Feeder locations** | feeder ID/location matches the active job | sends the head to the intended source |
| **Nozzles / tools** | required tool is present, clean, undamaged, and suitable for the package | supports stable pickup and placement |
| **Board support** | board is clamped/supported without interfering with underside features | reduces board movement and local deflection |
| **Fiducials / board map** | expected board references are found and the graphical placement map looks reasonable | confirms the job coordinate system matches the actual PCB |
| **First-board condition** | key components are present, correctly oriented, and reasonably centered on paste/pads | catches setup errors before a long run |

For double-sided assemblies, support pins or fixtures must also avoid previously placed components on the underside. A support point that presses into an underside component can damage the assembly or distort the board.

A useful rule is:

> **Verify with job and machine evidence, not from memory or from where a feeder “usually goes.”**

## 4.13.3 - Replenishment and Changeover Are Different Events

A **replenishment** replaces or splices a component supply during the same production job. A **changeover** prepares the machine for a different product, PCB revision, assembly side, or setup.

### During replenishment

The major risk is introducing the wrong material into an otherwise correct setup.

Typical checks include:

- replacement material identity;
- approved alternate status, if applicable;
- reel or lot information required by the local traceability system;
- correct feeder;
- correct feeder location;
- tape/pocket orientation and feed condition;
- successful pickup after the refill;
- no abnormal material, feeder, or pick alarms after restart.

### During a product changeover

More parts of the setup may change:

- active job/program;
- feeder cart or feeder arrangement;
- reels/trays/tubes;
- nozzle set;
- board width and conveyor setup;
- support pins/fixtures;
- board side;
- fiducial references;
- first-board verification requirements.

Offline feeder preparation and software-assisted setup verification can reduce machine downtime, but the setup still needs to be verified before normal production.

> **A fast changeover is useful only if the new setup is correct.**

Detailed line-balancing, feeder-layout optimization, and product-family setup strategy are beyond this section.

## 4.13.4 - Accuracy and Repeatability Are Not the Same

Two words are often used when discussing placement quality:

- **placement accuracy** - how close the placed component is to the intended target;
- **placement repeatability** - how tightly repeated placements cluster under the same operating conditions.

A machine can be repeatable but inaccurate. For example, if repeated placements form a tight cluster about $40\ \mu\text{m}$ to the right of the target, the process is consistent but biased.

A process can also have an average close to the target but poor repeatability if individual placements are widely scattered around that target. Accuracy and repeatability must therefore be judged from appropriate measurement data, not from one part.

Several parts of the placement process can affect accuracy or repeatability:

- board alignment and board support;
- feeder indexing and component presentation;
- nozzle condition and vacuum stability;
- component-recognition quality;
- machine calibration and mechanical condition;
- component geometry or coplanarity;
- board warpage;
- placement-force/Z-axis condition.

As an engineering scale, current high-performance placement-machine specifications for small SMT components are commonly expressed in **tens of micrometers** under defined test conditions. The exact specification depends on machine, head, component, operating mode, measurement method, and statistical definition. It should not be treated as a universal acceptance limit for every product.

> **One good first-board placement does not prove repeatability. A tight cluster in the wrong place does not prove accuracy.**

## 4.13.5 - Rated Throughput Is Not the Same as Production Throughput

Placement-machine speed is commonly reported in **components per hour (CPH)**. A brochure may list a maximum, optimum, or standardized-test CPH under defined conditions.

Actual production throughput depends on the real job, including:

- component count and component mix;
- feeder locations and head travel;
- component-recognition time;
- nozzle/tool changes;
- large or odd-shaped components;
- board transfer and fiducial recognition;
- feeder replenishment;
- machine stops and recovery;
- how work is divided among machines in the line.

For a simple placement-only estimate,

$$
\text{board rate} \approx
\frac{\text{actual placement rate (CPH)}}{\text{placements per board}}
$$

Suppose a production program has **500 placements per board**. A rated speed of $45{,}000$ CPH would correspond to

$$
\frac{45{,}000}{500}=90\text{ boards/h}
$$

if every placement occurred continuously at that rated rate.

If the simulated or measured job rate is instead $30{,}000$ CPH,

$$
\frac{30{,}000}{500}=60\text{ boards/h}
$$

before accounting for additional line stops or upstream/downstream limitations.

This is an **illustrative placement-limited calculation**, not a production guarantee.

Modern placement software can simulate cycle time and optimize sequences or feeder assignments. At this level, the important lesson is simpler:

> **Use rated CPH to understand machine capability; use the actual product, setup, and production evidence to understand real output.**

## 4.13.6 - Common Placement Problems: Observation First, Then Evidence

A placement symptom usually has more than one possible cause. Do not change several settings at once just because a part looks wrong.

| Observation | Possible placement-stage sources | First evidence to check |
|---|---|---|
| **Pickup failure** | empty/mis-presented feeder pocket, pickup position error, unsuitable/worn/dirty nozzle, poor vacuum seal, pickup-height/setup issue | feeder presentation, component-present indication, vacuum/pick confirmation, nozzle condition |
| **Dropped component** | loss of vacuum after successful pickup, poor nozzle seal, unstable pickup surface, damaged/contaminated nozzle, abnormal transfer event | pickup confirmation followed by drop/vacuum event, nozzle/component contact area, machine event history |
| **Missing component on PCB** | pickup failure, dropped part, empty source, skipped/disabled placement, failed release/placement event | machine log/placement history, source status, pickup/place confirmation |
| **Wrong component** | wrong reel/tray/tube, wrong feeder mapping, wrong job or unauthorized alternate | material ID, active job/BOM, feeder/reel verification record |
| **Wrong polarity / orientation** | incorrect library rotation/orientation, wrong supply orientation, wrong program data, setup mismatch | first-board marking/pin-1 check, job/library orientation, source orientation |
| **Shifted or rotated component** | board alignment error, pickup offset/vision issue, feeder presentation, nozzle condition, board movement/warpage, unstable seating in paste | whether many parts share the same pattern, fiducial result, component-recognition offset, feeder/nozzle evidence |

The **pattern** matters.

- If many components are shifted or rotated in a similar way, investigate the board/job alignment chain before changing an individual feeder.
- If one component type shows repeated pickup problems, investigate that feeder, component presentation, nozzle, and recognition data.
- If one reference designator is wrong while other parts of the same type are correct, investigate that placement record or local setup.
- If the wrong component is loaded, a normal-looking package does not prove the electrical value is correct. Small resistors, capacitors, or other parts may look nearly identical, so use material/setup verification evidence rather than appearance alone.

## 4.13.7 - A Basic Placement Troubleshooting Pattern

For an entry-level placement problem, use a short evidence chain:

> **Observation -> scope -> machine evidence -> physical check -> one corrective action -> verify again**

### Step 1 - Describe the observation

Use specific language:

- missing;
- shifted in X/Y;
- rotated;
- wrong orientation;
- wrong component;
- pickup alarm;
- dropped component.

Avoid starting with a cause such as “bad feeder” before checking evidence.

### Step 2 - Determine the scope

Ask whether the problem affects:

- one reference designator;
- one component type;
- one feeder;
- one board;
- many components across the board;
- multiple boards over time.

Scope often tells you where to look first.

### Step 3 - Check machine and setup evidence

Depending on the machine, useful evidence may include:

- feeder/pick alarms;
- component-present or vacuum confirmation;
- vision offsets;
- fiducial recognition result;
- active job and feeder map;
- material/reel verification record;
- nozzle ID or nozzle-use status;
- pickup/placement images or event history when available.

### Step 4 - Make one justified correction

Examples include correcting a setup mismatch, cleaning or replacing an approved nozzle according to the local procedure, correcting feeder presentation, restoring board support, or correcting a verified job/library error.

Do not “tune around” the symptom by entering offsets when the actual cause is a wrong reel, damaged nozzle, poor board support, or bad fiducial recognition.

### Step 5 - Verify the result

Run the required first-board or verification check again. A correction is not complete until the evidence shows that the problem is gone and the rest of the placement job remains correct.

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- placement setup makes the job, materials, feeders, tools, board handling, vision references, and first-board result agree;
- replenishment and changeover both require component/setup verification;
- board support and nozzle condition are part of placement quality, not just mechanical housekeeping;
- accuracy is closeness to the intended target, while repeatability is consistency of repeated placements;
- machine-rated CPH does not equal actual production throughput;
- missing, wrong, shifted, rotated, dropped, and pickup-failure symptoms can have several placement-stage causes;
- troubleshooting should begin with the **pattern of the problem and available evidence**, not with random offset or parameter changes.

Section 4.14 follows the assembled board into the **reflow oven**, where the solder paste and placed components are heated through a controlled thermal profile.
