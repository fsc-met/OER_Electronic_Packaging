# 4.21 - Depanelization and Secondary Assembly

Section 4.20 planned the sequence used to complete the main SMT and THT soldering operations. After those operations, a PCBA may still require board separation and additional physical assembly before it can move downstream.

Two types of physical work often remain:

- **depanelization**, which separates individual PCBAs from a manufacturing panel;
- **secondary assembly**, which installs mechanical, thermal, identification, or special interconnection hardware that is better added outside the primary SMT/THT soldering flow.

The practical question is:

> **How do we finish the physical assembly without damaging the board, solder joints, components, or access needed for later operations?**

The exact order is product dependent. Some products are depanelized before secondary hardware is installed, while others remain in panel form longer for handling, inspection, test, or fixture reasons. The chapter order is therefore a teaching sequence, not a universal factory route.

---

## 4.21.1 - A Soldered Assembly May Still Be in Panel Form

Many PCBAs are assembled as part of a **panel** containing several individual board units. The panel can provide:

- stronger edge handling during printing, placement, reflow, or wave/selective soldering;
- common tooling rails and fiducials;
- more efficient movement through automated equipment;
- a convenient way to process several small boards together.

At some point, the individual board must be separated from the panel. That separation is **depanelization** or **singulation**.

Depanelization is not just a cutting task. By the time it occurs, the board may already contain brittle ceramic components, area-array packages, connectors, and completed solder joints. Excessive bending or twisting during separation can damage an assembly that looked acceptable before the board was removed from the panel.

> **The goal is not simply to separate the board. The goal is to separate it while keeping mechanical strain on the completed PCBA under control.**

---

## 4.21.2 - Common Depanelization Methods

Three methods are important to recognize at the factory-floor level.

| Method | What you would see | Main strength | Main concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| **V-score / V-groove** | straight grooves machined into the top and bottom surfaces, leaving a thin web of laminate | fast separation of straight board boundaries | breaking can bend the board if the assembly is poorly supported |
| **Routed tabs / mouse bites** | most of the outline is routed away while small tabs retain the board; some tabs contain a row of small holes | supports irregular board outlines and controlled break locations | tab removal can leave edge nubs and can still flex the board if twisted off |
| **Router depanelization** | a rotating cutter follows a programmed path or cuts retaining tabs while the board/panel is fixtured | controlled, low-strain separation when properly supported | requires cutter access, suitable fixturing, and control of cutting debris/vibration |

A product may use more than one method. For example, a panel can combine routed features with straight scored edges. Other low-stress separation technologies also exist, but this section stays with the V-score, routed-tab, and router methods required by the Chapter 4 outline.

## 4.21.3 - V-Score: Break Only Along the Intended Groove

A **V-score** is a shallow V-shaped groove machined into the top and bottom surfaces of a PCB panel. A thin section of laminate, sometimes called the **web**, remains between the grooves so the units stay together during earlier processing.

V-scoring is well suited to long, straight separation lines. The final separation occurs by breaking or cutting through the remaining web.

Important practical points are:

- the score line must be an intentionally designed break line;
- components, solder joints, and copper features near the edge need adequate clearance for the product and process;
- the assembly should be supported close to the score line;
- the board should not be bent through a large angle simply because the score can be snapped;
- repeated flexing should be avoided.

Manual break-off is therefore appropriate only where the panel was designed for it and the approved process controls the board strain. A scored panel that can be broken by hand is not automatically a license to use large bending angles; many products use dedicated V-cut separation tools or other controlled methods to reduce flex.

---

## 4.21.4 - Routed Tabs and Mouse Bites Keep Irregular Boards in the Panel

A router can remove most of the material around an individual PCB while leaving small **breakout tabs** that hold the board in the panel.

Some tabs include **mouse bites**: a row of small drilled holes that reduces the amount of material remaining in the tab and defines a more predictable break region.

This approach is useful when:

- the board outline is not a simple rectangle;
- connectors or features require routed contours;
- the manufacturing panel still needs enough strength to survive earlier assembly operations.

After assembly, the tabs can be cut with an approved tool or separated as intended by the panel design.

Do not treat a routed tab as permission to twist the PCB free by hand. The same board-flex risk remains, especially when components are close to the edge or break line.

---

## 4.21.5 - Router Depanelization Reduces the Need to Bend the Board

A depaneling router uses a rotating cutter to cut the PCB outline or the remaining support tabs while the panel/board is held in a fixture.

Compared with manual snapping, a well-supported routing process can separate the unit with much less whole-board bending. Mechanical routing is still a contact process, however, so it should be described as **low-strain/controlled**, not literally stress-free. That makes routing attractive for assemblies with:

- sensitive components near the perimeter;
- irregular board outlines;
- dense component placement;
- higher risk from board flexure.

The fixture must hold the assembly securely without blocking the cutter path or contacting fragile components. Cutting debris must also be controlled so it does not contaminate the assembly.

Router depanelization is not completely force-free: cutting and fixturing still introduce local mechanical loads. The practical goal is **controlled support and localized cutting rather than uncontrolled flexing of the complete PCBA**.

---

## 4.21.6 - Board Bending Can Damage Components and Solder Joints

Depanelization becomes more critical after components are installed because the populated board is no longer just a piece of laminate.

Excessive board bending can place tensile or shear strain into:

- brittle ceramic components such as multilayer ceramic capacitors;
- solder joints near the panel edge or break line;
- large packages whose joints span a wide footprint;
- connectors or mechanically loaded components;
- copper features near the final board edge.

A useful first check is the **location of the component relative to the separation line**.

If a fragile component is close to the score or tab, reduce board flex by using the approved support/tooling and separation method. Do not use one hand to hold the finished board while the other hand twists the remaining panel away.

A component or joint can be mechanically damaged even when there is no obvious visible crack immediately after depanelization. This is why separation method, support, and component placement must be considered together.

---

## 4.21.7 - Choose the Depanelization Method from the Board Geometry and Risk

A simple planning comparison is:

| Board condition | Reasonable starting method |
|---|---|
| straight rectangular boundaries with adequate edge clearance | V-score or another validated straight-line separation process |
| irregular perimeter held by designed tabs | routed tabs with controlled tab cutting/separation |
| sensitive components close to the edge or low allowable board flex | supported router or another validated low-strain separation process |
| manual break-off | only where the score/tab design and work instruction specifically allow it |

These are planning categories, not universal process requirements. The approved panel drawing, component constraints, and available depaneling equipment control the actual method.

---

## 4.21.8 - Secondary Assembly Adds the Items That Do Not Belong in the Main Soldering Flow

After the primary soldering route, the PCBA may still require parts that are difficult, unnecessary, or undesirable to send through the main SMT/THT process.

Examples include:

- press-fit connectors;
- odd-form components;
- shields or cans;
- brackets, standoffs, screws, or other mechanical hardware;
- heat sinks and thermal-interface-material (TIM) hardware;
- labels;
- daughterboards or modules;
- staking or adhesive used for specified mechanical support.

These are grouped here as **secondary assembly** because they are added in separate operations or work cells outside the main automated soldering sequence. Secondary does not mean unimportant, and it does not mean the operation is always manual.

The exact sequence depends on access. A heat sink, shield, bracket, or daughterboard can hide fasteners, test points, connectors, or inspection features, so the order should be planned before the hardware is installed.

---

## 4.21.9 - Press-Fit Connections Use Controlled Mechanical Insertion

A **press-fit connection** is a solderless board-level interconnection in which a specially designed pin is pressed into a compatible plated through-hole. It is therefore different from the soldered THT connection introduced earlier in the chapter.

For a compliant press-fit pin, the press-fit section deforms as it enters the plated hole and produces the contact force required for the electrical and mechanical connection. The pin geometry, hole diameter/plating, and insertion process therefore form one engineered system; a press-fit pin should not be treated as a generic pin that can be forced into any plated hole.

A representative installation sequence is:

> **verify connector and PCB -> support PCB -> align pins to PTHs -> press with correct tooling -> seat to the required position -> verify the result**

The important physical idea is that press force must be controlled.

- The connector must be aligned before significant force is applied.
- The PCB should be supported by the intended fixture or support tool, preferably close to the insertion region so the press load does not bow the board.
- The pressing tool should act on the designed connector/terminal surface and remain aligned with the support tooling.
- Uncontrolled hammering, plier squeezing, or forcing a misaligned connector can damage pins, PTHs, the connector body, or the PCB.
- Production equipment may monitor press force, distance, or both, but the allowable process window is connector- and product-specific.

## 4.21.10 - Odd-Form Components and Mechanical Hardware Need Access and Support

An **odd-form component** is a part that does not fit the normal automated placement/process flow for a particular product or line. Examples can include large connectors, relays, transformers, switches, modules, or other unusually shaped parts.

They may be:

- manually inserted;
- placed by special tooling or a dedicated machine;
- soldered by a process already covered in this chapter;
- snapped or pressed into mechanical features;
- fastened with screws or other hardware.

Mechanical hardware such as **brackets, standoffs, and screws** should be installed according to the drawing and work instruction. The correct stack-up matters: a missing washer, wrong standoff, cross-threaded fastener, or excessive tightening can change board support or damage the assembly.

Do not substitute a generic torque rule for the specified fastener requirement. Torque depends on the screw, thread, material, locking method, and mechanical design.

---

## 4.21.11 - Shields, Daughterboards, and Modules Can Change Access

A shield can, module, or daughterboard may be installed after the main soldering process because it would otherwise interfere with placement, soldering, inspection, or test access. Some shield cans are themselves soldered during reflow; the `secondary assembly` case here refers to shields or covers that are clipped, fastened, snapped, or otherwise installed in a later operation.

Before installation, check:

- correct part and orientation;
- mating connector alignment;
- required standoffs or supports;
- clearance to nearby components;
- whether test points, fasteners, or inspection features will become hidden;
- whether the added part can contact or load another component when the board flexes.

A daughterboard should not be forced into a misaligned connector. Alignment and mechanical support should carry the assembly load as intended by the product design.

---

## 4.21.12 - Heat-Sink and TIM Hardware Is a Physical Assembly Operation

Some power devices or modules require a **heat sink** or another thermal path after the soldering operations are complete.

A **thermal interface material (TIM)** may be placed between mating surfaces to reduce the effect of microscopic air gaps and improve thermal contact. TIM can take several product-specific forms; the manufacturing task is to use the approved material and installation method rather than assuming one generic grease or pad is interchangeable with another.

At this stage, the manufacturing concerns are basic but important:

- use the correct heat sink, TIM, insulator, clip, screw, or bracket;
- keep the mating surfaces and TIM placement consistent with the work instruction;
- avoid damaging the package or bending the PCB during fastening;
- use the specified compression/torque/fastening method rather than an improvised value;
- preserve required electrical isolation where the design uses an insulating thermal interface.

Detailed heat-sink sizing and thermal-resistance design belong in Chapter 7. Detailed fastener, structural-support, shock, and vibration design belong in Chapter 8.

---

## 4.21.13 - Labels, Staking, and Adhesives Also Need Process Control

Secondary operations can also include identification labels and mechanically supporting selected components or wires with an approved adhesive or staking material.

For **labels**, verify the correct:

- product/revision or required identification information;
- placement location and orientation;
- surface condition;
- clearance from connectors, vents, optical features, test points, or required hardware.

For **staking/adhesive**, which here means localized mechanical support rather than conformal coating, verify:

- the material is approved for the product;
- it is applied only to the intended location;
- it does not block connectors, fasteners, test access, or service access;
- the required cure/process condition is followed;
- the cured material does not place an unintended load on a fragile component or lead.

Detailed materials selection, environmental protection, and reliability behavior are covered later in the book.

## 4.21.14 - Plan the Order of Secondary Operations

Secondary assembly is not one universal list performed in the same order for every product.

A useful planning sequence is:

1. identify which operations require the board to remain in panel form;
2. identify the intended depanelization method and support;
3. separate the board at the approved point in the route;
4. install press-fit, odd-form, or mechanical hardware using the required tooling;
5. install modules, shields, thermal hardware, labels, or staking in an order that preserves access;
6. perform the required downstream verification before important evidence becomes hidden.

Cleaning may need to occur before some labels, adhesives, thermal materials, or final hardware are installed. Therefore, the chapter sequence should not be mistaken for a universal factory route. Section 4.22 explains cleaning and protection processes next.

---

## 4.21.15 - A Practical Depanelization and Secondary-Assembly Check

Before releasing the assembly from these operations, an engineering technologist should be able to confirm:

### Depanelization

- the correct panel/board and intended separation line are identified;
- V-score, tab, router, or other approved separation method matches the work instruction;
- the assembly is supported near the separation region;
- the board is not being unnecessarily bent or twisted;
- components and joints near the separation edge are protected from mechanical strain and tool contact;
- cut/tab debris and edge condition are controlled as required.

### Secondary assembly

- correct hardware/component revision is present;
- press-fit or snap-in operations use the correct alignment and support tooling;
- screws, standoffs, brackets, shields, and modules match the required stack-up/orientation;
- heat-sink/TIM hardware is installed without damaging the device or PCB;
- labels and adhesives are applied at the intended locations;
- no new hardware blocks required test, inspection, connector, fastener, or rework access;
- the assembly is handled using the required ESD and mechanical precautions.

Formal acceptance requirements belong to the applicable product documentation and Chapter 5 quality/test processes. The purpose here is to recognize the physical operation, protect the already assembled board, and know what should be checked next.

---

## 4.21.16 - Section Takeaway

Depanelization and secondary assembly finish important physical work that may remain after the primary soldering route.

The most important practical points are:

- V-score, routed tabs/mouse bites, and router cutting are common ways to separate PCB units from a panel;
- manual break-off should occur only where the panel was intentionally designed for it;
- support the board near the separation region and avoid unnecessary bending or twisting;
- components and solder joints near the panel edge can be damaged by depanelization strain;
- press-fit connectors use controlled mechanical insertion into compatible plated through-holes and require alignment and PCB support;
- odd-form components, shields, brackets, standoffs, screws, daughterboards, heat-sink/TIM hardware, labels, and staking may require separate secondary operations;
- secondary hardware must be installed in an order that preserves access and does not mechanically damage completed work;
- the actual manufacturing order remains product dependent.

After these physical assembly operations, some products require residue removal or environmental protection. **Section 4.22 moves to cleaning and protection processes**, including cleaning-method awareness, drying, conformal coating, masking, curing, underfill, and potting/encapsulation.
