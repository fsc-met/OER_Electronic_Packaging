# 4.15 - Measuring and Verifying a Reflow Profile

Section 4.14 introduced the reflow oven and the temperature-versus-time profile experienced by a PCB assembly. It also established an important distinction:

> **oven setpoints describe the machine recipe; a measured profile describes what the actual PCB assembly experiences.**

A recipe can look reasonable on the oven screen and still produce different temperatures at different solder joints. Component mass, copper distribution, board thickness, airflow, conveyor speed, and oven condition all affect the product response.

This section develops the practical profiling workflow used to answer a basic manufacturing question:

> **Did the important locations on this assembly actually experience the required thermal process?**

The goal is not advanced thermal modeling. The goal is to understand how thermocouples, a profiling board, and a temperature data logger are used to measure the process, compare it with supplied limits, make a controlled adjustment, and verify again.

---

## 4.15.1 - Why the Oven Recipe Is Not Enough

A reflow recipe may specify:

- zone-temperature setpoints;
- conveyor speed;
- airflow or fan settings when adjustable;
- cooling settings;
- nitrogen settings when applicable.

Those settings define the **thermal environment** created by the oven. They do not directly tell us the temperature of a particular solder joint.

For example, a late oven zone may be set near 245 °C while a heavy, copper-coupled component on the PCB reaches only 232 °C. A small component near the board edge might reach 244 °C in the same run.

The process engineer therefore needs two kinds of information:

| Information | What it tells us |
|---|---|
| **Oven recipe** | what the machine was commanded to do |
| **Measured product profile** | what selected locations on the actual assembly experienced |

The second is the evidence used to verify the thermal process.

---

## 4.15.2 - Thermocouples and the Reflow Profiler

A **thermocouple** is a temperature sensor made from two dissimilar metal wires joined at a measurement junction. The small junction, often called the **bead**, is placed in thermal contact with the location whose temperature is being measured.

During a profiling run, several thermocouples can be connected to a **reflow profiler** or temperature data logger. The profiler records each channel as the instrumented board travels through the oven and produces temperature-versus-time traces.

A typical profiling setup therefore contains:

- a representative populated PCB or dedicated profiling board;
- several thermocouples attached at selected locations;
- a multichannel profiler/data logger;
- a thermal barrier that protects the logger when it travels through the oven, when required by the system;
- software that displays the recorded traces and calculates metrics such as ramp rate, peak temperature, time above liquidus (TAL), and cooling rate.

The profiler does not replace the oven controller. It provides independent measurement evidence about how the product responds to the oven recipe.

## 4.15.3 - Thermocouple Attachment Must Represent the Target

A thermocouple reports the temperature at its junction. If that junction is not actually coupled to the intended solder-joint location, the reading can be misleading.

A good attachment should:

- place the thermocouple junction in intimate thermal contact with the intended measurement point;
- keep the junction from moving during the run;
- avoid creating an unintended second junction between the thermocouple wires;
- add as little extra thermal mass as practical;
- survive the expected reflow temperature without releasing;
- avoid shielding the measurement point from the oven more than necessary.

For durable profile boards, a common preferred method is to secure a fine-gauge thermocouple at the solder-joint location using a **high-temperature solder** whose melting point is safely above the reflow peak. Validated thermally conductive adhesive or aluminum tape can also be used for some measurements and systems when they hold the bead tightly against the target.

Plain insulating tape placed directly over the thermocouple bead is generally a poor choice because it can alter heat transfer or allow the bead to move. Polyimide tape may still be useful for securing thermocouple **wires** away from the measurement point, provided it does not cover or displace the bead. Local work instructions and profiler/thermocouple manufacturer guidance should control the exact attachment method.

| Attachment condition | Measurement risk |
|---|---|
| Bead firmly coupled to intended solder-joint location | best chance of representing the target temperature |
| Bead touching only package body or PCB away from the joint | measures a different thermal location |
| Loose or lifting bead | reading may shift toward local oven-air temperature |
| Large attachment mass | sensor may respond more slowly than the actual joint |
| Insulating material covering the bead | can delay or distort the thermal response |

A useful rule is:

> **A clean temperature curve is not automatically a correct measurement. First verify that the sensor is measuring the intended physical location.**

---

## 4.15.4 - Use a Representative Profiling Board

The profiling board should represent the product being soldered as closely as practical.

A **populated board** is preferred because the component mix and copper structure influence the thermal response. A bare PCB does not reproduce the same thermal mass or airflow obstruction as the assembled product.

Depending on the production environment, the profile board may be:

- a representative production assembly reserved for profiling;
- a sacrificial product board fitted with thermocouples;
- a dedicated test vehicle that reproduces the important thermal features of the product.

The purpose is not to create a perfect laboratory specimen. The purpose is to measure a board that responds to the oven in a way that is representative of the product being built.

For a double-sided assembly, the relevant reflow pass should be profiled with a board state that represents that pass. Components already mounted on the opposite side can change the assembly's thermal mass and airflow response.

---

## 4.15.5 - Choose Measurement Locations Deliberately

One thermocouple cannot represent the entire assembly. Section 4.14 showed that different locations can follow different temperature histories.

Useful measurement locations commonly include:

- a **high-thermal-mass location**, such as a large package, shield, connector, or a joint strongly coupled to a large copper region;
- a **low-thermal-mass location**, such as a small passive component or lightly coupled joint;
- a location near the **center of the board**, where heating can lag on some products;
- a location near a **board edge**, where the thermal response may be faster;
- a **temperature-sensitive component** or other critical location with a defined maximum exposure;
- a hidden or difficult joint, such as a BGA location, when that joint is important to the process window.

The actual hottest and coldest locations must be established from product knowledge and profiling evidence rather than assumed from board position alone.

For simple products, a few well-chosen measurement channels may be enough. More complex boards may require more channels to capture high-mass, low-mass, sensitive, center/edge, leading/trailing, or other critical conditions. The number of channels should follow the thermal questions that need to be answered, not a fixed universal count.

## 4.15.6 - A Basic Reflow Profiling Workflow

A practical introductory workflow is:

1. **Identify the applicable process requirements.**
   - solder-paste profile guidance;
   - alloy/liquidus reference;
   - component temperature limits;
   - product-specific or customer requirements.
2. **Prepare a representative profiling board.**
3. **Attach thermocouples at the selected locations and record which channel measures which point.**
4. **Load an initial oven recipe.**
   - use an approved existing recipe, paste-supplier starting guidance, or a baseline from a similar validated product as appropriate.
5. **Run the instrumented board through the full reflow process.**
6. **Review each recorded trace and its calculated metrics.**
7. **Compare every important location with the applicable limits.**
8. **Make one small, logical recipe adjustment when needed.**
9. **Allow the profiling board and profiler to return close to the intended starting condition before a repeat run when required for a fair comparison.**
10. **Run the profile again and verify the result.**
11. **Document the accepted recipe and profiling evidence according to the local process-control system.**

The logic is simple:

> **measure -> compare -> adjust -> re-measure**

A recipe should not be considered verified because one thermocouple passes. The relevant measurement locations must fit the required process window at the same time.

## 4.15.7 - Read the Measured Trace Using the Same Quantities from Section 4.14

The profiler converts each thermocouple channel into a temperature-versus-time trace. The important quantities are the same ones introduced in Section 4.14, but now they are measured rather than assumed.

### Heating ramp

A simple average temperature slope between two points is

$$
R = \frac{T_2-T_1}{t_2-t_1}
$$

where $R$ is the average temperature rise rate over the selected interval.

This equation explains the physical idea, but profiling software and solder-paste guidance may define the reported ramp metric differently, such as a maximum rising slope or an average ramp over a stated interval. Compare a measured value only with a limit that uses the **same definition**.

### Peak temperature

The **peak temperature** is the maximum temperature reached by that thermocouple channel.

### Time above liquidus

If the trace crosses the defined liquidus reference $T_L$ at time $t_1$ during heating and returns below it at time $t_2$ during cooling,

$$
\text{TAL}=t_2-t_1
$$

### Cooling rate

The falling portion of the trace can be evaluated in the same slope form. Cooling rate is often reported as the magnitude of the negative temperature slope.

A profiler may calculate all of these values automatically, but the plotted curve should still be reviewed. A calculated pass does not excuse an obviously bad sensor attachment, discontinuous trace, or mislabeled thermocouple channel.

## 4.15.8 - Worked Multi-Thermocouple Example

Consider a populated PCB with four thermocouples:

- **TC1:** small passive component near a board edge;
- **TC2:** medium-size QFN near the center;
- **TC3:** large power package connected to a heavy copper region;
- **TC4:** temperature-sensitive connector location.

Assume the approved job documentation gives the following **illustrative process window** for this SAC305 paste/product combination:

| Metric | Supplied process limit for this example |
|---|---:|
| Average heating-ramp metric, as defined for this job | **0.7-2.0 °C/s** |
| Time above liquidus | **45-90 s above 217 °C** |
| Peak temperature | **235-245 °C** |
| Cooling-rate magnitude | **1-6 °C/s** |

These limits are supplied for this example. They are **not universal acceptance limits for every SAC305 process**.

The first profiling run gives:

| Channel / location | Ramp (°C/s) | TAL (s) | Peak (°C) | Cooling (°C/s) | Initial judgment |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| **TC1 - small edge passive** | 1.8 | 78 | 244 | 4.5 | within supplied window |
| **TC2 - center QFN** | 1.5 | 66 | 241 | 4.0 | within supplied window |
| **TC3 - power package / heavy copper** | 1.1 | 39 | 232 | 3.2 | **peak and TAL too low** |
| **TC4 - sensitive connector** | 1.7 | 72 | 243 | 4.6 | within supplied window |

The coldest measured peak is TC3 at 232 °C, while the hottest measured peak is TC1 at 244 °C. The peak-temperature spread is therefore

$$
\Delta T_{\text{peak}} = 244-232 = 12\ ^\circ\text{C}
$$

The important conclusion is not simply that the oven is "too cold." Three locations already meet the supplied limits, and TC1 is only 1 °C below the upper peak limit. Raising every zone aggressively could fix TC3 while overheating TC1 or TC4.

## 4.15.9 - Basic Recipe-Adjustment Logic

A useful first step is to connect the measured problem to the machine controls most likely to influence it.

| Measured evidence | Basic control to investigate first | Important caution |
|---|---|---|
| Ramp too fast or too slow | early-zone temperature pattern and airflow; conveyor speed may also influence the result | ramp definitions vary, and changing one control can affect later portions of the profile |
| Soak/equalization not appropriate | early/middle-zone temperatures and conveyor speed | not every paste uses the same soak strategy |
| Peak too low or too high | late/reflow-zone setpoints | hot and cold locations may respond differently |
| TAL too short or too long | conveyor speed and late-zone thermal shape | conveyor speed affects the entire oven residence time |
| Cooling too fast or too slow | cooling-zone intensity/airflow and conveyor speed | cooling changes can affect board/component thermal stress |
| Large hot-to-cold spread | profile shape, top/bottom heat balance, airflow, conveyor speed, and product thermal design | do not assume one control will reduce the spread without measurement |

For the worked example, TC3 needs more thermal exposure, but TC1 is already close to the upper peak limit. A reasonable engineering response is therefore to make a **small, controlled change** intended to improve TC3, then re-profile all four channels.

The exact first change depends on the oven and product. It might involve a modest change to selected late-zone setpoints, conveyor speed, or the earlier equalization portion of the recipe. None of these is a one-to-one control: a change intended to improve one metric can move several other metrics at the same time. The important rule is:

> **Do not change several controls at once and then guess which change helped. Make a logical adjustment, measure again, and watch both the coldest and hottest locations.**

### Example second run

Suppose the team makes **one small conveyor-speed reduction** and repeats the profile from a comparable starting condition. The new peak/TAL results are:

| Channel | TAL after adjustment (s) | Peak after adjustment (°C) | Verification result |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| **TC1** | 86 | 245 | within supplied window, but at upper peak limit |
| **TC2** | 74 | 242 | within supplied window |
| **TC3** | 51 | 236 | now within supplied window |
| **TC4** | 80 | 244 | within supplied window |

This illustrative second run shows why all channels must be checked again. The change corrected the cold location, but it also moved the hot location closer to its upper limit. A further slowdown would not be justified from this evidence without another reason.

This is process development by evidence, not by oven-screen appearance.

---

## 4.15.10 - Re-Profile and Verify

After an adjustment, run the instrumented board again and compare the new traces with the same requirements.

A basic verification decision should ask:

- Did the previously failing location move into the required window?
- Did any previously acceptable location move out of the window?
- Is the peak spread across the critical locations reasonable for this product?
- Are all thermocouple traces smooth and believable?
- Did any sensor move, loosen, or produce an implausible trace?
- Was the repeat run started from a comparable board/profiler condition?
- Is the recipe now stable enough to document and use according to the local process-control procedure?

The profile may also need to be rechecked after significant events such as:

- a major oven maintenance action;
- changes to conveyor, airflow, heaters, or cooling hardware;
- a PCB/component/material revision that changes thermal behavior;
- transfer to a different oven;
- evidence that the process has drifted.

The exact re-profiling schedule is company- and product-specific. The important idea is that thermal profiling is not a one-time theoretical calculation.

---

## 4.15.11 - What to Recognize in a Profiling Report

A beginning technologist should be able to look at a reflow profiling report and identify:

- the product/job, assembly side or reflow pass when applicable, and oven recipe used;
- thermocouple channel names and physical locations;
- temperature-versus-time traces;
- ramp metric;
- soak information when applicable;
- liquidus reference and TAL;
- peak temperature;
- cooling rate;
- pass/fail or comparison against the applicable process limits;
- evidence of an abnormal or unreliable thermocouple trace.

The report is useful only when the measurement locations and process requirements are known. A graph with unlabeled channels or unclear limits is weak manufacturing evidence.

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- reflow profiling measures what the PCB assembly actually experiences rather than assuming oven setpoints equal product temperature;
- thermocouples must be attached so their measurement junctions represent the intended solder-joint or component location;
- a representative populated profiling board is needed because board construction and component thermal mass affect the profile;
- measurement locations should include expected thermal extremes and critical or temperature-sensitive areas;
- a profiler records multiple temperature-versus-time traces and extracts ramp, TAL, peak, and cooling information;
- every important thermocouple location must be compared with the applicable paste, component, and product requirements;
- recipe changes should be small and evidence-driven, followed by another profiling run that rechecks **all** critical channels;
- a passing recipe is established by **measure -> compare -> adjust -> re-measure**, not by oven settings alone.

With the reflow process now measured and verified, Section 4.16 combines printing, placement, materials, and reflow evidence to examine **common SMT defects and their possible sources**.

Corrected or qualified:
