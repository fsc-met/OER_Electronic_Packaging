# 4.9 - Print Quality and Solder Paste Inspection (SPI)

Section 4.8 introduced the solder-paste printer and the setup controls that produce a repeatable print. The next question is whether the printed deposits actually meet the needs of the PCB being assembled.

A paste deposit can look acceptable from above and still contain too little material. Another deposit may have enough volume but be shifted toward a neighboring pad. **Solder paste inspection (SPI)** turns those conditions into measurements before components are placed and the board enters the reflow oven.

A useful working sequence is:

> **print -> inspect the deposits -> interpret the evidence -> release or hold the board -> check the related print process when needed**

The purpose of SPI is to detect print problems early and give the printer operator or process engineer useful evidence. It does not identify every root cause or certify the finished solder joint.

---

## 4.9.1 - What a Good Print Looks Like

For a given product, a good print has deposits in the intended locations, with enough paste and a controlled shape for the component terminals that will be placed on them. Comparable features should print consistently, without unwanted paste joining adjacent deposits.

The intended deposit is not necessarily an exact copy of the copper pad. Stencil apertures may deliberately change the deposit's size or shape. Therefore, print quality is judged against the **qualified product-specific print target**, not against a rule that every deposit must cover the entire pad.

| Printed condition | What the evidence may show | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **complete, controlled deposit** | intended location, repeatable footprint and volume, clear gap to neighboring deposits | provides the planned paste material for placement and reflow |
| **insufficient or missing paste** | low measured area and/or volume; a partial or absent deposit | may leave too little solder available for the intended joint |
| **excess paste** | high volume, enlarged footprint, or paste outside the intended region | can increase the chance of bridging or solder beads |
| **offset / misalignment** | deposit displaced from its intended print location | can reduce terminal-to-paste overlap or bring paste closer to an adjacent feature |
| **bridging / smearing** | unintended paste connection or thin tail between neighboring deposits | can create an unwanted solder connection after reflow |
| **irregular deposit** | asymmetrical shape or unexpectedly different heights within similar features | may indicate inconsistent filling or release |

These are **pre-placement print conditions**. A visible paste bridge is not yet a confirmed post-reflow solder bridge, and a low-volume paste deposit is not yet a measured electrical open. Section 4.7 introduced the visual appearances; this section adds quantitative evidence and the next process check.

---

## 4.9.2 - Where SPI Fits in the SMT Line

In a representative SMT line, SPI is located **after solder-paste printing and before component placement**:

> **printer -> SPI -> pick-and-place -> reflow -> post-reflow inspection**

The system measures exposed paste deposits on the PCB. Depending on the product and inspection plan, it may measure all programmed deposits or a selected set. It compares the results with the intended print geometry and the limits in the inspection program.

If a result is outside the approved limits, the system may flag the board for operator review, hold, or a defined recovery action. Cleaning and reprinting may be possible **when allowed by the product and local procedure**; a failed board is not automatically sent back through the printer without review.

The key distinction is that **SPI inspects the paste before placement**. AOI after placement or reflow and X-ray inspection answer different questions about components and solder joints.

---

## 4.9.3 - The Four Measurements to Recognize First

A modern 3D SPI system measures the deposit's surface and compares it with a reference. Four measurements provide most of the first-level print evidence.

| SPI measurement | What it describes | Typical reported unit | What an abnormal result can suggest |
|---|---|---|---|
| **height** | paste height above the chosen local reference surface | $\mu\text{m}$ | thin, peaked, or uneven deposit; possible release variation |
| **area** | footprint of the detected paste deposit | $\text{mm}^2$ or percentage of a defined reference area | missing material, spread, or a changed deposit outline |
| **volume** | three-dimensional amount of printed paste | $\text{mm}^3$ or percentage of a defined reference volume | insufficient/excess deposition or changing transfer |
| **X/Y offset** | lateral displacement of the deposit from its programmed target | $\mu\text{m}$ or $\text{mm}$ | board/stencil registration or local placement of the print pattern |

**Shape** and **bridging** are also important inspection outputs. A shape result asks whether the deposit outline and height profile are appropriate; bridging checks whether unwanted paste connects regions that should remain separate.

The word **reference** matters. Some programs report deposit area relative to the expected aperture footprint, some relative to a defined nominal area, and others show pad coverage. An intentional aperture reduction or offset should not be mistaken for a printing defect because the reference was chosen incorrectly.

For scale, a stencil may be about **0.10–0.15 mm (100–150 $\mu\text{m}$)** thick in a conventional SMT example. Deposit height is often of the same order, but it is not guaranteed to equal stencil thickness: release, deposit shape, the measurement definition, and local PCB surface height also matter. Likewise, offsets on the order of **25–50 $\mu\text{m}$** are useful fine-feature scale examples; **50 $\mu\text{m}$ is 0.05 mm**. Whether an offset is acceptable depends on the intended aperture location, pad/terminal geometry, and the qualified limit, not on the number alone.

## 4.9.4 - Why 3D Inspection Adds Information

A **2D inspection** uses the top-view image to evaluate the deposit's footprint and position. It can identify missing paste, an apparent bridge, or a visibly shifted print, but it does not directly measure the full height profile or true deposited volume.

A **3D SPI system** uses an optical height-measurement method to reconstruct the deposit surface. Current systems may use projected light patterns and camera-based measurement. The system combines its measured surface/height information with the detected deposit region to calculate volume.

This matters because two deposits can have nearly the same top-view area but very different amounts of paste. Imagine two deposits with the same **0.20 $\text{mm}^2$** footprint. If one has a mean height of **100 $\mu\text{m}$** and the other **60 $\mu\text{m}$**, their approximate volumes are **0.020 $\text{mm}^3$** and **0.012 $\text{mm}^3$**, respectively. The second carries **40% less paste volume** even though the footprints match.

Those example volumes use **footprint area × mean height**. A real SPI system obtains volume from the measured three-dimensional height distribution; multiplying a pad's nominal area by a single peak-height reading is not a reliable substitute.

## 4.9.5 - Reading Volume Percentage and Transfer Efficiency

SPI results often express measured volume as a percentage. First establish **what the percentage is relative to**. A program may compare the print with a qualified target or with the theoretical stencil-aperture volume. These references are not necessarily identical.

For a rectangular aperture, the theoretical volume introduced in Section 4.7 is:

$$
V_{\text{aperture}} = LWT
$$

where $L$ and $W$ are the aperture's opening dimensions and $T$ is stencil thickness. All three dimensions must use the same length unit.

When measured volume is compared with that theoretical aperture volume, the ratio is called **transfer efficiency**:

$$
TE = \frac{V_{\text{measured}}}{V_{\text{aperture}}}\times 100\%
$$

### Worked print example

Suppose an aperture is $0.50\text{ mm}$ long, $0.40\text{ mm}$ wide, and the stencil is $0.12\text{ mm}$ thick.

$$
V_{\text{aperture}} = (0.50)(0.40)(0.12)
= 0.024\text{ mm}^3
$$

The 3D SPI system measures $0.0192\text{ mm}^3$ of paste on the corresponding pad. For scale, $0.024\text{ mm}^3$ is **24 nanoliters (nL)**; these are small, process-sensitive deposits.

$$
TE = \frac{0.0192}{0.024}\times 100\% = 80\%
$$

The measured paste deposit is **80% of the theoretical aperture volume**. This is useful information about filling and release, but **80% is not automatically a pass or a fail**. The approved limits must account for the component, aperture geometry, paste, process capability, and the relevant joint requirement.

The quantity measured here is **solder-paste volume**, which includes both alloy powder and flux vehicle. It is not the same as the volume of solid metal remaining in the finished solder joint after reflow.

For a first engineering scale, an illustrative inspection program might use a volume window of **80–120% of its defined nominal target** for one family of features. That example is not a universal SPI acceptance rule, and its nominal target may differ from theoretical aperture volume. Smaller or unusual apertures may require a different qualified window. A single threshold is not suitable for every pad on a complex board.

Introductory printing examples also sometimes use roughly **70–100% transfer efficiency** as a reference scale. This is **not an acceptance band**: a qualified process may have different targets, and a reported transfer efficiency above 100% is not automatically a sensor failure. Check the deposit geometry, print condition, and measurement reference before judging the result.

## 4.9.6 - From a Failed Deposit to a First Process Check

A failed SPI measurement identifies **what appears abnormal**, not the one certain physical cause. Start by matching the measurement pattern to a short list of related process checks.

| SPI evidence | Useful first checks | Avoid this premature conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| **one missing/low-volume deposit** | corresponding stencil aperture, paste blockage, local release | that the whole printer needs more force |
| **several low deposits in the same region** | local board support, stencil-to-board contact, regional aperture cleanliness | that one failed aperture explains every result |
| **many deposits shifted the same direction** | fiducials, board location/clamping, product/stencil registration | that paste chemistry is the cause |
| **excess area, smearing, or an apparent bridge** | stencil underside, gasketing, paste condition, aperture/stencil match | that a post-reflow electrical short already exists |
| **volume slowly decreasing across boards** | stencil buildup, paste condition and open time, bead condition, print setup | that a single SPI value proves paste drying |

These checks are related to the mechanisms introduced in Sections 4.7 and 4.8. Make changes through the qualified setup procedure and verify the next print rather than changing several unrelated settings at once.

## 4.9.7 - Read the Map, Not Only the Alarm

Many SPI systems show results as a board or panel map. The **location and grouping** of abnormal deposits can be more useful than a single pass/fail count.

Consider an illustrative board with two rows of three similar deposits. Each percentage below is measured volume relative to the **same defined reference volume** for that feature family.

| Pad location | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **Row A** | 98% | 101% | 97% |
| **Row B** | 82% | 80% | 81% |

For this **teaching example only**, suppose the approved reference window for these pads is **90–110%**. All three Row B deposits would be flagged. Their shared location suggests checking something common to that region, such as local support/contact or the corresponding stencil apertures, before changing a global squeegee setting. The data alone do not establish which cause is present.

If one isolated pad were low while neighboring pads remained stable, the first check would more likely focus on that pad's aperture or local release. If every deposit were shifted the same direction, alignment would become a stronger first check.

## 4.9.8 - Use Trends Before a Defect Becomes Widespread

An SPI system can also display results over successive boards. A stable average and repeatable distribution are generally more useful than a single lucky pass.

Suppose the average volume percentage for one comparable feature family changes across eight boards:

| Board in sequence | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Average volume | 99% | 99% | 97% | 96% | 95% | 94% | 92% | 88% |

With the same **illustrative 90–110% window**, Board 8 is below the lower limit. The decreasing values on Boards 3–7 were useful earlier warning evidence even before the result crossed that limit. The next step is to verify the data and inspect relevant conditions such as stencil cleanliness, paste condition, and the print setup. After an approved correction, another print must be measured to confirm recovery.

Detailed control charts, process capability, and automated closed-loop correction belong in later manufacturing-engineering treatment. Some current printer/SPI systems can exchange data and suggest or execute corrections, but those actions still require a qualified process and sensible limits.

## 4.9.9 - False Calls, Limits, and Measurement Confidence

SPI results are measurements made by an optical system. A flagged result may reflect a real abnormal print, a reference/program error, or a measurement problem.

Before changing the printer, confirm that:

- the correct product, board side, inspection program, and reference geometry were loaded;
- the board and fiducials are recognized correctly;
- the measurement region is associated with the intended pad/deposit;
- the PCB surface/height reference is reasonable for the actual board condition;
- an unusual shape, contamination, reflection, or board warp has not confused the measurement;
- the flagged deposit and neighboring results agree with the visible evidence where practical.

Do not respond to frequent false calls simply by widening every limit. That may hide real defects. Conversely, an unnecessarily tight, unverified limit may stop a stable process without improving product quality. Verify the measurement and review the limit against qualified product evidence before changing the inspection program.

> **Practical distinction:** A **detection limit** determines what the SPI program flags. A **release decision** follows the approved inspection and disposition procedure, supported by measurement confidence and the product's requirements.

---

## 4.9.10 - What SPI Cannot Tell You

SPI measures the **printed paste before components are placed and before reflow**. It cannot directly verify:

- whether the component will be placed correctly;
- whether solder will wet the pad and component termination during reflow;
- the finished joint's internal voiding or hidden-interface condition;
- the final joint's electrical or mechanical performance;
- whether a later reflow recipe or handling step will be correct.

A print with acceptable volume is a useful starting condition, not a guarantee of a good solder joint. Later placement, reflow, inspection, and test still matter.

---

## 4.9.11 - First-Board SPI Review Checklist

For a first board or changeover, a beginning technologist should be able to answer:

1. **Correct reference?** Is the SPI program for this board, side, stencil/aperture set, and inspection plan?
2. **Correct measurements?** Do height, area, volume, and offset refer to the intended deposits and units?
3. **Any missing, low, excess, shifted, smeared, or connected deposits?**
4. **A single pad or a pattern?** Are the abnormal results isolated, grouped, or board-wide?
5. **A one-board event or a trend?** Are comparable deposits changing across successive boards?
6. **A verified call?** Does the image/measurement support the alarm, or is measurement/program review needed?
7. **Next action defined?** Is the board released, held for review, or handled by an approved recovery procedure?
8. **Check one related item first?** Has the evidence been connected to an appropriate stencil, paste, board-support, alignment, or printer check?
9. **Result verified?** After an approved correction, was the print measured again?

The goal is to move from **a red SPI alarm** to **a defensible first check and a verified next print**.

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- SPI normally follows solder-paste printing and precedes component placement;
- a controlled deposit must meet the intended product-specific volume, footprint, location, and separation requirements;
- **3D SPI** measures height/shape and calculates volume in addition to area and X/Y offset;
- a top-view footprint alone may hide a low-volume deposit;
- transfer efficiency compares measured volume with theoretical stencil-aperture volume, but a percentage is meaningful only when its reference is defined;
- a missing deposit, regional cluster, common offset, and gradual trend suggest **different first checks**, not one automatic diagnosis;
- inspection limits and false calls require a qualified reference and appropriate review;
- SPI provides early process evidence, not a guarantee of the solder joint after reflow.
