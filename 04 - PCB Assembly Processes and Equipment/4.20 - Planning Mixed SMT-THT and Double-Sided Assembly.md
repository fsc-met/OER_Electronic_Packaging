# 4.20 - Planning Mixed SMT/THT and Double-Sided Assembly

Section 4.19 compared wave, selective, and hand soldering as different ways to form through-hole joints. The next step is to combine the processes from this chapter into a **manufacturing route for a complete board**.

A process route is the ordered sequence used to build the PCBA. There is no single correct route for every product. The route depends on:

- which components are on each side of the PCB;
- whether the board uses SMT, THT, or both;
- which components can tolerate reflow or wave exposure;
- whether first-side components can remain secure during a later thermal cycle;
- whether wave or selective-solder tooling can physically reach the required joints;
- production volume and mix;
- where inspection or verification should occur;
- whether later access for rework or secondary assembly must be preserved.

The practical planning question is:

> **Which process should happen first, which process should happen later, and what must be protected or kept accessible at each step?**

---

## 4.20.1 - Start with the Board, Not with a Favorite Process

Before selecting machines, identify the physical assembly that must be built.

Ask:

1. Are SMT components mounted on one side or both sides?
2. Are THT components present?
3. Which THT parts are compatible with reflow, wave, selective, or hand soldering?
4. Are there bottom-side SMT components near THT leads that would interfere with a solder wave or selective nozzle?
5. Are there large or heavy components that could create retention or board-support problems?
6. Which areas must remain accessible for fixtures, pallets, nozzles, inspection, test, or later secondary assembly?
7. Which completed joints/components must be protected from unintended solder contact, flux, mechanical disturbance, or additional heat?

A route should follow the product's geometry and process constraints. It should not force the board through a process simply because a machine is available.

A useful first planning rule is:

> **Use broad, high-throughput processes where the board is compatible with them. Use localized processes where only selected joints need heat or solder exposure.**

This is a starting rule, not a universal recipe.

---

## 4.20.2 - Representative Assembly Routes

The following routes are common enough to use as planning models.

| Board / assembly condition | Representative route | Main planning issue |
|---|---|---|
| **Single-sided SMT** | print -> place -> reflow | simplest SMT route; verify profile and post-reflow condition |
| **Double-sided SMT** | print/place/reflow Side 1 -> flip -> print/place/reflow Side 2 | first-side components experience the second reflow cycle |
| **SMT + THT, wave-compatible underside** | SMT reflow -> THT insertion -> wave solder | broad underside exposure must be compatible with the assembly |
| **Double-sided SMT + THT** | Side 1 SMT -> Side 2 SMT -> THT insertion -> selective solder or controlled hand soldering | local access and protection of existing SMT become important |
| **Selected THT by pin-in-paste** | print SMT/PTH paste -> place SMT -> insert compatible THT -> reflow | enough solder-paste volume and THT reflow-temperature compatibility are required |

These are representative routes, not five mandatory categories. Real products may combine or reorder operations when the component set, tooling, or process requirements justify it.

## 4.20.3 - Single-Sided SMT Is the Simplest Planning Case

For an SMT-only board populated on one side, the route is usually straightforward:

> **print -> inspect paste as required -> place -> reflow -> post-reflow verification**

The main planning work is already covered by earlier sections:

- the stencil and paste deposits must support the component set;
- feeders/nozzles and placement data must support correct placement;
- the reflow profile must satisfy the solder paste and component limits;
- visible and hidden-joint inspection needs depend on the product and inspection plan.

The route becomes more complex when components are placed on the second side or when THT components are added.

---

## 4.20.4 - Double-Sided SMT Commonly Uses Two Reflow Passes

A **double-sided SMT assembly** has surface-mount components on both sides of the PCB. Here, `double-sided` describes **component assembly on two board sides**, not the number of copper layers in the PCB.

A common route is:

1. print, place, and reflow **Side 1**;
2. flip the board;
3. print, place, and reflow **Side 2**.

The important point is that the first-side solder joints are not isolated from the second reflow cycle. With the same reflow alloy on both sides, those first-side joints are commonly heated above liquidus again and can re-melt during the second pass. That controlled reflow is normal; the planning problem is keeping the component in position and keeping the total thermal exposure within the qualified process.

Small and moderate-mass components often remain attached because the molten solder joints provide enough surface-tension force to retain them. Heavier components, components with less effective wetted-joint area, or poorly supported assemblies can have less retention margin. There is no one component-mass limit that applies to every footprint and solder-joint geometry.

Therefore, side selection is an engineering decision. A common planning approach is to place:

- lighter and more easily retained components on the side processed first;
- heavier, larger, or more gravity-sensitive components on the side processed second when practical;
- components with important thermal limits where their total heat exposure can be controlled and verified.

Adhesive can be used for selected components when the process requires additional retention, but it adds material, dispensing/cure control, and rework considerations. It should not be treated as an automatic requirement for every double-sided SMT board.

## 4.20.5 - The Second Reflow Is Also a Second Thermal Exposure

Even when first-side components stay in place, the assembly experiences another complete heating cycle.

The second reflow can affect:

- solder-joint intermetallic growth;
- component moisture/reflow exposure;
- PCB warpage;
- temperature-sensitive components;
- the thermal response of the board because one side is already populated.

This does not mean double-sided reflow is inherently unreliable. It means the process must be treated as a planned two-pass thermal process.

Important checks include:

- use components and materials qualified for the intended thermal history;
- maintain moisture-sensitive-device controls from Section 4.5;
- profile the relevant reflow pass using a board state representative of that pass, as discussed in Section 4.15;
- support the board if weight, panel size, or heating creates excessive sag or warpage;
- avoid unnecessary extra thermal cycles.

Do not default to a special high-melting first-side alloy simply to prevent second-pass reflow. Different alloys can add material, profile, compatibility, and rework complications. Use a mixed-alloy strategy only when the product and validated process specifically require it.

---

## 4.20.6 - Adding THT Usually Adds an Insertion and Joining Step

A common mixed-technology route is:

> **SMT reflow -> THT insertion -> THT soldering**

This order is useful because tall THT bodies and protruding leads can interfere with:

- stencil contact;
- printer board support;
- SMT placement-head motion;
- reflow-conveyor clearance;
- board handling or flipping.

THT insertion is therefore often performed after the main SMT reflow operations.

The THT joining method then depends on the board.

### Wave soldering can fit when

- many THT joints can be processed together;
- the underside can tolerate the wave exposure;
- board orientation and component geometry support solder access and drainage;
- component/joint thermal mass can be handled within the validated wave process;
- a pallet can protect areas that should not contact the wave, if required.

### Selective soldering can fit when

- THT joints are limited to selected connectors or local groups;
- bottom-side SMT makes broad wave exposure undesirable;
- nozzle access and keep-out space are available;
- local programming provides a better fit than a board-wide solder wave.

### Hand soldering can fit when

- only a few special joints remain;
- production volume is low;
- odd-form parts, wires, terminals, or engineering modifications are difficult to automate;
- the approved process allows controlled manual assembly.

Do not choose the method from throughput alone. Access, thermal mass, repeatability, tooling, nearby SMT, and product requirements also matter.

---

## 4.20.7 - Bottom-Side SMT and Wave Soldering Require a Clear Strategy

The phrase **"bottom-side SMT through wave soldering"** can describe two different manufacturing strategies. They should not be confused.

### Strategy A - Bottom-side SMT is intentionally wave soldered

In a traditional mixed-technology route, selected bottom-side SMDs can be attached to the PCB with adhesive before wave soldering. The later wave then solders:

- the protruding THT leads;
- the wave-compatible bottom-side SMD terminations.

This route requires:

- components suitable for wave exposure;
- adhesive placement and cure where required for retention;
- component orientation compatible with wave travel and drainage;
- enough clearance to reduce shadowing and bridging;
- a wave process designed for the mixed assembly.

Only suitable bottom-side components should be used in such a route. Many fine-pitch, bottom-terminated, area-array, or otherwise wave-incompatible packages require another strategy.

### Strategy B - Bottom-side SMT was already reflow soldered and must be protected

If the bottom side has already been assembled by reflow, the process plan may instead protect those components from the wave using:

- a selective or masking pallet;
- keep-out areas;
- controlled wave-contact openings;
- or a different THT soldering method such as selective soldering.

In this case, the goal is **not** to wave-solder the existing SMT joints again. The goal is to expose only the THT locations that need solder.

> **Orientation can help solder access, wetting, and drainage, but it is not a substitute for component retention, masking, or wave compatibility.**

---

## 4.20.8 - Pin-in-Paste Can Eliminate a Separate THT Soldering Step for Selected Parts

**Pin-in-paste (PiP)**, also called **intrusive reflow** or **paste-in-hole**, uses solder paste and the reflow process to form selected through-hole joints.

A simplified route is:

1. print solder paste onto the SMT pads and the required PTH region;
2. place the SMT components;
3. insert the compatible THT component into the paste-filled/overprinted PTH location;
4. reflow the assembly so the SMT and selected THT joints form in the same thermal process.

The advantage is that a separate wave, selective, or hand-soldering step may be avoided for those selected through-hole parts. Unlike ordinary wave or selective soldering, however, the **THT component body also travels through the reflow thermal cycle**, so its reflow-temperature capability must be confirmed.

The main limitations are important:

- enough paste volume must be available to fill the PTH and form the required joint;
- the stencil/printing method must provide that extra volume without creating nearby SMT print problems;
- the THT component body must tolerate the reflow thermal profile;
- lead/hole geometry and insertion must be suitable;
- the process must be verified for the actual product.

PiP is therefore a **route option**, not a universal replacement for wave or selective soldering.

## 4.20.9 - Route Planning Is a Constraint Check

A practical process planner can organize the decision around the following questions.

| Decision factor | Question to ask | Typical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Component side** | Which parts are on Side 1 and Side 2? | determines flipping, second reflow, and underside exposure |
| **Package / mounting type** | Is each part SMT, soldered THT, press-fit, or another secondary part? | determines the joining process |
| **Thermal exposure** | Can the component and existing joints tolerate the next heating step? | may change side order or joining method |
| **Gravity / retention** | Will first-side components remain secure during second reflow? | may change side assignment or require adhesive/support |
| **Wave compatibility** | Can the underside safely contact the wave? | determines wave versus pallet/selective/local process |
| **Access / keep-out** | Can the wave opening, selective nozzle, iron, fixture, and inspection system reach the joint? | may make a theoretically possible process impractical |
| **Fixture / pallet need** | Does the board need support or local masking? | adds tooling and setup requirements |
| **Volume / product mix** | Is the same route repeated at high volume or changed frequently? | affects the value of automated versus manual/local processes |
| **Inspection / test sequence** | Where should process evidence be collected before later steps hide or complicate the defect? | affects where SPI, AOI, X-ray, electrical, or visual checks are placed |
| **Rework / secondary access** | Will the route block later access to a connector, fastener, joint, or serviceable part? | may change assembly order |

A good route does not merely make every connection once. It makes the connections in an order that remains **physically accessible, thermally compatible, inspectable, repeatable, and protected from avoidable disturbance**.

---

## 4.20.10 - Inspection Should Follow the Process Risk

Inspection and test methods are covered in detail in Chapter 5, but process planning still needs basic verification points.

Examples include:

- inspect solder-paste deposits before placement when SPI is part of the process;
- verify placement/reflow results before covering or obstructing critical areas;
- inspect THT insertion before soldering when polarity, seating, retention, or lead condition matters;
- inspect the result of wave, selective, hand, or PiP soldering before later hardware blocks access;
- perform downstream electrical or functional test where required by the product plan.

The planning principle is:

> **Collect useful evidence while the process condition is still visible and before the next operation makes the problem harder to isolate.**

Detailed acceptance criteria, test strategy, SPC, and disposition decisions belong in Chapter 5.

## 4.20.11 - Bounded Process-Planning Case

Consider an instructional PCBA with the following features:

- about **70 top-side SMT components**, including one BGA and several QFN/SOIC packages;
- about **18 small bottom-side SMT passives**;
- one **20-pin THT connector** near the board edge;
- one **2-lead THT power component**;
- no requirement to intentionally wave-solder the bottom-side SMT;
- enough open space around the THT connector for a selective-solder nozzle.

The factory has a standard SMT line, a selective-soldering machine, hand-solder stations, and a wave-soldering machine.

### Step 1 - Choose the SMT side order

Place the lighter bottom-side passives first and reflow them on **Side 1**. Then flip the board and assemble the more complex/heavier top-side SMT on **Side 2**.

This gives the top-side BGA and larger packages the second reflow pass rather than hanging underneath during that pass.

### Step 2 - Insert the THT components after SMT reflow

Insert the connector and power component after both SMT sides are complete. This avoids placing tall THT parts in the way of printing, placement, board flipping, and reflow handling.

### Step 3 - Choose the THT soldering method

A full wave process is possible only if the bottom-side SMT can be protected by suitable pallet/tooling and the board geometry supports the wave route.

Selective soldering is a reasonable starting choice for this case because:

- only two THT component groups need soldering;
- existing bottom-side SMT should remain outside molten-solder contact;
- the connector has usable nozzle access;
- the local process avoids exposing the full underside to the wave.

The resulting route is:

> **bottom SMT print/place/reflow -> flip -> top SMT print/place/reflow -> THT insertion -> selective solder -> downstream verification**

Useful checkpoints would normally be placed after each reflow pass and again after THT soldering so that defects are found before later operations make them harder to isolate. The detailed inspection method and acceptance plan belongs in Chapter 5.

### Step 4 - Check alternatives instead of assuming the first route is final

Before releasing the plan, ask:

- Is the 20-pin connector qualified for pin-in-paste reflow?
- Can the stencil provide enough PiP paste volume without creating nearby print problems?
- Is selective-nozzle access still clear after all THT parts are inserted?
- Would a pallet make wave soldering more efficient at the expected production volume?
- Does the 2-lead power component require a different local soldering method because of thermal mass or access?

These questions turn the process plan into an engineering decision rather than a memorized sequence. The selected route is appropriate to this case; another board with different underside clearance, component thermal limits, or production volume could justify a different route.

## 4.20.12 - A Practical Route-Planning Checklist

Before approving a mixed or double-sided assembly route, confirm:

### Board and component arrangement

- SMT/THT classification is correct;
- component side assignment is known;
- tall/heavy/heat-sensitive parts have been identified;
- bottom-side SMT near later soldering operations has been identified.

### Thermal sequence

- first-side components can tolerate the second reflow pass;
- moisture/reflow controls are satisfied;
- the board state used for profiling represents the actual pass;
- unnecessary thermal cycles have been avoided.

### THT joining method

- wave exposure is compatible with the underside, or required pallet/masking is available;
- selective-nozzle access and keep-out are available if selective soldering is used;
- hand soldering is limited to appropriate low-volume/special joints;
- PiP is used only for compatible components and validated paste/PTH geometry.

### Access and downstream operations

- fixtures/supports do not block required joints;
- inspection can occur before important evidence is hidden;
- later test, depanelization, secondary hardware, and rework access have been considered.

If one item fails, revise the route before changing process settings.

---

## 4.20.13 - Section Takeaway

Mixed and double-sided PCB assembly is mainly a **sequencing and compatibility problem**.

The most important practical points are:

- single-sided SMT commonly uses one print/place/reflow sequence;
- double-sided SMT commonly uses two placement/reflow passes, so first-side joints can re-melt during the second pass;
- component retention during second reflow depends on component mass, solder-joint geometry, surface tension, support, and process conditions;
- mixed SMT/THT assembly usually adds THT insertion and a suitable joining process after the main SMT operations;
- wave soldering works best when broad underside exposure is compatible with the board;
- selective or hand soldering localizes the THT joining process when only selected joints should receive solder/heat;
- bottom-side SMT that is intentionally wave soldered requires a different strategy from already-reflowed SMT that must be protected from the wave;
- pin-in-paste can integrate selected THT joints into the reflow process when solder volume and component thermal compatibility are verified;
- the route should remain physically accessible, thermally compatible, inspectable, and repeatable.

After the primary soldering route is complete, the PCBA may still require mechanical operations. **Section 4.21 moves to depanelization and secondary assembly**, including board separation, press-fit or odd-form hardware, shields, brackets, heat-sink hardware, labels, and related post-solder operations.
