# 4.26 - Integrated Factory-Floor Case: Plan, Build, and Troubleshoot

The previous sections developed the assembly process one piece at a time: material control, solder-paste printing, placement, reflow, THT assembly, mixed-technology planning, and first-board readiness. A manufacturing technologist must eventually bring those pieces together.

This section uses one simplified factory-floor case to answer six practical questions:

1. What process route should be used?
2. Which major machines or stations are required?
3. What setup information must be correct before the build starts?
4. What does the supplied process evidence say about the observed problem?
5. What should be checked next?
6. Is the build ready to continue?

The goal is **engineering judgment**, not expert process optimization.

> **Factory-floor rule:** Start with the product and the evidence. Choose the process that fits the board, stop when required evidence does not agree, and make the smallest logical correction before continuing.

---

## 4.26.1 - Case Brief: Industrial Sensor Controller Rev B

A manufacturing line is preparing to build an **Industrial Sensor Controller, Rev B**. The board uses both SMT and THT components.

The assembly contains:

- small SMT resistors and capacitors;
- one QFN microcontroller;
- one BGA memory device with hidden solder joints;
- four small 0603 capacitors on the bottom side;
- one 2 x 8 THT connector mounted from the top side.

The bottom side contains only the four small 0603 capacitors. The top side contains the main SMT population, including the QFN, BGA, resistors, capacitors, and the body of the THT connector.

The available factory processes are:

- solder-paste printing;
- 3D solder-paste inspection (SPI);
- pick-and-place;
- convection reflow;
- manual or assisted THT insertion;
- selective soldering;
- visual/AOI inspection;
- X-ray inspection when required for hidden joints.

The factory can also perform wave soldering, but the underside contains SMT components. Broad wave exposure would therefore require additional compatibility and protection planning. For this case, the released process plan confirms adequate selective-solder nozzle access to the J1 pins and the required local keep-out around that soldering operation.

## 4.26.3 - Stop and Make the Six Decisions

Before reading the worked analysis, use the supplied information to answer these questions.

1. **Process route:** What is a reasonable assembly sequence?
2. **Major equipment:** Which machines or stations are needed?
3. **Setup inputs:** What must be verified at each major process?
4. **Problem interpretation:** Which process stage is most strongly supported by the evidence?
5. **Next evidence/check:** What should be checked before changing the process?
6. **Production readiness:** Should the board continue when one required first-board check fails?

The remaining subsections work through the same six decisions. For self-study, record your answer before reading each worked decision.

A useful final answer should not merely name a process. It should connect the **product geometry**, **controlled setup**, and **available evidence**.

---

## 4.26.4 - Decision 1: Choose a Reasonable Process Route

Because the bottom side contains only four small 0603 capacitors, a reasonable route is:

1. **bottom side:** print -> SPI -> place -> reflow;
2. flip the board;
3. **top side:** print -> SPI -> place -> reflow;
4. insert THT connector J1;
5. selectively solder J1;
6. perform the required post-assembly inspection and verification.

This route makes the bottom side the first SMT pass. The first-side components are small and light, so they are reasonable candidates to remain on the underside during the second reflow pass.

With the same reflow alloy on both sides, the first-side joints can be heated above liquidus and re-melt during the second reflow. That controlled second-pass reflow is not automatically a process failure. The engineering questions are whether the first-side components remain physically retained and whether the assembly stays within the qualified thermal process.

J1 is inserted after the SMT reflow passes because the connector body and protruding pins could interfere with stencil contact, board support, placement-head clearance, board handling, or reflow transport.

Selective soldering is a reasonable THT process for this case because:

- only one local connector needs THT soldering;
- bottom-side SMT is already present;
- the selective nozzle can physically access the connector pins;
- localized solder application avoids the broad underside solder contact of a full wave process.

This does not mean selective soldering is always better than wave soldering. A different board geometry, THT count, production volume, or pallet strategy could lead to another valid route.

## 4.26.5 - Decision 2: Identify the Major Machines and Stations

The selected route can now be translated directly into factory-floor equipment.

| Process step | Major equipment / station | Main purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **SMT print** | solder-paste printer | deposit controlled paste through the correct stencil |
| **paste verification** | SPI | check paste deposits before components are placed |
| **SMT placement** | pick-and-place machine | place the correct parts at the programmed locations and orientations |
| **SMT soldering** | reflow oven | heat the assembly through the required thermal process |
| **THT insertion** | manual/assisted insertion station | install and verify J1 before soldering |
| **THT soldering** | selective-solder machine | flux, preheat, and solder the selected J1 pins |
| **visible inspection** | visual/AOI as applicable | check visible component and solder conditions |
| **hidden-joint inspection** | X-ray when required | inspect solder joints hidden under U2 |

A manufacturing route should name the **physical equipment that creates or verifies the condition**, not only the process name.

---

## 4.26.6 - Decision 3: Identify the Key Setup Inputs

Each process needs a small set of controlled inputs. A line can contain the right equipment and still build the wrong product if these inputs do not agree.

| Process | Key setup inputs to verify |
|---|---|
| **printing** | correct product/revision and side; correct stencil; correct paste identity/status; board support; printer program/recipe; board orientation |
| **SPI** | correct inspection program; correct board side/orientation; applicable product-specific limits |
| **placement** | correct placement program; feeder/material assignment; nozzles/tools; component library/orientation; fiducials and board support |
| **reflow** | correct oven recipe; oven ready/stable; applicable profile evidence for the board state being processed |
| **THT insertion** | correct J1 identity; correct orientation/polarity or keying; correct seating; leads accessible for soldering |
| **selective soldering** | correct program; nozzle/tool access; flux/preheat setup; board alignment; soldering path for J1 |
| **inspection** | correct board/revision; correct inspection method/program when automated; hidden-joint method available when required |

The checklist is not asking the technologist to optimize every machine parameter. It is asking whether the **correct controlled setup exists for this product and pass**.

## 4.26.7 - First-Board Evidence Packet

After the first board completes the bottom-side pass and reaches the top-side SMT pass, the following first-board evidence is available.

| Evidence source | Supplied result | Initial interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **SPI, R17 Pad A** | **63% of nominal paste volume** | outside the supplied 80-120% case limit |
| **SPI, R17 Pad B** | **98% of nominal paste volume** | inside the supplied case limit |
| **pre-reflow placement image** | R17 centered; correct component and orientation | no strong placement evidence for the later lift |
| **measured reflow profile near R17** | peak **241 °C**; TAL **66 s above 217 °C** | inside the supplied 235-245 °C / 45-90 s window |
| **post-reflow AOI** | R17 is tombstoned | physical defect observed after reflow |
| **U2 top-side optical view** | package position appears reasonable | hidden solder joints still cannot be judged from the top view alone |

The strongest abnormal evidence in the packet is that **Pad A received much less paste than Pad B**.

The two deposits differ by:

$$
98\%-63\%=35\text{ percentage points}
$$

That difference is not itself a universal tombstoning limit. It simply makes the print imbalance obvious in this case.

## 4.26.8 - Decisions 4 and 5: Troubleshoot from Evidence

Section 4.16 used the recurring troubleshooting sequence:

> **observation -> evidence -> likely mechanism -> upstream check -> next action**

Apply that sequence to R17.

### Observation

R17 is tombstoned after reflow.

### Evidence

- Pad A paste volume = 63% of nominal;
- Pad B paste volume = 98% of nominal;
- the supplied SPI criterion is 80-120%;
- pre-reflow placement is centered;
- the supplied nearby measured reflow peak and TAL are within the case window; this does not prove every board location is thermally identical, but it makes an oven-first response poorly supported by the evidence provided.

### Likely mechanism

A tombstone develops when the two ends of a small component experience an unbalanced force during solder melting and wetting.

The available evidence shows a strong **paste-deposit imbalance** before placement. Unequal solder volume can contribute to unequal wetting-force development during reflow and is therefore the strongest upstream clue in this bounded case.

### Upstream process to check

The strongest evidence points first to the **printing process**.

Reasonable next checks include:

- the two stencil apertures for R17;
- partial clogging or poor paste release;
- stencil underside contamination;
- local stencil-to-board contact/gasketing;
- print alignment at R17;
- board support beneath the local area.

The evidence does **not** support changing the oven first. The nearby measured profile is already inside the supplied case limits, and the placement image does not show a gross placement error.

### Next action

A reasonable response is:

1. hold the board/process at the appropriate point;
2. inspect and correct the print-related condition;
3. clean/reprint or use the controlled recovery method allowed by the local process;
4. verify both R17 deposits with SPI;
5. repeat any downstream verification invalidated by the correction;
6. confirm the post-reflow result before authorizing normal production.

This is the same evidence-based logic used throughout the chapter:

> **Do not change the last process simply because the defect became visible after the last process.**

---

## 4.26.9 - Decision 6: Recognize the Production-Readiness Problem

The R17 problem is not only a solder-defect problem.

The supplied SPI result already showed Pad A at **63%**, below the case requirement of **80%**. If the board was allowed to continue into placement and reflow without an authorized response, the production-readiness system failed to act on available evidence.

The correct logic should have been:

> **SPI fail -> hold -> investigate/correct -> reprint as required -> SPI pass -> continue**

This is exactly why first-board checks are gates rather than documentation exercises.

The production-readiness issue is therefore:

> **A required first-board verification failed, but the process continued downstream.**

That is a stronger finding than simply saying "R17 tombstoned." The tombstone is the defect observation; continuing after the failed SPI gate is the readiness-control failure.

Even if the final board had looked acceptable, ignoring the failed gate would still be poor process control.

---

## 4.26.10 - Hidden-Joint Awareness: U2 Is a Different Inspection Problem

U2 is a BGA. Its solder balls are underneath the package body.

A top-side optical image can confirm useful things such as:

- package presence;
- gross position;
- orientation marking;
- obvious package damage.

It cannot directly show the condition of the hidden solder joints.

If the inspection plan requires internal solder-joint verification, X-ray is an appropriate method for examining hidden conditions such as bridges/shorts, insufficient or missing connections, voiding, and head-in-pillow indications.

This section does not set BGA workmanship acceptance criteria. Detailed inspection strategy and acceptance belong to Chapter 5.

The practical lesson is:

> **Use an inspection method that can physically observe the feature you need to judge.**

---

## 4.26.11 - Final Integrated Build Decision

After the print-related cause at R17 is corrected, the team should not skip directly to normal production.

A reasonable restart sequence is:

1. verify the corrected print condition;
2. confirm R17 passes SPI using the supplied job limits;
3. place the board and verify the first placement;
4. reflow using the approved recipe with applicable profile evidence;
5. inspect the post-reflow result;
6. complete THT insertion and selective soldering;
7. perform the required visible inspection and any hidden-joint inspection required by the inspection plan;
8. close any remaining hold condition;
9. authorize normal production according to the local procedure.

The exact signoff system may differ among factories. The engineering logic does not:

> **correct -> reverify -> repeat affected downstream checks -> release only when the required evidence agrees**

## 4.26.13 - Case Takeaway

This case connects the major Chapter 4 skills into one factory-floor decision process.

You should now be able to:

- select a reasonable route for a mixed SMT/THT board;
- explain why light first-side SMT components can be a practical choice for a double-reflow route;
- recognize when selective soldering is a reasonable THT process for a board that already contains bottom-side SMT;
- identify the major machines and setup inputs required by the route;
- interpret SPI, placement, reflow-profile, and post-reflow evidence together;
- recognize that a visible post-reflow defect does not automatically identify the process that caused it;
- choose the next evidence/check before adjusting the process;
- recognize a failed first-board gate as a production-readiness issue;
- understand why a BGA needs hidden-joint inspection awareness;
- use the recurring engineering pattern:

> **plan the route -> verify the setup -> build the first board -> read the evidence -> hold when required -> correct -> reverify -> continue**

Section 4.27 summarizes the complete Chapter 4 learning path from factory-floor process recognition through production readiness.
