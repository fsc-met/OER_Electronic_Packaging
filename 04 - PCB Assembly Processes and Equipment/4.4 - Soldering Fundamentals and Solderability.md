# 4.4 - Soldering Fundamentals and Solderability

Section 4.3 distinguished SMT, THT, mixed-technology, and other board-level attachment approaches. Many of those processes use solder, but the equipment is easier to understand after the basic joining physics is clear.

A useful first model is:

> **solderable surfaces + suitable flux + sufficient thermal input + molten solder -> wetting + interfacial reaction -> solidified solder joint**

The important point is that soldering is not simply "melting metal onto a joint." The solder must wet the intended surfaces and form a metallurgical connection.

---

## 4.4.1 - Soldering Is Metallurgical Joining, Not Adhesive Bonding

In normal electronics soldering, the **solder alloy** melts while the PCB pad and component termination remain solid.

The molten solder then interacts with the exposed metal surfaces. A successful soldered connection requires:

- surfaces that are solderable;
- enough flux activity to remove or control surface oxides;
- enough thermal energy at the joint;
- molten solder that wets the intended surfaces;
- interfacial reaction between the solder and the joined metals;
- controlled solidification without disturbing the joint.

A solder joint provides an **electrical connection** and a **mechanical attachment**. In some products, it also contributes to heat conduction between a component and the PCB.

A hardened blob of solder is not automatically a good solder joint. If the solder did not wet the pad or termination, the metal may be present without a reliable metallurgical connection.

> **Practical rule:** Solder must wet the intended metal surfaces. Simply placing molten solder next to a component lead or pad is not enough.

---

## 4.4.2 - The Solder Alloy Sets the Basic Temperature Scale

Solder is a filler metal chosen to melt at a temperature well below the melting temperatures of the PCB copper and most component terminations.

Two alloys are useful for building temperature intuition.

| Representative alloy | Composition | Phase-change temperature | Engineering context |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **Sn63/Pb37** | 63% tin, 37% lead | **183 °C** | eutectic tin-lead alloy; now mainly legacy or used where an applicable exemption/special requirement permits it |
| **SAC305** | 96.5% tin, 3.0% silver, 0.5% copper | about **217-220 °C** | common lead-free electronics solder alloy |

For the eutectic Sn63/Pb37 alloy, melting and solidification occur at approximately one temperature, 183 °C. SAC305 has a small solidus-to-liquidus range instead of one eutectic melting point.

These temperatures describe the **solder alloy**, not the required setting of an oven, soldering iron, wave pot, or other machine. The equipment must transfer enough heat to the actual joint while staying within the limits of the PCB, components, flux chemistry, and qualified process.

Many modern electronic products use lead-free solder because regulations such as the European Union RoHS framework restrict lead in electrical and electronic equipment. Exemptions and specialized applications still exist, so "lead-free" should not be treated as a universal statement about every electronic assembly.

> **Representative scale, not a process recipe:** The melting range helps explain why a lead-free SAC305 process normally requires a higher joint temperature than a Sn63/Pb37 process. Detailed reflow and soldering-process temperatures belong in later sections.

---

## 4.4.3 - Oxides and Contamination Can Block Wetting

Metal surfaces exposed to air do not remain perfectly clean. Oxides form naturally, and handling or storage can also introduce contamination.

Possible surface problems include:

- oxide or tarnish;
- fingerprints and oils;
- dust or process contamination;
- aged or damaged surface finish;
- contamination on a component termination.

These conditions matter because molten solder must make intimate contact with a solderable metal surface.

A PCB surface finish from Chapter 2 helps protect the underlying copper and provides a surface intended for later assembly, but surface condition can still change through handling, storage, thermal exposure, or contamination.

If the surface condition is poor, molten solder may:

- remain rounded instead of spreading;
- wet only part of the pad or termination;
- pull back from an area that initially appeared wet;
- form an irregular or incomplete connection.

This is why soldering problems cannot be diagnosed only by asking whether the solder became hot enough. Surface condition and chemistry matter too.

---

## 4.4.4 - Flux Prepares and Protects the Soldering Surfaces

**Flux** is the chemical system used to support wetting during soldering.

At an introductory level, flux has three important jobs:

1. **remove or reduce oxides** on the surfaces being joined;
2. **limit re-oxidation** while the surfaces are being heated;
3. **promote wetting** by helping molten solder contact the prepared metal surfaces.

Flux chemistry must be compatible with the solder alloy, surface finishes, process temperature, and product requirements.

Flux is not a universal repair material. It cannot make every contaminated, damaged, incompatible, or badly degraded surface solderable.

More flux is also not automatically better. Depending on the flux chemistry and process:

- insufficient or exhausted flux can contribute to poor wetting;
- excessive or inappropriate flux can leave unwanted residue, increase process variability, or contribute to defects such as spatter or voiding.

Detailed flux classifications, solder-paste chemistry, cleaning decisions, and residue requirements are intentionally deferred to later sections.

---

## 4.4.5 - Wetting, Surface Tension, and Contact Angle

Molten solder naturally tends to minimize its free surface area. This behavior is associated with **surface tension** and helps explain why a drop of molten solder tends to form a rounded shape.

During soldering, two tendencies compete:

- **cohesion** within the molten solder tends to keep the liquid together;
- interaction between the solder and the prepared solid surface promotes **wetting** and spreading.

**Wetting** means that the molten solder spreads over and adheres to the intended solderable surface.

A useful visual indicator is the **contact angle**, usually written as $\theta$.

- A smaller contact angle generally indicates better spreading and wetting.
- A large contact angle indicates that the solder is remaining more bead-like and wetting poorly.
- Exact acceptance cannot be assigned from one universal contact-angle number; joint geometry, materials, process conditions, and applicable workmanship requirements matter.

Three related conditions should be distinguished:

| Condition | What happens physically | What you may observe |
|---|---|---|
| **Wetting** | molten solder spreads over and adheres to the solderable surface | smooth transition onto the intended metal surface; relatively low contact angle |
| **Non-wetting** | molten solder does not establish effective wetting on an area | rounded/beaded solder, incomplete coverage, large contact angle |
| **Dewetting** | solder initially wets an area and then retracts | irregular recession of solder, exposed areas, uneven remaining solder |

Surface tension is not simply "good" or "bad." It helps shape solder fillets and can contribute to component self-alignment during reflow, but unbalanced wetting forces can also move small components. Those process-specific effects are developed later in the chapter.

## 4.4.6 - A Metallurgical Interface Forms During Soldering

When soldering is successful, the interface is more than mechanical contact between two metals.

A simplified sequence is:

1. flux prepares and protects the solderable surfaces;
2. the solder reaches its molten state;
3. molten solder wets the pad and component termination;
4. atoms diffuse and the molten solder reacts with the surface metal;
5. a thin **intermetallic compound (IMC)** region forms at the interface;
6. the solder solidifies and the metallurgical joint remains.

At a copper/tin-rich interface, copper-tin intermetallic phases commonly form. The exact phases, thickness, and morphology depend on the surface finish, solder alloy, time, temperature, and prior thermal history.

The key beginner-level idea is:

> **Some intermetallic formation is necessary for a soldered metallurgical bond, but more intermetallic is not automatically better.**

Excessive thermal exposure can promote additional intermetallic growth and metal dissolution. Detailed intermetallic kinetics and long-term reliability analysis belong in Chapter 9.

## 4.4.7 - The Joint Must Receive the Right Thermal Input

A soldering machine controls a heat source, but the **joint temperature** is what determines whether the solder and flux experience the intended thermal conditions.

Heat can reach a solder joint in different ways:

- from a soldering-iron tip through the lead, pad, and solder;
- from hot circulating gas during reflow;
- from preheating and contact with molten solder in wave or selective soldering;
- from other qualified local heating methods.

The heat-source temperature is not automatically the same as the temperature of the solder joint. Board copper area, component mass, contact quality, heating time, airflow, tooling, and neighboring structures all affect heat transfer.

A useful three-state model is:

| Thermal condition | Possible result |
|---|---|
| **Too little heat / too little time** | flux may not activate as intended; solder may not fully melt; wetting may be slow or incomplete |
| **Appropriate thermal input** | surfaces are prepared, solder becomes molten, wetting and interfacial reaction occur, and the joint solidifies after the heat is removed |
| **Too much heat / too much time** | flux may be exhausted or degraded; PCB/components can be damaged; excessive dissolution or IMC growth can occur; warpage or other thermal damage may increase |

For example, a SAC305 joint at 210 °C is still below the alloy's normal 217-220 °C melting range. That does **not** mean an oven or soldering iron should simply be set to 220 °C; the machine setting must account for how heat is transferred to the joint and for the qualified limits of the complete assembly.

## 4.4.8 - Hand Soldering and Reflow Use the Same Basic Physics

Hand soldering and reflow look very different on the factory floor, but the basic joining requirements are the same.

| Feature | Hand soldering | Reflow soldering |
|---|---|---|
| **How heat reaches the joint** | mainly by local conduction from a heated tip | mainly by controlled heating of the assembly, commonly with forced hot gas |
| **Where solder/flux come from** | commonly flux-cored solder wire plus any approved additional flux | solder paste already contains solder-alloy powder and a flux medium |
| **What must happen** | surfaces heat, flux acts, solder melts, solder wets, joint solidifies | surfaces heat, flux acts, paste alloy melts, solder wets, joint solidifies |
| **What can still go wrong** | poor surface condition, inadequate heat transfer, exhausted/inappropriate flux, movement during solidification | poor surface condition, unsuitable paste/flux condition, inadequate or excessive thermal exposure, disturbed or unbalanced wetting |

The same fundamentals also apply to wave and selective soldering even though the equipment delivers solder and heat differently.

This is useful for troubleshooting: a process may use a very different machine, but the engineer can still ask whether the surfaces were solderable, the flux was effective, sufficient heat reached the joint, wetting occurred, and the joint solidified under control.

---

## 4.4.9 - Solderability Is Not the Same as Process Quality

**Solderability** is the ability of a metal surface to be wetted by molten solder under specified soldering or test conditions.

Both sides of a connection matter:

- the PCB land or plated-hole surface;
- the component termination, lead, or pin.

Solderability can be affected by:

- surface-finish type and condition;
- oxidation or tarnish;
- handling contamination;
- storage time and environment;
- previous thermal exposure;
- component termination condition.

The soldering process also matters, but the two ideas should not be confused.

> **A solderable part can still be soldered poorly if the process is wrong, and a well-controlled process cannot always rescue a surface with poor solderability.**

Industry standards such as J-STD-002 and J-STD-003 define solderability test methods for component terminations and printed boards. This section uses them only for terminology awareness; product acceptance must follow the applicable drawing, specification, standard revision, and customer requirements.

---

## 4.4.10 - What Would You Check Next?

A visible solder condition is evidence, not a complete root-cause diagnosis.

| Observation | Possible mechanisms to consider | Reasonable next checks |
|---|---|---|
| solder beads up and does not spread on one area | oxidation/contamination, ineffective flux, insufficient joint heating, degraded or incompatible surface | compare surface condition, material/finish history, flux condition, and joint thermal evidence |
| one side of a connection wets while the other does not | different surface conditions, unequal heating, local flux problem | compare the two surfaces and heat paths before changing machine settings |
| solder appears to wet and then pulls back | dewetting caused by surface condition, contamination, or process/material interaction | inspect finish condition and contamination history; verify flux and thermal process |
| many similar joints show poor wetting | common material, storage, flux, or thermal-process issue | look for a shared lot, surface finish, flux/paste condition, or process condition |
| joint looks dull but otherwise has continuous wetting | appearance may be alloy/process dependent | do not judge by shine alone; compare geometry, wetting, process evidence, and the applicable workmanship requirement |

One older rule of thumb is that a "good" solder joint is shiny and a "bad" joint is dull. That is not a reliable universal test, especially for many lead-free alloys and surface conditions. Use the actual wetting geometry, process evidence, and applicable acceptance requirements instead.

The troubleshooting habit introduced in Section 4.1 still applies:

> **Observe the condition -> identify the physical mechanism -> check the available evidence -> then decide what process variable or material should be investigated.**

---

## Section Takeaway

The main ideas from this section are:

- soldering is a **metallurgical joining process**, not an adhesive process;
- the solder alloy melts while the pad and component termination normally remain solid;
- Sn63/Pb37 melts at about **183 °C**, while common SAC305 lead-free solder melts over about **217-220 °C**;
- oxides and contamination interfere with solder wetting;
- flux removes or controls oxides, protects surfaces during heating, and supports wetting;
- surface tension helps determine molten-solder shape, while wetting describes how solder spreads over and adheres to the intended surface;
- wetting, non-wetting, and dewetting are different physical conditions;
- a thin intermetallic region is part of a true metallurgical solder bond, but excessive interfacial reaction is undesirable;
- soldering requires the right thermal input at the **joint**, not simply a machine setpoint;
- solderability describes the ability of a surface to be wetted under specified conditions and is different from overall soldering-process quality;
- troubleshooting should use observed evidence before changing the process.

Section 4.5 next looks at two important handling controls that protect components before and during assembly: **electrostatic-discharge control and moisture-sensitive-device handling**.
