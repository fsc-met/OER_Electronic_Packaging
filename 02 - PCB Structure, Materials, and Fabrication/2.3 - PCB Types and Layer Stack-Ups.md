# 2.3 - PCB Types and Layer Stack-Ups

Section 2.2 introduced the physical anatomy of a printed circuit board: copper conductors, dielectric layers, plated interconnections, surface coatings, and mechanical features. The next question is how those elements are **organized into different board constructions**.

Not every PCB has the same number of copper layers or the same mechanical form. Some boards are simple and rigid. Others are flexible, combine rigid and flexible regions, use high-density interconnect structures, or place the circuit on a metal base for improved heat spreading.

For the remainder of Chapter 2, a **conventional rigid multilayer PCB** will be the primary teaching platform. Other constructions are introduced here at recognition level so that readers can identify them and understand why they may be selected.

A second important idea is the **layer stack-up**:

> **A PCB stack-up is the ordered arrangement of conductive and dielectric layers through the board thickness.**

A stack-up is not only an electrical description. It is also part of the board's physical construction and affects fabrication, thickness, warpage tendency, heat flow, mechanical behavior, and later electrical performance.

---

## 2.3.1 - Common PCB Construction Types

PCB constructions can be grouped in several useful ways. These groups are not always mutually exclusive. For example, a rigid board can also use high-density interconnect (HDI) features.

### Single-Layer Board

A **single-layer** or **single-sided** PCB has one patterned copper layer on an insulating substrate.

It is the simplest board construction discussed in this chapter.

Typical characteristics include:

- one conductive routing layer;
- simple fabrication compared with multilayer constructions;
- limited routing freedom because conductors cannot cross on separate copper layers;
- suitability for relatively simple circuits when electrical, mechanical, thermal, and packaging requirements allow.

A single-layer board is not automatically thin, weak, or low quality. Its overall behavior depends on the substrate material, thickness, copper geometry, board size, support conditions, and intended application.

### Two-Layer Board

A **two-layer** or **double-sided** PCB has patterned copper on both sides of the insulating board structure.

The two copper layers provide more routing freedom than a single-layer board. Plated holes or vias can provide electrical connection between the two sides.

A two-layer construction may support:

- routing on both outer surfaces;
- surface-mount and through-hole assembly;
- a larger number of electrical interconnections than a comparable single-layer board;
- intentional use of one surface or large copper region as a reference or power-distribution area when appropriate.

However, a two-layer board does not automatically provide a dedicated continuous ground plane. The actual copper pattern depends on the design.

### Multilayer Rigid PCB

A **multilayer rigid PCB** contains more than two conductive copper layers separated by dielectric material and bonded into one rigid structure.

Multilayer construction allows the designer to distribute different functions through the thickness of the board. Depending on the design, layers may be used for:

- signal routing;
- ground or other reference planes;
- power distribution;
- large copper regions for current carrying or heat spreading;
- combinations of these functions.

Internal electrical connections are made through plated interconnect structures such as vias. Detailed via types are covered in Section 2.6.

Multilayer construction can support high routing density and can provide dedicated plane layers, but increasing layer count also increases fabrication and documentation complexity. Layer count by itself does not determine whether a board is mechanically stiff, thermally effective, electrically well behaved, or easy to manufacture.

### Flex PCB

A **flexible printed circuit**, or **flex PCB**, uses a flexible dielectric construction so that the circuit can bend or fold as required by the product.

Flex circuits are useful when the interconnection must:

- conform to limited space;
- connect moving or separated regions;
- reduce the need for discrete wiring or connectors;
- accommodate a controlled bend or fold.

A flex circuit is not simply a very thin rigid FR-4 board. Its materials, conductor geometry, bend behavior, reinforcement, and fabrication requirements are different.

This chapter introduces flex only at recognition level. Detailed flex design is outside the scope of this book.

### Rigid-Flex PCB

A **rigid-flex PCB** integrates rigid board regions and flexible circuit regions into one interconnected construction.

The rigid regions can support components and connectors, while the flexible regions provide integrated electrical connection between rigid sections.

The important recognition-level distinction is:

> **Rigid-flex is an integrated board construction, not merely two separate rigid PCBs connected by a removable cable.**

Rigid-flex can reduce some connectors and cable assemblies, but it introduces specialized fabrication, mechanical, and reliability considerations.

### High-Density Interconnect (HDI)

**High-density interconnect (HDI)** describes a printed-board interconnection approach that uses finer features and high-density structures to support greater interconnection density.

HDI may involve features such as:

- fine conductor geometry;
- small capture lands;
- microvias;
- sequential build-up structures;
- increased interconnection density.

HDI should not be treated as a separate mechanical board category in the same way as rigid versus flex. A rigid, flex, or rigid-flex construction can incorporate HDI technology.

Detailed microvia and HDI structures are deferred to Section 2.6.

### Metal-Core / Insulated-Metal-Substrate Board

A **metal-core PCB** or **insulated metal substrate (IMS)** uses a metal base as an important part of the board construction. A dielectric layer electrically isolates the circuit copper from the metal base.

A common reason for selecting this construction is to provide a more effective path for spreading or conducting heat away from heat-generating components.

At recognition level, the structure can be thought of as:

> **circuit copper -> dielectric insulation -> metal base**

Actual metal-core and IMS constructions vary. They should not be assumed to use the same layer arrangement, materials, or fabrication sequence as the conventional rigid FR-4 multilayer boards that are the main focus of this chapter.

### Recognition-Level Comparison

| Construction | Main physical characteristic | Why it may be selected |
|---|---|---|
| **Single-layer** | One patterned copper layer | Simple interconnection and fabrication |
| **Two-layer** | Copper on both sides | More routing freedom than one copper layer |
| **Multilayer rigid** | Multiple copper and dielectric layers bonded into a rigid board | Higher routing density and dedicated internal layer functions |
| **Flex** | Flexible dielectric/circuit construction | Bending, folding, or compact interconnection |
| **Rigid-flex** | Integrated rigid and flexible regions | Component support plus built-in flexible interconnection |
| **HDI** | Fine-feature, high-density interconnection structures | Higher connection density in limited area |
| **Metal-core / IMS** | Circuit electrically insulated from a metal base | Improved heat-spreading path in suitable applications |

The table is intentionally qualitative. Choosing a board construction requires balancing product requirements, fabrication capability, cost, electrical needs, thermal needs, mechanical constraints, and reliability.

## 2.3.2 - What a Layer Stack-Up Describes

For a multilayer board, saying that the PCB has "four layers" or "six layers" is not enough to describe its construction.

A **stack-up** identifies the vertical order of conductive and dielectric layers and, in a fabrication definition, normally also includes information such as:

- copper-layer order;
- dielectric materials;
- dielectric thicknesses;
- copper thicknesses;
- core and prepreg construction;
- total board thickness;
- special controlled requirements where applicable.

At this point in the chapter, the focus is the **organization and engineering meaning** of the stack-up rather than detailed fabrication specifications.

### Copper Layers

Copper layers can serve different roles.

A layer may contain:

- signal traces;
- power-distribution copper;
- a ground or other electrical reference plane;
- a combination of copper features.

The labels **signal layer**, **ground plane**, and **power plane** describe intended electrical functions. They do not mean that every point on a particular layer must always contain copper.

#### Signal Layers

A **signal layer** is used primarily for routed conductors that carry electrical signals between circuit locations.

Signal layers may be:

- outer layers;
- internal layers;
- mixed with other copper functions when appropriate.

Detailed routing, return-path, impedance, crosstalk, and signal-integrity rules are deferred to Chapter 5.

#### Ground and Power Planes

A **plane** is a relatively large copper region. Multilayer boards often use one or more internal layers as ground/reference planes or power-distribution planes.

Plane layers can contribute to:

- electrical reference and return paths;
- power distribution;
- electromagnetic behavior;
- heat spreading;
- copper distribution within the board.

These functions are coupled. The electrical design of reference planes is treated in Chapter 5, while thermal effects are developed in Section 2.11 and Chapter 6.

## 2.3.3 - An Illustrative Four-Layer Stack-Up

A four-layer rigid board provides a useful example because it is complex enough to show the purpose of internal layers without becoming difficult to visualize.

One **illustrative** arrangement is:

| Layer / region | Example role |
|---|---|
| Top copper - Layer 1 | Signal routing and component connection |
| Prepreg-derived dielectric | Electrical insulation and spacing between L1 and L2 |
| Inner copper - Layer 2 | Ground or reference plane |
| Core dielectric | Electrical insulation, spacing between L2 and L3, and part of the board structure |
| Inner copper - Layer 3 | Power or other reference/distribution plane |
| Prepreg-derived dielectric | Electrical insulation and spacing between L3 and L4 |
| Bottom copper - Layer 4 | Signal routing and component connection |

This is an example, not a universal four-layer stack-up.

A real four-layer board may use different layer functions, dielectric thicknesses, copper weights, materials, and ordering based on the product requirements and the fabricator's process.

## 2.3.4 - Dielectric Spacing Matters

The distance between copper layers is established by the dielectric construction.

In the illustrative four-layer stack-up, the spacing between L1 and L2 and between L3 and L4 is formed by prepreg-derived dielectric, while the spacing between L2 and L3 is formed by the core. **All three regions provide dielectric spacing between adjacent copper layers.**

Dielectric spacing affects several engineering behaviors:

- the total board thickness;
- electrical coupling between conductive layers;
- controlled-impedance possibilities;
- heat flow through the board thickness;
- mechanical construction and stress distribution;
- manufacturability of the selected stack.

A smaller spacing is not automatically better, and a larger spacing is not automatically stronger. The effect depends on the materials, layer functions, copper geometry, overall board thickness, and application.

Detailed dielectric properties such as dielectric constant, thermal conductivity, coefficient of thermal expansion, and glass-transition temperature are introduced in Section 2.4.

Detailed electrical stack-up design is deferred to Chapter 5.

## 2.3.5 - Stack-Up Symmetry

A multilayer board is commonly designed with attention to **symmetry about its mid-plane**.

At an introductory level, a symmetric stack-up means that the overall construction above and below the mid-plane is reasonably balanced with respect to such factors as:

- dielectric materials;
- dielectric thicknesses;
- copper-layer placement;
- copper thickness;
- overall construction.

Symmetry is important because the board is processed through lamination and later experiences additional thermal cycles during assembly. Materials and copper regions do not all expand, contract, and stiffen the structure in exactly the same way.

When the construction is strongly asymmetric, the resulting internal forces can create a greater tendency for the board to curve or twist.

> **Stack-up symmetry helps reduce the tendency for thermally and process-induced deformation, but it does not guarantee a perfectly flat board.**

Warpage also depends on material properties, copper pattern, processing history, board geometry, and later assembly conditions. Detailed warpage behavior is developed in Section 2.10.

### Symmetry Does Not Mean Every Layer Is Identical

A symmetric board does **not** require the top copper pattern to be an exact mirror image of the bottom copper pattern.

Instead, the engineering goal is to avoid large, unnecessary imbalance in the construction.

For example, a board may have:

- different signal routes on the top and bottom;
- different local copper shapes;
- different component footprints;

while still using a reasonably symmetric overall laminate construction.

## 2.3.6 - Copper Balance

**Copper balance** refers to managing the amount and distribution of copper so that the board is not strongly unbalanced from one side, layer, or region to another.

Copper balance matters because copper differs from the surrounding dielectric materials in:

- stiffness;
- thermal expansion;
- thermal conductivity;
- manufacturing behavior.

Large copper imbalance can contribute to:

- uneven dimensional change during processing;
- localized stress;
- uneven plating or etching conditions in some processes;
- increased tendency toward distortion or warpage.

Copper balance is related to stack-up symmetry, but the two terms are not identical.

- **Stack-up symmetry** concerns the overall vertical construction.
- **Copper balance** concerns how copper amount and distribution are arranged within that construction.

Detailed copper balancing and thieving concepts are introduced in Section 2.5.

## 2.3.7 - Layer Ordering Is an Engineering Decision

The order of signal layers, reference planes, power-distribution regions, cores, and prepreg-derived dielectric regions affects several parts of PCB behavior simultaneously.

Layer ordering can influence:

### Electrical behavior

- reference paths;
- coupling between layers;
- electromagnetic field distribution;
- controlled-impedance design.

### Thermal behavior

- how effectively copper spreads heat laterally;
- how far heat must travel through dielectric regions;
- whether thermal vias can connect useful copper regions.

### Mechanical behavior

- stress distribution through the board thickness;
- symmetry;
- warpage tendency;
- local bending response.

### Manufacturing behavior

- lamination construction;
- drilling and plating requirements;
- registration;
- process complexity;
- yield and cost.

This is why a PCB stack-up should be treated as an **engineering construction**, not merely a list of layer numbers.

---

## 2.3.8 - Why Layer Count Alone Is Not Enough

It is tempting to assume:

- more layers = stiffer board;
- more layers = better cooling;
- more layers = better electrical performance;
- more layers = better PCB.

Those statements are too simple.

### Mechanical stiffness

Board bending stiffness depends strongly on overall thickness, material properties, board geometry, copper placement, and support conditions.

A six-layer board can be thinner and less stiff than a thicker two-layer board.

Detailed first-order stiffness analysis is developed in Section 2.10.

### Thermal performance

Additional layers may provide opportunities for more copper planes and thermal paths, but cooling depends on:

- copper amount;
- copper continuity;
- layer location;
- dielectric thickness and thermal conductivity;
- thermal vias;
- component-to-board thermal interfaces;
- the downstream path from the board to the environment.

Adding layers without creating a useful heat-flow path does not automatically improve cooling.

### Electrical performance

Additional layers can provide routing space and dedicated reference planes, but electrical performance depends on how the layers are organized and used.

A poorly planned multilayer stack-up can still create electrical problems.

Detailed electrical stack-up design belongs in Chapter 5.

### Manufacturability and cost

Additional layers can increase fabrication steps, registration requirements, process complexity, inspection needs, and cost.

A higher layer count should therefore be selected because it helps meet real product requirements, not because a larger number is inherently better.

> **Layer count provides design opportunities. Stack-up quality depends on how those layers are arranged and used.**

## 2.3.9 - Reading a Stack-Up as an Engineering Technology Student

When reviewing a PCB stack-up, do not stop at the number of layers.

Ask:

1. **How many copper layers are present?**
2. **Which layers are mainly signal layers?**
3. **Which layers contain large reference or power planes?**
4. **What dielectric regions separate the copper layers?**
5. **Is the overall construction reasonably symmetric about the mid-plane?**
6. **Is copper distribution strongly unbalanced?**
7. **What is the overall board thickness?**
8. **Are there unusual constructions such as flex regions, HDI structures, or a metal base?**
9. **Does the stack-up appear compatible with the electrical, thermal, mechanical, and manufacturing needs of the product?**
10. **Which details still need confirmation from the PCB fabricator?**

These questions help connect a stack-up drawing to real manufacturing and product behavior.

They also prepare for Section 2.13, where PCB fabrication data and supplier communication are discussed directly.

## Key Terms Introduced in This Section

**Single-layer PCB**
A printed board with one patterned conductive copper layer.

**Two-layer PCB**
A printed board with patterned copper on both sides of the board structure.

**Multilayer PCB**
A printed board containing more than two conductive layers separated by dielectric material and bonded into one construction.

**Flex PCB**
A printed circuit built on a flexible dielectric construction so that the circuit can bend or fold as required.

**Rigid-flex PCB**
An integrated printed-board construction containing both rigid and flexible regions.

**HDI**
High-density interconnect; an interconnection approach using fine features and high-density structures such as microvias to increase connection density.

**Metal-core PCB / IMS**
A PCB construction in which an electrically insulated metal base is an important part of the physical and thermal structure.

**Stack-up**
The ordered arrangement of conductive and dielectric layers through the PCB thickness.

**Signal layer**
A copper layer used primarily for routed signal conductors.

**Plane**
A relatively large copper region used for functions such as electrical reference, power distribution, or heat spreading.

**Stack-up symmetry**
Reasonable balance of the overall multilayer construction about the board mid-plane.

**Copper balance**
Management of copper amount and distribution to avoid large unnecessary imbalance across layers or board regions.

## Section Takeaway

PCB construction is more varied than simply "one layer" versus "many layers."

Important board constructions include:

- single-layer;
- two-layer;
- multilayer rigid;
- flex;
- rigid-flex;
- HDI;
- metal-core / IMS.

For the remainder of Chapter 2, the conventional rigid multilayer PCB will be the main teaching platform.

Within a multilayer board, the **stack-up** defines how conductive and dielectric layers are arranged through the thickness. Layer ordering, dielectric spacing, symmetry, and copper balance all matter because the same construction must satisfy electrical, thermal, mechanical, and manufacturing requirements.

The most important caution is:

> **More layers do not automatically mean a stiffer, cooler, electrically better, or more manufacturable PCB.**

The next section examines the **materials and properties** that give the stack-up its physical behavior, including thermal conductivity, coefficient of thermal expansion, glass-transition temperature, stiffness, moisture absorption, and dielectric behavior.

### Targeted technical corrections made during QA

#### Correction 1 - Layer count and stiffness

The lecture correctly notes later that board thickness primarily controls global bending stiffness and that layer count does not directly determine thickness.

The OER therefore avoids the earlier oversimplification that a higher layer count inherently provides better mechanical performance.

Public wording:

> A six-layer board can be thinner and less stiff than a thicker two-layer board.

This is presented qualitatively. Detailed stiffness equations remain in Section 2.10.

#### Correction 2 - Layer count and thermal performance

The lecture states that increasing layer count improves thermal performance and later explains that copper arrangement and dielectric spacing are important.

The OER uses the more technically defensible formulation:

> Additional layers may provide opportunities for more copper planes and useful thermal paths, but heat spreading depends on copper amount, continuity, placement, dielectric construction, vias, interfaces, and the complete path to the environment.

No universal "more layers = better cooling" claim remains.

#### Correction 3 - Layer count and electrical performance

The OER does not state that a multilayer PCB is automatically electrically superior.

More layers can provide dedicated reference planes and routing opportunities, but electrical performance depends on the actual stack-up and routing.

Detailed signal-integrity requirements remain deferred to Chapter 5.

#### Correction 4 - HDI classification

HDI is treated as an **interconnection technology/architecture**, not as a mutually exclusive mechanical board type.

The public text explicitly notes that rigid, flex, or rigid-flex boards may incorporate HDI features.

#### Correction 5 - Rigid-flex classification

Rigid-flex is defined as an integrated construction containing rigid and flexible regions, rather than simply separate rigid boards connected by a removable flex cable.

#### Correction 6 - Copper balance versus symmetry

The public section distinguishes:

- stack-up symmetry = balance of the vertical construction about the mid-plane;
- copper balance = management of copper amount/distribution.

The two concepts are related but not identical.

#### Correction 7 - Symmetry qualification

The section does not claim that symmetry guarantees a flat board.

It states that symmetry helps reduce deformation tendency while warpage also depends on material properties, copper pattern, processing history, geometry, and assembly conditions.
