# 2.9 - Fabrication Capabilities, Tolerances, and Producibility

Section 2.8 followed a rigid multilayer PCB through the major fabrication operations. The next question is different:

> **What geometry and tolerances can those real processes produce repeatedly, economically, and with acceptable yield?**

That question cannot be answered with one universal list of minimum dimensions.

A PCB fabricator works with real imaging, etching, drilling, lamination, plating, solder-mask, and profiling processes. Every process has variation. The practical design problem is therefore not only whether a feature is **physically possible**, but whether it can be produced **consistently enough for the required product, yield, cost, and quality level**.

This leads to a central rule for PCB engineering:

> **A manufacturing minimum is not automatically a good design target.**

A feature may be technically producible at a fabricator's stated limit while still requiring tighter control, special processing, additional inspection, lower panel utilization, or greater yield risk. Preferred or recommended capabilities usually provide more process margin.

This section therefore emphasizes **capability thinking** rather than memorizing fixed numbers.

Engineering judgment also needs a sense of scale. For that reason, the numerical values in this section are presented as **typical mainstream rigid-FR-4 fabrication ranges or common teaching defaults**. They are intended to build intuition about what is routine, aggressive, or unusually tight. They are not substitutes for the selected fabricator's current capability table or the product's governing requirements.

---

## 2.9.1 - "Minimum" Can Mean Several Different Things

PCB discussions often use words such as *minimum*, *limit*, *rule*, *tolerance*, and *requirement* as though they mean the same thing. They do not.

At least six different sources of limits should be distinguished.

| Term | What it means | Practical question |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical process limit** | A boundary imposed by process physics, equipment, materials, or process control | Can the process physically produce the feature at all? |
| **Fabricator minimum capability** | The tightest feature or tolerance a specific fabricator states it can manufacture under stated conditions | Will this supplier accept and attempt this feature? |
| **Preferred / recommended capability** | A less aggressive value that the fabricator considers more robust for normal production | What geometry gives better process margin, yield, and cost? |
| **EDA / design rule** | A rule configured in PCB design software | What does the CAD system permit or flag? |
| **Industry-standard requirement** | A design, test, material, or acceptance requirement defined by an applicable industry document | What requirement applies to this product or acceptance class? |
| **Customer / product requirement** | A requirement on the drawing, specification, purchase documentation, or product definition | What must this particular product satisfy? |

These categories can overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

For example, an EDA rule may be set to 0.10 mm trace spacing. That does **not** prove that every fabricator can manufacture 0.10 mm spacing on every copper thickness and layer construction. Conversely, a fabricator may be able to produce a feature smaller than the design rule if the rule was intentionally configured conservatively.

An industry standard also does not automatically define a supplier's manufacturing capability. Standards may define design principles, material requirements, test methods, or acceptance criteria, while the actual process capability still depends on the selected manufacturing site and process.

The customer requirement adds another layer. A product may intentionally specify a tighter dimensional, flatness, plating, or inspection requirement than the fabricator's normal default. That requirement must be agreed before production rather than assumed after the boards are built.

## 2.9.2 - Capability Is Usually Conditional

A quoted manufacturing capability should rarely be read as an isolated number.

A trace-width capability, for example, can depend on:

- starting copper thickness;
- whether the layer is inner or outer;
- etching and plating route;
- copper density and local feature geometry;
- panel construction;
- required finished copper;
- inspection and acceptance requirements.

A hole or via capability can depend on:

- finished board thickness;
- drilled diameter;
- whether the hole is plated;
- aspect ratio;
- hole type;
- material system;
- plating process;
- registration requirement.

A solder-mask capability can depend on:

- mask material and imaging process;
- pad geometry;
- mask registration;
- opening size;
- local mask-web width.

Therefore, a capability matrix is more useful than one isolated minimum value.

This is also why the phrase **"our minimum trace is X"** should trigger another question:

> **Under what board construction, copper thickness, layer type, and production conditions?**

The same fabricator can have different capability levels for different technologies or cost tiers.

## 2.9.3 - Tolerance Describes Allowed Variation, Not Process Perfection

A **nominal dimension** is the intended target value.

A **tolerance** defines how much variation from that target is allowed.

For a simple bilateral tolerance,

$$
X = X_\text{nominal} \pm T
$$

the acceptable interval is:

$$
X_\text{nominal}-T \le X \le X_\text{nominal}+T
$$

This notation does not mean that the process produces values uniformly across the whole tolerance band. Real manufacturing has a process distribution affected by equipment, tooling, materials, calibration, environment, and previous process steps.

A process can therefore be:

- centered comfortably inside the required tolerance;
- shifted toward one limit;
- more variable than expected;
- capable for one feature but marginal for another.

For producibility, the useful question is not only:

> "Is the nominal value legal?"

It is also:

> "How much manufacturing margin remains after normal variation is considered?"

A design that uses many features near the edge of capability can require more process control even if every nominal dimension individually appears acceptable.

## 2.9.4 - Trace Width and Spacing Capability

Section 2.8 showed that copper patterns are created through imaging, plating where applicable, and etching.

Those operations do not reproduce digital geometry perfectly.

For narrow traces and spaces, important contributors include:

- resist-image resolution;
- registration;
- copper thickness;
- etching behavior;
- lateral etch at conductor edges;
- plating build-up on outer layers;
- local copper distribution;
- inspection resolution and process control.

For ordinary rigid FR-4 with about 1 oz copper, a useful current mainstream reference is:

- **0.10 mm (4 mil)** trace width and spacing: common minimum capability for standard low-cost fabrication;
- **0.15 mm (6 mil)** or larger: a comfortable preferred value when routing density allows;
- approximately **0.09 mm (3.5 mil)**: available from some modern multilayer/direct-imaging processes, but it should be treated as a tighter capability rather than a portable default.

The Chapter 2 reference range of **4-6 mil (about 0.10-0.15 mm)** therefore remains a sound engineering-scale summary.

Copper thickness matters strongly. A process that can hold 0.10 mm / 0.10 mm on 1 oz copper may require appreciably wider features on 2 oz or heavier copper because more copper must be etched and outer-layer plating changes the finished geometry.

A useful tolerance sense is that finished trace width may vary by roughly **±20%** under common PCB acceptance/manufacturing practice. This does not mean every trace should be designed 20% larger; it means that nominal CAD width is not an exact physical width after fabrication.

The important trend is:

- narrower traces contain less conductor cross-section;
- narrower spacing leaves less margin for imaging or etching variation;
- thicker copper makes fine-feature etching more difficult;
- dense fine features require tighter process control.

> **Minimum capability answers "can it be made?" Preferred capability asks "can it be made robustly and economically?"**

Detailed electrical sizing of traces belongs in Chapter 5. Detailed layout DFM rules belong in Chapter 3. Here, the focus is fabrication capability.

## 2.9.5 - Drill Diameter, Finished-Hole Tolerance, and Hole Position

A hole has more than one relevant dimension.

For a plated through-hole, these can include:

- drill-tool diameter;
- drilled-hole geometry before plating;
- copper deposited on the wall;
- final finished-hole diameter;
- positional location of the hole.

These dimensions are related but not identical.

### Mechanical drill size

For conventional rigid PCB fabrication:

- **0.20 mm (8 mil)** is a useful routine minimum mechanical-drill value;
- **0.20-0.30 mm (8-12 mil)** is a common practical range for ordinary through-via drills;
- **0.15 mm (6 mil)** is now supported by many modern fabricators, but it is more process-sensitive and may carry additional cost or restrictions.

This reconciles the Chapter 2 reference range of **0.20-0.30 mm** with current commercial capability tables that increasingly list 0.15 mm as an available minimum.

Smaller mechanical drills are more difficult because tool stiffness decreases, tool wear becomes more important, debris removal becomes more difficult, and a given board thickness produces a higher aspect ratio.

### Finished-hole size tolerance

A useful mainstream sense for ordinary finished holes is:

- plated through-hole finished-size tolerance: approximately **±0.08 to ±0.10 mm**;
- tighter plated or press-fit holes: approximately **±0.05 mm** when the process is specifically controlled;
- non-plated holes: commonly around **±0.05 to ±0.10 mm**, depending on size and routing/drilling method.

Some fabricators use asymmetric plated-hole tolerances rather than a simple ± value because plating tends to reduce the final opening. Therefore the drawing should specify the **finished-hole requirement** when fit is important.

### Hole positional tolerance

A hole can have the correct diameter but still be misplaced.

For mainstream rigid-board fabrication, hole-position capability is commonly on the order of **±0.05 to ±0.10 mm (about 2-4 mil)**. A useful teaching value for ordinary multilayer work is approximately **±0.075 mm (3 mil)**, while tighter processes may hold about ±0.05 mm.

Position error can come from:

- machine positioning;
- drill-tool behavior;
- material movement;
- panel registration;
- accumulated registration error from earlier layers.

For mounting holes, positional error can affect fit with hardware or enclosures. For plated holes and vias, it reduces the available annular-ring margin even when hole diameter is correct.

## 2.9.6 - Annular Ring Is a Tolerance-Margin Problem

Section 2.6 introduced the annular ring as the copper surrounding a plated hole.

For an ideal concentric circular pad and hole, the nominal radial annular ring is:

$$
a_\text{nominal} = \frac{D_p-D_h}{2}
$$

where:

- $D_p$ = pad or land diameter;
- $D_h$ = relevant hole diameter.

For ordinary rigid PCB design, a useful mainstream sense is:

- **0.15 mm (6 mil)**: common minimum annular-ring design value for plated holes;
- **0.20-0.25 mm (8-10 mil)**: preferred range when space allows and higher manufacturing margin is desired.

Some modern processes can accept smaller rings, but those tighter values should not be treated as a portable default across suppliers, layer counts, and copper weights.

Real fabrication adds positional variation.

A useful first-order screening model is to represent the combined relative pad-to-hole offset by $e$. In the worst direction,

$$
a_\text{min} \approx a_\text{nominal}-e
$$

This is a simplified geometry model, not an acceptance standard. Its value is that it makes the tolerance problem visible.

### Example

Suppose a design uses:

- pad diameter: $0.60\ \text{mm}$;
- hole diameter: $0.30\ \text{mm}$.

Then:

$$
a_\text{nominal}
=
\frac{0.60-0.30}{2}
=
0.15\ \text{mm}
$$

The nominal design therefore begins at a common mainstream minimum value.

If the combined relative pad-to-hole offset in the worst direction is $0.05\ \text{mm}$,

$$
a_\text{min}
\approx
0.15-0.05
=
0.10\ \text{mm}
$$

The narrow side has now fallen below the common 0.15 mm teaching minimum even though the **nominal** pad-and-hole geometry originally met it.

That is the important lesson:

> **Annular ring must include margin for drill position and layer registration, not only ideal concentric geometry.**

A design with 0.20-0.25 mm nominal ring can tolerate substantially more relative movement before approaching breakout.

## 2.9.7 - Via Aspect Ratio Couples Hole Size to Board Thickness

Section 2.6 introduced the through-hole aspect-ratio concept:

$$
\text{Aspect ratio} = \frac{T_b}{D_h}
$$

where $T_b$ is the relevant board thickness and $D_h$ is the drill/hole diameter used by the fabricator for the capability definition.

The important point is that hole size cannot be reviewed independently from board thickness.

For the same drilled diameter:

- a thicker board produces a higher aspect ratio;
- a higher aspect ratio makes desmear, metallization, and plating more difficult;
- achieving uniform copper through a deep narrow hole requires greater process capability.

For mainstream rigid-board work:

- **8:1** is a useful routine through-hole aspect-ratio limit;
- **10:1** is a common upper capability for many modern processes but should be treated as a tighter/conditional value;
- values substantially above 10:1 normally move into more specialized fabrication.

The Chapter 2 reference range of **8:1 to 10:1** is therefore appropriate.

For example,

$$
\frac{1.6\ \text{mm}}{0.20\ \text{mm}} = 8:1
$$

A 1.6 mm board with a 0.15 mm mechanical hole would be approximately:

$$
\frac{1.6}{0.15} \approx 10.7:1
$$

Even though a 0.15 mm drill may be available, that combination is no longer a routine 8:1 geometry. This is why a quoted minimum drill size cannot be used without checking board thickness.

> **Always review via diameter and board thickness together.**

## 2.9.8 - Solder-Mask Registration and Minimum Mask Web

Solder mask has its own manufacturing capability.

The mask image must align with pads, vias, test points, and other exposed conductive features. The fabricated mask can shift relative to copper because of imaging and registration variation.

If a mask opening is too small or too tightly aligned to a pad, normal variation can cause:

- solder mask on the pad;
- uneven pad exposure;
- unintended copper exposure nearby.

A **mask web** or **mask dam** is the narrow strip of solder mask intentionally left between adjacent openings.

For engineering-scale context:

- **0.05-0.10 mm per side** is a common conservative solder-mask opening expansion/registration allowance for ordinary NSMD pads;
- modern laser/direct-imaging processes can support smaller clearances, including approximately **0.03 mm** in some processes and even 1:1 pad/opening treatment under tightly controlled conditions;
- **0.10 mm (4 mil)** is a useful common minimum teaching value for a solder-mask web;
- current production examples span roughly **0.07 mm for LDI** to **0.13 mm for more conventional imaging**.

Therefore a 0.08 mm mask web is not automatically impossible, but it is not a robust portable default across fabricators. It may be acceptable with an LDI process and unacceptable or costly with another mask process.

If a designed mask web is narrower than the qualified process can reliably image, develop, and cure, the web may:

- disappear;
- break;
- merge into neighboring openings;
- vary significantly across production.

Detailed pad and mask DFM rules are developed in Chapter 3.

## 2.9.9 - Copper-to-Edge Clearance and Profiling Capability

Section 2.8 showed that routing, scoring, and other profiling operations create the final board or array geometry.

The board edge therefore has manufacturing tolerance.

Copper placed close to the routed edge can be affected by:

- route-position variation;
- tool diameter and compensation;
- local board movement;
- edge damage;
- dimensional movement accumulated from earlier processing.

For ordinary CNC-routed rigid boards, useful mainstream values are:

- **0.20-0.25 mm (8-10 mil)** minimum outer-layer copper-to-edge clearance;
- about **0.40 mm (16 mil)** for inner-layer copper/planes in more conservative standard processes;
- **±0.10 to ±0.20 mm** typical CNC-routed board-profile dimensional tolerance.

These are fabrication-scale values. They should not be confused with the much larger component-to-board-edge clearances used for assembly access and depanelization.

V-scored edges generally need more copper clearance because the score has a wider positional/depth envelope. A value around **0.45 mm** is a useful manufacturing-scale reference for copper-to-V-score clearance in standard processes.

The practical lesson is:

> **Copper-to-edge clearance must be matched to the profiling method, not treated as one universal number.**

Detailed component-to-edge DFM belongs in Chapter 3.

## 2.9.10 - Board Thickness and Thickness Tolerance

Overall PCB thickness is determined by the combined stack-up:

- core thicknesses;
- prepreg construction and cured thickness;
- copper layers;
- outer plating;
- solder mask and surface finish;
- lamination behavior.

Therefore nominal board thickness is not an exact physical constant.

For common rigid FR-4:

- **1.6 mm** is a very common nominal thickness;
- for finished boards **1.0 mm and thicker, ±10%** is a widely used standard thickness tolerance;
- for boards below about 1.0 mm, **±0.10 mm** is a common absolute tolerance used by mainstream fabricators.

For a 1.6 mm board, ±10% corresponds to approximately:

$$
1.44\ \text{mm} \le T \le 1.76\ \text{mm}
$$

This tolerance matters when the PCB must fit:

- card guides;
- edge connectors;
- slots;
- enclosure features;
- press-fit or mechanical interfaces;
- controlled stack or spacing geometry.

The next section develops why thickness strongly affects flexural stiffness. Here, the manufacturing point is that **finished thickness has measurable process variation and must be specified appropriately when the mechanical interface depends on it**.

## 2.9.11 - Copper Thickness Has Nominal Values and Process Variation

Copper thickness is commonly described using copper weight.

Representative nominal foil relationships are approximately:

- $1/2$ oz/ft²: **17-18 μm**;
- 1 oz/ft²: **34-35 μm**;
- 2 oz/ft²: **68-70 μm**.

The Chapter 2 values of 18, 35, and 70 μm are therefore correct engineering approximations.

They should not be interpreted as a promise that every finished copper feature has exactly that thickness.

In particular:

- inner-layer copper is primarily based on the starting foil followed by patterning/etching;
- outer-layer copper can receive additional electroplated copper;
- plating thickness varies across a panel and among features;
- local copper density and current distribution influence deposition.

Finished track width itself may also vary by roughly ±20% under common acceptance practice.

### Through-hole wall copper

For ordinary plated through-holes:

- approximately **18-25 μm** is a useful current mainstream range for standard hole-wall copper;
- **20 μm** is a common minimum fabrication/acceptance value;
- 25 μm or higher may be specified for tighter reliability or product requirements.

This reconciles the Chapter 2 teaching value of **20-25 μm** with current supplier capability tables that may list an average around 18 μm or a standard range beginning at 18 μm.

Therefore, **starting foil**, **finished surface copper**, and **minimum hole-wall copper** are different quantities.

## 2.9.12 - Bow, Twist, and Registration Are Also Capabilities

Not every fabrication capability is a small local feature dimension.

Some requirements apply to the whole board or panel.

### Bow and twist

A finished PCB may deviate from perfect flatness because of:

- material imbalance;
- copper imbalance;
- lamination stress;
- thermal history;
- thickness variation;
- asymmetric construction.

A widely used rigid-board acceptance benchmark is:

- **maximum bow/twist about 0.75% for boards intended for surface-mount assembly**;
- **about 1.5% for boards without surface-mount components**, unless a tighter product requirement is specified.

This is more useful than treating 0.75-1.0% as a universal range because assembly type matters. High-density assembly may require substantially tighter flatness.

Section 2.10 develops the physical mechanics of stiffness and warpage. Here, bow/twist is treated only as a manufacturing capability and acceptance variable.

### Layer-to-layer registration

Inner copper layers, drilled holes, and outer features must remain sufficiently aligned.

For ordinary multilayer rigid-board fabrication, a useful mainstream sense is:

- approximately **±0.075 to ±0.10 mm (3-4 mil)** layer-to-layer registration;
- approximately **±0.05 mm (2 mil)** for tighter advanced processes.

Registration error can affect:

- annular ring;
- plane clearances;
- pad-to-hole relationships;
- internal copper-to-hole spacing;
- conductor location relative to the final profile.

### Hole-to-hole and feature-to-feature position

Mainstream hole-position tolerance is commonly about **±0.05 to ±0.10 mm**, overlapping the same general scale as registration error.

A board can therefore meet each hole-diameter tolerance while still fail a positional relationship required for:

- mounting-hole patterns;
- connector alignment;
- tooling features;
- repeated hole fields.

## 2.9.14 - How to Read the Typical-Value Summary

The summary table is most useful when the reader distinguishes **routine**, **minimum**, and **preferred** values.

### Example A - Trace width at a mainstream minimum

Suppose a PCB uses a 0.10 mm trace.

The table identifies 0.10 mm as a common mainstream minimum for approximately 1 oz copper.

The correct conclusion is not:

> "0.10 mm is a good default trace width."

The better conclusion is:

> "0.10 mm is routinely manufacturable by many standard processes, but it leaves less process margin than 0.15 mm or larger."

If electrical and routing constraints permit, the wider trace is usually more producible.

### Example B - 1.6 mm board with a 0.20 mm drill

The aspect ratio is:

$$
\text{Aspect ratio}
=
\frac{1.60}{0.20}
=
8:1
$$

An 8:1 through-hole aspect ratio is a useful routine upper design point for mainstream processing.

If the same board used a 0.15 mm mechanical drill,

$$
\frac{1.60}{0.15}
\approx
10.7:1
$$

The smaller drill may still exist in the fabricator's drill table, but the combined board-thickness/drill geometry is no longer a routine 8:1 design.

### Example C - A 0.08 mm solder-mask web

A 0.08 mm web sits:

- below the common 0.10 mm teaching/default value;
- above the approximately 0.07 mm capability of some modern LDI processes;
- below the approximately 0.13 mm requirement of some conventional processes.

Therefore 0.08 mm is a good example of a feature that may be **manufacturable but not portable across suppliers/processes**.

The correct response is to identify the intended mask process or supplier before treating the geometry as production-ready.

### Example D - Copper 0.20 mm from a routed edge

A 0.20 mm outer-layer copper-to-edge clearance is near the aggressive lower end of mainstream routed-board capability.

A value closer to 0.25 mm or greater gives more margin. An inner plane may require roughly 0.40 mm in a conservative standard process.

Again, the lesson is not to memorize one number. It is to recognize where a design sits relative to the normal manufacturing scale.

## 2.9.15 - Producibility Is More Than Passing Individual Minimum Rules

**Producibility** describes how suitable a design is for repeatable manufacture using the intended production process.

A PCB can pass every individual minimum rule and still have poor producibility.

Examples include a board that simultaneously uses:

- minimum trace width over large areas;
- minimum trace spacing;
- minimum mechanical drill size;
- high via aspect ratio;
- small annular rings;
- narrow mask webs;
- copper close to the routed edge;
- tight layer registration;
- tight board-thickness and flatness requirements.

Each feature may be nominally manufacturable by itself. Their combination can reduce process margin and make the overall product more sensitive to normal variation.

This affects **yield**.

Yield is the fraction of manufactured product that meets requirements without being rejected or requiring corrective action.

Lower process margin can increase:

- inspection burden;
- special process controls;
- engineering review;
- scrap risk;
- rework or remake risk;
- production cycle time;
- unit cost.

The relationship is not perfectly linear, and every fabricator's cost model is different. The general engineering trend is still important:

| Design relationship to process capability | Typical manufacturing implication |
|---|---|
| Comfortably inside preferred capability | Wider process margin, simpler production control, generally better yield potential |
| Near minimum capability in a few locations | Greater sensitivity to variation; may require focused review or control |
| Many interacting features near minimum capability | Reduced producibility margin; greater yield and cost risk |
| Outside qualified capability | Redesign, alternate process, alternate fabricator, or explicit special-process development may be required |

This is why producibility should be reviewed at the **board level**, not only one dimension at a time.

## 2.9.16 - EDA Design Rules Must Reflect the Intended Manufacturing Process

PCB CAD tools can enforce:

- minimum trace width;
- minimum spacing;
- hole size;
- annular-ring geometry;
- copper-to-edge clearance;
- mask clearances;
- other dimensional rules.

These checks are valuable, but the software does not automatically know the capability of the intended manufacturing site unless those rules were configured correctly.

A design can therefore:

- pass DRC and still violate the chosen fabricator's capability;
- fail a conservative DRC rule even though the fabricator could manufacture the feature;
- satisfy one supplier's rules but not another supplier's rules.

This creates an important workflow:

> **Fabricator capability -> approved design rules -> layout -> DRC -> manufacturing review**

Design-rule checking is therefore part of manufacturability control, but it is not a substitute for supplier capability review.

Detailed DFM methods, layout rules, and assembly-related constraints are developed in Chapter 3.

## 2.9.17 - Engineering Use of Capability Data

Before a PCB design is released, capability data should answer questions such as:

- What is the intended fabricator's minimum and preferred trace/space capability?
- What mechanical-drill diameters are supported?
- What aspect ratio is allowed for the planned board thickness?
- What drill and layer-registration tolerances apply?
- What annular-ring requirement must remain after fabrication?
- What solder-mask registration and minimum web can be held?
- What copper-to-edge clearance is required for the selected profiling route?
- What finished board-thickness tolerance applies?
- What copper/plating requirements apply?
- What bow/twist requirement applies to the product?
- Which features are already close to the supplier's minimum capability?

A good engineering review does not ask only:

> "Can the PCB be fabricated?"

It also asks:

> **"Where is the design close to the edge of capability, and is that margin appropriate for the expected volume, cost, and reliability?"**

Section 2.13 later develops the manufacturing-data package, quality records, and engineering-supplier communication in more detail.

---

## Key Terms Introduced in This Section

**Capability**  
The range of feature sizes, tolerances, materials, or process outcomes that a manufacturing process or fabricator can achieve under defined conditions.

**Physical process limit**  
A boundary imposed by the underlying process physics, materials, or equipment.

**Fabricator minimum capability**  
The tightest value a specific fabricator states it can manufacture under stated process conditions.

**Preferred capability**  
A less aggressive feature or tolerance that provides more process margin than the stated minimum.

**Tolerance**  
The permitted variation from a nominal dimension or specified value.

**Registration**  
The positional alignment among features created in different manufacturing operations, such as inner copper layers, drilling, outer imaging, and solder mask.

**Manufacturing margin**  
The separation between a design requirement and the edge of qualified manufacturing capability.

**Producibility**  
The degree to which a design can be manufactured repeatedly with acceptable yield, quality, cost, and process control.

**Yield**  
The fraction of produced items that meet requirements rather than being rejected or requiring corrective action.

**Capability matrix**  
A structured set of manufacturing capabilities and constraints used to compare design geometry with the intended fabrication process.

---

## Section Takeaway

PCB fabrication capability is not one universal set of minimum numbers.

The most important distinctions are:

- **physical process limit** is not the same as **fabricator capability**;
- **fabricator minimum capability** is not the same as **preferred capability**;
- **EDA design rules** must be configured from the intended manufacturing requirements;
- **industry standards** and **customer requirements** are not interchangeable with supplier capability;
- trace width, spacing, drilling, annular ring, solder mask, profiling, thickness, plating, flatness, and registration all contain manufacturing variation;
- via diameter must be considered together with board thickness through aspect ratio;
- nominal annular ring must include margin for relative hole and layer-position variation;
- a design can pass individual minimum rules and still have poor overall producibility;
- using more manufacturing margin generally improves yield robustness and reduces cost risk.

Section 2.10 next examines one of the most important global board dimensions in more detail: **PCB thickness**. The focus shifts from thickness as a manufacturing tolerance to thickness as a strong driver of flexural stiffness, deformation, and warpage.

### Current Cross-Fabricator Numerical Verification

A second numerical QA pass was completed against current published fabrication capability/tolerance tables.

Representative verification sources included:

- JLCPCB rigid-PCB capability tables;
- PCBWay standard and advanced capability/tolerance tables;
- Eurocircuits manufacturing-tolerance and classification guidance;
- an additional multilayer capability table used only to cross-check layer-registration scale.

Key reconciliations:

- **Trace/space:** 0.10/0.10 mm remains a mainstream standard minimum; 0.15 mm is a sensible preferred teaching value.
- **Mechanical drill:** the curriculum's 0.20-0.30 mm range remains a good routine/preferred range, while current mainstream capability increasingly reaches 0.15 mm.
- **Aspect ratio:** 8:1 is routine; about 10:1 is a common tighter upper mainstream capability.
- **PTH finished-hole tolerance:** about ±0.08 to ±0.10 mm is a useful mainstream range; ±0.05 mm is tighter.
- **Hole position:** published mainstream values cluster around ±0.05 to ±0.10 mm.
- **Annular ring:** 0.15 mm is a common minimum design/capability value; 0.20-0.25 mm is a strong preferred range.
- **Solder-mask web:** current published processes span approximately 0.07 mm LDI to 0.13 mm conventional, with 0.10 mm a useful common teaching value.
- **Copper to routed edge:** current fabrication minimums are typically around 0.20-0.25 mm outer-layer, with more conservative inner-plane clearance around 0.40 mm.
- **CNC profile tolerance:** approximately ±0.10 to ±0.20 mm is mainstream.
- **Board thickness:** ±10% for boards ≥1 mm is strongly cross-verified.
- **Hole-wall copper:** current mainstream published values span roughly 18-25 μm, with 20 μm a common minimum reference.
- **Bow/twist:** the more useful general distinction is ≤0.75% for boards intended for SMD assembly and about ≤1.5% for boards without SMDs, unless a tighter product requirement applies.
- **Layer registration:** ordinary multilayer capability is commonly on the order of ±0.075 to ±0.10 mm; ~±0.05 mm is a tighter/advanced level.

Important reconciliation with Chapter 3:

The Chapter 3 lecture value of **1.0-1.5 mm copper-to-edge clearance** is retained as a deliberately conservative DFM/layout guideline, not a fabrication minimum. Section 2.9 now uses the substantially smaller current **fabrication capability** scale (roughly 0.20-0.25 mm outer-layer for routed edges) so the two concepts are not conflated.
