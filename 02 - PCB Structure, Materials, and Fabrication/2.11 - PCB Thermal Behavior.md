# 2.11 - PCB Thermal Behavior

Section 2.10 showed that PCB thickness, stack-up construction, and copper distribution affect mechanical behavior. The same physical construction also controls how heat moves through the board.

A PCB is therefore not only an electrical interconnect and mechanical structure. It is also part of the product's **thermal path**.

At the board level, the most important thermal questions are:

- Where is heat generated?
- In which direction can heat spread easily?
- Where does the stack-up create a thermal bottleneck?
- Can copper planes or thermal vias create a more useful path?

This section develops those questions at an introductory PCB level. Detailed system-level thermal design, heat sinks, convection, radiation, thermal-resistance networks, and cooling-system selection are developed in Chapter 6.

---

## 2.11.1 - Copper and PCB Laminates Conduct Heat Very Differently

Section 2.4 introduced thermal conductivity, $k$, as a material property describing how readily heat conducts through a material.

Representative values for first-order comparison are:

| Material | Representative thermal conductivity |
|---|---:|
| Copper | $\approx 390\ \mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)}$ |
| Conventional FR-4-type laminate | $\approx 0.30\ \mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)}$ |
| Typical prepreg region | $\approx 0.20$–$0.30\ \mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)}$ |

Using the representative values above,

$$
\frac{k_{\mathrm{Cu}}}{k_{\mathrm{FR4}}}
\approx
\frac{390}{0.30}
\approx
1300
$$

so, as an order-of-magnitude comparison, copper's thermal conductivity is roughly **three orders of magnitude higher** than that of conventional FR-4-type laminate.

The exact values depend on the actual laminate construction, reinforcement, resin content, fillers, temperature, and test direction. The comparison is still useful because it explains an important PCB design principle:

> **Useful PCB heat paths are usually created by copper, while dielectric layers often form the thermal bottlenecks between those paths.**

---

## 2.11.2 - Heat Moves in Two Important Directions

Heat can move through a PCB in two broad directions.

### In-plane heat flow

**In-plane heat flow** occurs along the X-Y plane of the board.

It is strongly influenced by:

- copper traces;
- copper pours;
- power and ground planes;
- large thermal lands.

Continuous copper can spread heat away from a local hot region over a much larger board area.

### Through-thickness heat flow

**Through-thickness heat flow** occurs in the Z direction.

This path often crosses:

- solder;
- copper pads;
- prepreg;
- core laminate;
- internal copper planes;
- plated via barrels;
- solder mask or interface materials where applicable.

Because FR-4 and prepreg conduct heat poorly compared with copper, dielectric portions of the Z-direction path can create high thermal resistance.

## 2.11.3 - Copper Planes Spread Heat Laterally

A local electronic component may generate heat over only a small footprint.

If that heat remains concentrated near the component, the local board temperature can rise sharply.

A copper plane or large copper pour can:

- collect heat from the component region;
- spread that heat laterally;
- increase the effective board area participating in heat transfer;
- reduce the severity of a local hot spot.

The copper plane does not make the FR-4 itself more conductive. Instead, it provides a low-resistance path that allows heat to reach a larger area.

This is why a continuous plane can be thermally more useful than isolated copper that does not connect the hot region to a meaningful heat-rejection path.

Layer count alone is not the controlling variable. A multilayer board with poorly connected copper may spread heat less effectively than a simpler board with a well-designed thermal path.

---

## 2.11.4 - Through-Thickness Dielectric Layers Can Be Thermal Bottlenecks

For one-dimensional conduction through a simple uniform layer,

$$
Q = \frac{kA\Delta T}{L}
$$

or, in thermal-resistance form,

$$
R_{\mathrm{cond}}=\frac{L}{kA}
$$

where:

- $Q$ = heat-transfer rate, in watts (W);
- $k$ = thermal conductivity, in $\mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)}$;
- $A$ = cross-sectional area normal to heat flow, in $\mathrm{m^2}$;
- $L$ = conduction-path length, in meters (m);
- $\Delta T$ = temperature difference, in kelvins (K) or degrees Celsius for a temperature difference;
- $R_{\mathrm{cond}}$ = conduction thermal resistance, in $\mathrm{K/W}$.

This relationship gives three direct design lessons:

1. higher $k$ lowers thermal resistance;
2. larger area $A$ lowers thermal resistance;
3. longer path length $L$ raises thermal resistance.

For a PCB dielectric, the low value of $k$ can dominate the Z-direction path even when the dielectric layer is thin.

The board-level thermal design question is therefore not simply:

> "How much copper is on the board?"

A better question is:

> **Does the copper create a continuous useful path around or through the thermal bottleneck?**

---

## 2.11.5 - Worked Example: Heat Conduction Through a Dielectric Region

Consider a simplified dielectric layer between two copper regions.

Assume:

- dielectric thickness: $L=0.20\ \mathrm{mm}=2.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m}$;
- effective heat-flow area: $A=10\ \mathrm{mm}\times10\ \mathrm{mm}=1.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2}$;
- dielectric thermal conductivity: $k=0.30\ \mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)}$.

The first-order conduction thermal resistance is

$$
R_{\mathrm{cond}}
=
\frac{L}{kA}
=
\frac{2.0\times10^{-4}}
{0.30(1.0\times10^{-4})}
\approx
6.7\ \mathrm{K/W}
$$

If $2\ \mathrm{W}$ must pass through this idealized region,

$$
\Delta T
=
Q R_{\mathrm{cond}}
\approx
(2)(6.67)
\approx
13.3^\circ\mathrm{C}
$$

This does **not** predict the complete temperature rise of a real PCB or component. Real boards have three-dimensional heat spreading, multiple parallel paths, copper planes, vias, packages, solder joints, convection, and radiation.

The example is useful because it shows why even a thin dielectric region can become an important thermal bottleneck when heat must cross it over a limited area.

---

## 2.11.6 - Thermal Vias Create Vertical Copper Paths

A plated via contains a thin copper barrel extending through the PCB thickness.

When vias are placed to support heat transfer, they can create vertical conduction paths that partially bypass the low-conductivity dielectric.

A **thermal-via field** can provide many vertical copper conduction paths in parallel.

The thermal benefit depends on factors such as:

- number of vias;
- via diameter;
- copper plating thickness;
- board thickness;
- connection to useful copper on both sides or on internal planes;
- spacing and distribution of the vias.

More vias do not automatically solve a thermal problem. The vias must connect the hot region to a useful heat-spreading or heat-rejection region.

Dense via fields also interact with:

- fabrication capability;
- copper plating;
- mechanical stress;
- electrical design;
- solder behavior where vias are near or inside pads.

Those tradeoffs are treated in the relevant fabrication, DFM, assembly, and later thermal-design sections.

## 2.11.7 - A Complete Heat Path Matters More Than One Thermal Feature

A single thermal feature is useful only when it connects into a complete path.

Examples:

- a large copper pour is less useful if it is isolated from the hot component;
- thermal vias are less useful if they terminate in small isolated copper regions;
- an internal plane spreads heat well only if heat can reach it;
- a bottom-side thermal pad helps only if heat can continue from that pad into air, a chassis, a heat sink, or another useful sink.

This leads to a practical board-level rule:

> **Collect heat -> conduct it through bottlenecks -> spread it over a larger area -> provide a path to the next thermal destination.**

## 2.11.8 - Thermal Gradients Also Affect Mechanical Behavior

Thermal behavior and mechanical behavior are coupled.

A board does not need to be at one uniform temperature.

Local hot spots and temperature gradients can produce:

- local thermal expansion;
- differential strain between materials and regions;
- changing residual-stress distributions;
- temporary or persistent changes in curvature;
- cyclic loading of vias, solder joints, and interfaces.

Section 2.10 introduced this coupling from the warpage side.

The thermal perspective is:

> **A better-spread temperature field can reduce severe local thermal gradients, while a concentrated hot spot can increase both thermal and thermomechanical loading.**

Detailed thermomechanical stress analysis belongs in Chapter 7, and detailed reliability analysis belongs in Chapter 8.

## 2.11.9 - Practical PCB-Level Thermal Design Levers

At the PCB level, useful thermal-design actions can include:

1. **Use copper close to heat sources.** Provide thermal lands, pours, or planes that can collect heat.
2. **Provide lateral spreading area.** Continuous copper is generally more useful for spreading than narrow isolated traces.
3. **Use thermal vias where vertical conduction is needed.** Connect surface heat-collection regions to internal or opposite-side copper.
4. **Reduce unnecessary dielectric bottlenecks.** Stack-up geometry affects the distance and area over which heat must cross low-conductivity material.
5. **Connect to the next thermal destination.** A bottom-side copper region may connect to airflow, a chassis, a heat spreader, or a heat sink.
6. **Avoid assuming that more layers automatically mean better cooling.** Thermal performance depends on copper continuity, location, connection, and the entire heat path.
7. **Coordinate thermal decisions with electrical and manufacturing requirements.** Copper planes, vias, pad geometry, and stack-up changes affect more than thermal performance.

Thermal design is therefore another example of why PCB decisions cannot be made one discipline at a time.

---

## Key Terms Introduced in This Section

**In-plane heat flow**  
Heat conduction mainly along the X-Y plane of the PCB.

**Through-thickness heat flow**  
Heat conduction through the PCB thickness in the Z direction.

**Heat spreading**  
Redistribution of heat from a concentrated region into a larger area.

**Thermal bottleneck**  
A region of comparatively high thermal resistance that limits heat flow through the overall path.

**Thermal via**  
A plated via intentionally used as part of a vertical heat-conduction path.

**Thermal plane**  
A continuous copper plane used partly or primarily to spread heat over a larger PCB area.

**Conduction thermal resistance**  
A first-order measure of resistance to heat conduction through a layer, represented for a simple uniform one-dimensional path by $R_{\mathrm{cond}}=L/(kA)$.

---

## Section Takeaway

PCB thermal behavior depends strongly on both **material properties** and **how conductive paths are arranged**.

The most important ideas are:

- copper conducts heat far more effectively than conventional PCB dielectric materials;
- copper planes and pours are especially effective for lateral heat spreading;
- through-thickness heat flow is often limited by low-conductivity dielectric layers;
- thermal vias provide vertical copper paths that can reduce the Z-direction bottleneck;
- a thermal feature is most useful when it forms part of a complete path from the heat source to a larger spreading or heat-rejection region;
- increasing layer count alone does not guarantee good thermal performance;
- thermal gradients can also contribute to thermomechanical stress and warpage.

This section has treated the PCB as one part of a larger heat path. Chapter 6 develops the complete thermal-design problem, including heat sources, conduction, convection, radiation, thermal-resistance networks, heat sinks, airflow, and verification.

Section 2.12 next returns to the fabricated PCB itself and examines **common PCB-level fabrication and material failures**.
