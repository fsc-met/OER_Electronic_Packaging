# 2.8 - How a Rigid Multilayer PCB Is Fabricated

Section 2.7 examined the finishes that protect intentionally exposed copper and prepare those surfaces for later soldering, contact, or another use. Those finishes make more sense when they are placed inside the complete manufacturing sequence.

A rigid multilayer PCB does not begin as a finished green board with holes and pads. It is built through a sequence of **material preparation, imaging, material removal, lamination, drilling, metallization, plating, coating, profiling, testing, and inspection** operations.

The exact routing varies among fabricators and board technologies. A board using sequential lamination, microvias, special surface finishes, embedded features, or unusual materials may require additional or reordered operations. The sequence developed here represents a common rigid multilayer manufacturing route and emphasizes the **function of each operation**, not one universal factory recipe.

The most useful way to understand PCB fabrication is to ask the same questions at every stage:

> **What enters the operation? What does manufacturing do to it? What physically changes? What can go wrong? What is checked before the board proceeds?**

At the highest level, the fabrication chain can be grouped into five physical transformations:

1. **Create the inner copper layers.**
2. **Build and laminate the multilayer structure.**
3. **Create and metallize the holes.**
4. **Create and protect the outer circuitry.**
5. **Profile and verify the finished bare board.**

## 2.8.1 - Starting Materials and Panel Preparation

Fabrication begins with manufactured PCB laminate materials rather than raw fiberglass and resin.

For a conventional rigid multilayer FR-4 board, important starting materials include:

- **copper-clad cores** that will become patterned inner layers;
- **prepreg** that will bond layers during lamination;
- **copper foil** that will become the outer conductive layers;
- process materials such as photoresist, temporary plating/etch resists, solder mask, and the selected surface-finish materials.

The copper-clad core already consists of cured dielectric laminate with copper bonded to one or both surfaces. The PCB fabricator converts that laminate into circuit layers; the fabricator is not normally manufacturing the FR-4 laminate itself from raw glass cloth as part of this board-building sequence.

Production is commonly performed in **manufacturing-panel format**. One panel may contain one board image, several repeated board images, process coupons, tooling features, and unused border areas required for handling and registration. Working at panel scale allows imaging, plating, drilling, and other equipment to process multiple future boards together.

Before imaging, copper surfaces must be sufficiently clean and prepared for the next coating operation. Oils, oxidation, particles, or residues can interfere with photoresist adhesion and cause defects later in the process. Cleaning may therefore include controlled mechanical and/or chemical surface preparation appropriate to the fabricator's process.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** copper-clad core material, prepreg, outer copper foil, and manufacturing data.
- **Operation:** material verification, panel preparation, cleaning, and process setup.
- **Physical change:** the copper surface is conditioned and the working panel is prepared for imaging.
- **Immediate risk:** wrong material, wrong copper construction, contamination, handling damage, or poor resist adhesion.
- **Verification:** material identification, panel condition, thickness/construction records, and surface cleanliness are checked before imaging.

This first operation appears simple, but an error here can propagate through nearly every later step. Using the wrong laminate, wrong copper foil, or wrong panel orientation cannot be corrected by more accurate drilling or plating later.

---

## 2.8.2 - Inner-Layer Imaging

The copper on an inner-layer core must be selectively protected so that the desired circuit pattern can later remain while unwanted copper is removed.

A light-sensitive **photoresist** is applied to the cleaned copper surface. The circuit image is then transferred to the resist using phototools or direct-imaging equipment. Modern fabrication may use laser direct imaging, while other routes use optical exposure through artwork. The underlying function is the same: convert digital circuit information into a patterned resist on the physical copper surface.

After exposure, the resist is **developed**. Depending on the resist system, selected regions remain on the copper while other regions are removed. For the common subtractive inner-layer process described here, the remaining resist protects the copper that is intended to become traces, planes, and other conductive features.

Registration is critical. The imaged pattern must be positioned correctly relative to tooling targets and to the other layers that will later be stacked into the multilayer board. A copper pattern can be perfectly etched yet still be unusable if it is shifted relative to the future drill pattern or adjacent layers.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** cleaned copper-clad inner-layer core and circuit image data.
- **Operation:** apply photoresist, expose the intended circuit image, and develop the resist.
- **Physical change:** an invisible digital pattern becomes a physical resist pattern on copper.
- **Immediate risk:** resist voids, contamination, poor exposure, incorrect polarity/data, dimensional scaling error, or layer misregistration.
- **Verification:** resist coverage, image registration, pattern completeness, and process-control targets are checked before etching.

The important idea is that imaging does **not** yet create the copper circuit. It creates the temporary protective pattern that tells the etching operation which copper should remain.

---

## 2.8.3 - Inner-Layer Etching and Resist Stripping

After imaging and development, the panel contains two kinds of copper area:

- copper protected by resist;
- copper exposed to the etching chemistry.

During **etching**, the exposed copper is chemically removed. The resist-covered copper remains and becomes the conductive pattern of the inner layer.

After the unwanted copper has been removed, the remaining photoresist is stripped away. The result is no longer a uniformly copper-clad core. It is a dielectric core carrying patterned copper traces, planes, pads, and registration features on the intended surfaces.

Etching is not perfectly one-dimensional. Chemical attack can also act laterally at conductor edges. Process time, chemistry condition, copper thickness, spray behavior, and resist quality therefore influence the final conductor geometry. Excessive material removal can narrow features or create opens; insufficient etching can leave unwanted copper and create shorts or reduced clearances.

This is one reason design dimensions and manufacturing dimensions cannot be treated as ideal mathematical geometry.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** developed inner-layer resist pattern over copper-clad core.
- **Operation:** etch exposed copper, then strip the remaining photoresist.
- **Physical change:** a continuous copper sheet becomes isolated inner-layer conductors and planes.
- **Immediate risk:** overetch, underetch, residual copper, conductor narrowing, edge irregularity, resist failure, or unintended open/short conditions.
- **Verification:** conductor geometry and pattern completeness are inspected before the layer is accepted for lamination.

The sequence can be summarized as:

> **photoresist pattern -> remove unwanted copper -> strip resist -> patterned inner copper layer**

## 2.8.4 - Inner-Layer Inspection

Once the inner copper layer has been patterned, its circuit geometry should be checked **before it is buried inside the multilayer stack**.

This inspection point is strategically important. After lamination, the inner-layer copper is no longer directly accessible for ordinary visual inspection or rework. Detecting a major inner-layer defect before lamination prevents the fabricator from spending additional time and material building a multilayer panel around a defective layer.

Automated optical inspection (**AOI**) is commonly used to compare the manufactured copper pattern against design intent or a derived reference. Depending on the process, inspection can identify conditions such as:

- missing conductor segments;
- unwanted copper bridges;
- excessive conductor narrowing;
- local nicks or protrusions;
- registration errors;
- incomplete clearances;
- other pattern deviations.

Inspection does not mean that every possible future reliability issue can be predicted from appearance alone. It is a process gate intended to remove clearly nonconforming inner layers before they become inaccessible.

After inspection, inner-layer copper surfaces may receive a controlled surface treatment that improves bonding to the resin system during lamination. Traditional oxide treatments and alternative oxide/adhesion-promotion processes are examples. The exact chemistry is fabricator-specific and is not the focus here.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** etched and stripped inner-layer cores.
- **Operation:** optical/process inspection and, where applicable, surface preparation for lamination.
- **Physical change:** the circuit pattern may not change geometrically, but the layer is qualified and its copper surface may be conditioned for bonding.
- **Immediate risk:** undetected opens/shorts, dimensional errors, contamination, or inadequate bonding surface condition.
- **Verification:** inspection results and layer registration are accepted before the layer enters layup.

The manufacturing logic is straightforward:

> **Inspect what will soon become hidden.**

---

## 2.8.5 - Multilayer Layup

A multilayer PCB is created by arranging patterned inner-layer cores, prepreg sheets, and outer copper foils in the required order.

This arrangement is called the **layup**.

The stack must match the approved layer construction. A simple conceptual sequence might contain:

> outer copper foil -> prepreg -> patterned core -> prepreg -> patterned core -> prepreg -> outer copper foil

Actual constructions vary widely in layer count, core thickness, prepreg style, copper weight, dielectric spacing, and special material requirements.

During layup, the important manufacturing tasks are **identity, order, orientation, cleanliness, and registration**.

Each patterned core must be placed in the correct layer position and orientation. Registration features are used so that inner copper patterns align with one another and with the future drill pattern. Particles, resin contamination, folds, damaged prepreg, or wrong material placement can create local thickness or bonding problems.

The prepreg is not simply a passive spacer. It contains reinforcement impregnated with partially cured resin. During the next operation, that resin must flow and cure sufficiently to bond the stack into one rigid structure.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** accepted patterned inner-layer cores, prepreg sheets, and outer copper foils.
- **Operation:** stack the materials in the specified sequence and register the layers.
- **Physical change:** separate sheet materials become one organized but not-yet-bonded multilayer stack.
- **Immediate risk:** wrong layer order, flipped layer, misregistration, trapped contamination, damaged prepreg, or incorrect material construction.
- **Verification:** stack order, material identity, orientation, tooling/registration, and traveler documentation are checked before pressing.

Layup itself does not permanently bond the layers. It establishes the geometry that lamination must preserve.

---

## 2.8.6 - Lamination: Heat, Pressure, Resin Flow, and Cure

The prepared multilayer stack is placed into a lamination process where **heat and pressure** convert the loose layup into a rigid bonded panel.

As temperature rises, resin in the prepreg softens and flows. That flow allows resin to fill spaces around etched copper features and between adjacent layers. Continued heating promotes resin cure and cross-linking while pressure maintains intimate contact and controls the stack geometry.

The physical transformation is substantial:

> **separate cores + prepreg + copper foil -> bonded multilayer composite panel**

Several interacting mechanisms matter:

- **resin flow** fills relief around patterned copper;
- **pressure** maintains contact and helps control thickness;
- **cure** converts the prepreg resin into a more fully cross-linked solid;
- **cooling** brings the laminated panel back toward handling temperature.

Lamination can also generate residual stress. Sources include thermal-expansion mismatch during heating/cooling, resin cure shrinkage, and nonuniform stiffness from copper distribution. The board can contain residual stress even when it appears flat immediately after lamination. Detailed warpage behavior is developed later in Section 2.10.

Representative lamination-related problems include poor bonding, voiding, resin starvation or excess resin movement, thickness variation, layer shift, and residual-stress buildup. The goal here is not to define acceptance limits, but to recognize the cause-and-effect chain.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** registered multilayer layup.
- **Operation:** controlled heat, pressure, cure, and cooling.
- **Physical change:** prepreg resin flows and cures, bonding separate layers into a rigid multilayer panel.
- **Immediate risk:** voids, poor bonding, excessive layer movement, incorrect final thickness, resin-distribution problems, or residual stress.
- **Verification:** panel thickness, registration indicators/coupons, bonding condition, and process records are checked before drilling.

## 2.8.7 - Drilling

After lamination, the panel has the internal layer structure but does not yet have the drilled through-holes required for many vias, plated component holes, tooling features, and mechanical features.

Mechanical CNC drilling is widely used for conventional through-holes. Laser drilling is important for much smaller features such as many microvias, but advanced HDI process routing is outside the scope of this representative conventional multilayer sequence.

A critical idea from Section 2.6 should be preserved:

> **Drilling creates hole geometry. It does not create electrical connectivity.**

The drill must pass through a composite of copper and glass-reinforced dielectric. Tool diameter, tool condition, spindle behavior, feed conditions, panel stack setup, entry/backup materials, and accumulated heat all affect the hole that is produced.

Drilling can create or reveal several manufacturing problems:

- positional error relative to pads or inner-layer lands;
- diameter variation;
- burrs;
- rough hole walls;
- tool wear or breakage;
- local damage;
- **resin smear** on the hole wall caused by frictional heating and mechanical action.

Resin smear is especially important because it can cover the exposed edge of an inner-layer copper connection and interfere with later metallization.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** laminated multilayer panel and drill data.
- **Operation:** mechanically or, where appropriate, laser-create specified holes.
- **Physical change:** cylindrical or shaped openings are created through selected portions of the multilayer structure.
- **Immediate risk:** drill wander, wrong diameter, smear, burrs, roughness, heat damage, or reduced annular-ring geometry due to misregistration.
- **Verification:** hole diameter/position, tool monitoring, registration targets, and representative hole-wall condition are checked before chemical hole preparation.

The next operation exists because a drilled hole is not yet a clean, conductive plated interconnection.

---

## 2.8.8 - Hole Preparation and Desmear

The freshly drilled hole wall contains exposed dielectric, glass reinforcement, and selected inner-layer copper edges. It can also contain resin smear, debris, and mechanically damaged material.

Before copper can form a reliable conductive barrel, the hole wall must be prepared.

**Desmear** removes resin smear and exposes clean inner-layer copper surfaces where the future plated barrel must make electrical contact. Depending on the material and process, hole preparation may also modify the resin/glass surface so the subsequent metallization adheres reliably.

The exact chemistry is fabricator-specific. Plasma processes and chemical oxidizing systems are examples, but this section focuses on the manufacturing function rather than the reaction chemistry.

The physical goal is:

> **drilled hole with contaminated/smeared wall -> clean, conditioned hole wall with inner-layer copper exposed for connection**

Too little cleaning can leave insulating residue between the future barrel and the inner-layer copper. Excessive treatment can damage dielectric or change local geometry. Uniform treatment through deep holes can also become more difficult as aspect ratio increases.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** mechanically/laser drilled multilayer panel.
- **Operation:** clean, desmear, and condition the hole walls.
- **Physical change:** drilling residues and smeared resin are removed; the dielectric wall and inner-layer copper interfaces are prepared for metallization.
- **Immediate risk:** incomplete smear removal, contamination, over-etching/over-conditioning, glass-fiber damage, or nonuniform treatment through the hole depth.
- **Verification:** representative hole-wall cleanliness, exposed inner-layer copper condition, and process controls are checked before initial copper deposition.

This operation is easy to overlook because it adds no visible outer-board feature. Its importance becomes clear when considering the interface that must later carry current through the plated hole.

---

## 2.8.9 - Initial Copper Deposition and Hole Metallization

After drilling and desmear, much of the hole wall is still **nonconductive dielectric**. Electrolytic copper plating cannot simply begin on an electrically insulating surface.

The hole wall must first be made sufficiently conductive.

A common route activates the prepared surface and deposits a thin, continuous layer of **electroless copper** over the hole wall and panel surface. Other direct-metallization processes can establish conductivity by different means. The exact chemistry may vary, but the functional objective is the same:

> **nonconductive prepared hole wall -> continuous conductive starting surface**

This initial copper layer is not necessarily the full final barrel thickness. It creates the conductive path required for subsequent electrolytic copper build-up.

Continuity matters. A skipped or poorly covered region can later become a plating void or a weak electrical interface. Adhesion also matters because the deposited copper must remain bonded to the conditioned dielectric and connect to the exposed inner-layer copper features.

The distinction between **initial metallization** and **later copper build-up** is important. Initial metallization makes electroplating possible; later pattern/plating operations build the required conductor and barrel copper.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** clean, desmeared drilled panel with exposed inner-layer copper at connection sites.
- **Operation:** activate and metallize the hole wall using electroless copper or another qualified direct-metallization route.
- **Physical change:** insulating hole walls become electrically conductive surfaces capable of accepting electrolytic plating.
- **Immediate risk:** plating skips, voids, contamination, poor adhesion, incomplete coverage, or weak connection to inner-layer copper.
- **Verification:** conductive continuity, coverage, representative hole-wall condition, and process controls are checked before outer-layer imaging/plating.

## 2.8.10 - Outer-Layer Imaging

At this stage, the panel has a laminated multilayer structure, drilled holes, and an initial conductive copper layer over the outer surfaces and hole walls.

The outer-layer circuit pattern must now be defined.

In a common **pattern-plating** route, a plating resist is applied and imaged so that the areas intended to receive additional copper remain exposed while other areas are covered by resist. This polarity is important: the resist is being used to control **where metal will be added**, not merely to protect copper during the first inner-layer etch.

That distinction helps explain why outer-layer fabrication is not simply a repeat of inner-layer fabrication.

Inner-layer patterning was essentially:

> **protect desired copper -> etch unwanted copper**

A representative outer pattern-plating route becomes:

> **define where copper should be built -> plate copper -> later remove unwanted base copper**

Outer-layer imaging must align the circuit pattern with drilled holes, pads, and the internal layer structure. Registration errors can reduce annular ring, shift pads relative to holes, or create insufficient spacing.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** metallized panel with continuous conductive copper on the outer surfaces and hole walls.
- **Operation:** apply, expose, and develop plating resist to define the outer circuit pattern.
- **Physical change:** a temporary resist image defines where later electroplated copper will be added.
- **Immediate risk:** resist defects, image misregistration, wrong image polarity/data, contamination, or blocked plating areas.
- **Verification:** resist quality, outer-layer registration, pad-to-hole alignment, and imaging targets are checked before electroplating.

The outer-layer image is therefore a **plating map** as well as part of the circuit-definition process.

---

## 2.8.11 - Pattern Copper Plating

With the plating resist developed, selected outer conductor regions and the plated-hole interiors are electrically exposed.

The panel is then electroplated so copper builds on:

- exposed outer-layer traces and pads;
- annular-ring regions;
- conductive hole walls.

This operation simultaneously increases outer-conductor copper thickness and builds the plated through-hole barrel.

That is a key manufacturing distinction:

> **Outer-layer copper thickness is influenced by electroplating, while many inner-layer conductors are primarily defined by etching the original copper foil.**

Because electroplating depends on electrical current distribution and solution transport, copper build-up is not automatically identical at every feature. Large copper areas, isolated features, dense hole regions, panel geometry, current density, agitation, and plating setup can influence local deposition.

In many pattern-plating processes, a temporary metal such as tin is also deposited over the intended circuitry to act as an **etch resist** during the later outer-layer etching operation. This is one common route, not a universal requirement for every fabricator or finish system.

Panel-plating routes also exist. The exact routing can differ, but the functional need to produce conductive hole barrels and final outer circuitry remains.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** outer-layer plating-resist image on a conductive metallized panel.
- **Operation:** electroplate copper onto exposed circuitry and hole walls; apply temporary etch-resist metal where the process requires it.
- **Physical change:** outer conductors thicken and the initial thin hole metallization develops into a stronger continuous copper barrel.
- **Immediate risk:** insufficient or excessive copper build-up, nonuniform plating, hole-wall voids, poor current distribution, or plating onto unintended areas.
- **Verification:** representative copper build-up, barrel coverage, process coupons, and plating controls are checked before resist stripping and etching.

This operation is where the drilled and initially metallized hole becomes much closer to the finished electrical interconnection discussed in Section 2.6.

---

## 2.8.12 - Outer-Layer Etching

After pattern plating, the desired outer circuitry is protected by the plated copper and, in many routes, a temporary metallic etch resist. The remaining plating resist is removed to expose the unwanted base copper.

The panel then enters an outer-layer etching operation.

Unprotected base copper is removed, leaving the plated conductor pattern behind. If a temporary tin or other etch-resist layer was used, that temporary metal is subsequently stripped where required by the process.

The final outer copper geometry now emerges:

- traces;
- pads;
- planes/pours;
- annular rings;
- plated-hole connections.

Outer-layer etching has the same general material-removal idea as inner-layer etching, but the material stack and process history are different because the desired features have received electroplated copper.

Etching variation can change conductor width and spacing. Residual copper can create shorts; excessive removal can narrow or open conductors. Registration errors from earlier imaging can now appear as pad/hole or clearance problems.

Before the copper is hidden beneath solder mask, outer circuitry is commonly inspected using optical and process-control methods appropriate to the fabricator.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** pattern-plated panel with plating resist and protected intended circuitry.
- **Operation:** strip plating resist, etch unwanted base copper, and remove temporary etch-resist metal where applicable.
- **Physical change:** the continuous outer copper background becomes isolated final outer-layer conductors while plated hole barrels remain connected to intended pads/layers.
- **Immediate risk:** residual copper, overetch, underetch, conductor narrowing, opens, shorts, or registration-related geometry loss.
- **Verification:** outer-layer pattern, conductor geometry, pad/hole relationship, and absence of obvious shorts/opens are checked before solder mask.

## 2.8.13 - Solder Mask Application and Imaging

Once the outer copper circuitry is complete, most of that copper should no longer remain exposed.

A **solder mask** is applied over the outer surfaces. Liquid photoimageable solder mask is common, although other mask technologies exist. The mask is imaged and developed so that selected conductive features remain exposed while traces and other protected copper are covered.

The exposed openings may include:

- component pads;
- test points;
- selected plated holes;
- connector/contact areas;
- other features that must remain accessible.

The solder mask is then cured to develop its final protective properties.

The physical transformation is not simply a color change. Before solder mask, outer copper traces and pads can be broadly exposed. After mask processing, only intentionally accessible areas remain open.

Mask registration matters. If the mask shifts relative to copper, it can partially cover a pad, reduce the opening around a fine-pitch feature, expose unintended copper, or reduce the mask web between adjacent pads.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** inspected bare outer circuitry.
- **Operation:** clean/prepare surface, apply solder mask, image/develop openings, and cure the mask.
- **Physical change:** most outer copper becomes covered by a permanent insulating/protective polymer while selected pads and contact features remain exposed.
- **Immediate risk:** misregistration, mask on pad, unintended copper exposure, insufficient mask web, pinholes/voids, contamination, poor adhesion, or incomplete cure.
- **Verification:** opening registration, coverage, cure/adhesion, cleanliness, and visible defects are checked before surface finishing or subsequent marking steps.

Solder mask protects the copper that should remain covered. The next operation protects the copper that intentionally remains exposed.

---

## 2.8.14 - Surface Finish

The solder-mask process leaves selected pads, contacts, and other required conductive surfaces exposed.

Those copper surfaces must now be protected from oxidation and prepared for their downstream function. This is the role of the **surface finish** developed in Section 2.7.

Depending on the board specification, the finish may be:

- HASL or lead-free HASL;
- ENIG;
- OSP;
- immersion silver;
- immersion tin;
- a selective or specialized finish;
- another qualified finish system.

The exact manufacturing chemistry and therefore the detailed process sequence differ significantly among finishes. HASL involves a solder-based coating and hot-air leveling; ENIG builds electroless nickel with a thin immersion-gold layer; OSP applies a very thin organic preservative. These are different processes serving a common functional need.

Surface finish is applied only where the board design and mask openings require exposed conductive functionality.

The finish must not be thought of as decoration. It directly affects solderability, pad planarity, storage robustness, contact behavior, inspection, and downstream assembly.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** solder-masked panel with intentionally exposed copper features.
- **Operation:** apply the specified metallic or organic surface finish using the qualified process for that finish type.
- **Physical change:** exposed copper receives a protective/functional surface while solder-mask-covered copper remains masked.
- **Immediate risk:** incomplete coverage, contamination, excessive or insufficient deposition, poor planarity, finish-specific process defects, or unintended coating of areas that should remain different.
- **Verification:** finish coverage, appearance/process controls, solderability-related requirements, and any finish-specific inspection criteria are checked before release to later fabrication steps.

The representative sequence here places surface finishing before legend/marking, but the exact order of finish and nomenclature operations can vary with the fabricator and selected finish system.

---

## 2.8.15 - Legend and Marking

A finished bare PCB must often carry information that helps assembly, inspection, service, traceability, and product identification.

The **legend**, sometimes called silkscreen or nomenclature, may include:

- component reference designators;
- polarity/orientation marks;
- connector identifiers;
- board part number and revision;
- test or inspection markings;
- manufacturer or lot information where required;
- regulatory or product-specific markings.

Traditional screen printing is one method. Digital inkjet and other marking processes may also be used.

This operation does not create an electrical conductor. It adds readable information to selected board surfaces without interfering with pads, contacts, test points, holes, or other functional features.

Placement matters. A legend that overlaps a solderable pad, masks a test point, obscures a fiducial, or becomes unreadable after assembly does not fulfill its intended manufacturing function.

Marking materials must also be compatible with the fabrication and assembly sequence. Cure conditions and adhesion matter because the legend should remain legible through later handling and assembly.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** electrically patterned, solder-masked, and finished panel.
- **Operation:** apply and cure/complete the specified nomenclature or digital markings.
- **Physical change:** permanent or controlled markings are added to the board surface without changing the circuit connectivity.
- **Immediate risk:** marking over pads/test points, poor registration, illegible text/symbols, missing revision/identifier, poor adhesion, or incomplete cure.
- **Verification:** content, location, legibility, orientation, and interference with functional features are checked.

Although electrically passive, good marking is part of manufacturability because it reduces assembly and inspection ambiguity.

## 2.8.16 - Profiling, Routing, and Board-Outline Features

Until late in fabrication, individual PCB images are often processed as part of a larger manufacturing panel.

The panel must now be converted into the required physical board geometry.

**Profiling** creates the final board outline and may also create:

- routed slots;
- cutouts;
- notches;
- edge features;
- tabs;
- breakaway features;
- rails or other panelization features;
- V-score lines where that process is specified.

NC routing is widely used, while scoring, punching, or other processes may be appropriate for particular board constructions and production routes.

An important distinction is that profiling does not always mean that every PCB is fully separated into an individual loose board. For assembly efficiency, multiple boards may remain connected in a **panelized array** using tabs, breakaway webs, or score lines. Final depanelization can occur later after component assembly.

This operation is therefore better understood as creating the required **board-outline and depanelization geometry**, not simply "cutting the board out."

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** processed manufacturing panel containing completed bare-board images.
- **Operation:** route, score, mill, or otherwise create board outlines, slots, cutouts, and panelization/depanelization features.
- **Physical change:** a rectangular process panel becomes the specified board or board-array geometry.
- **Immediate risk:** dimensional error, wrong slot/cutout size, burrs, damaged edges, exposed/cut copper near the edge, delamination, incorrect score depth, or weakened breakaway features.
- **Verification:** outline dimensions, slot/cutout location, edge quality, panel tabs/rails, and critical copper-to-edge relationships are checked.

Detailed capability values and edge-clearance rules are developed in Section 2.9 and Chapter 3.

## 2.8.17 - Electrical Testing

A PCB can look correct and still contain an electrical open or short.

Before shipment, the bare board is therefore electrically tested against the intended circuit connectivity.

Two broad functions are checked:

- **continuity:** features that are supposed to be electrically connected actually are connected;
- **isolation:** features that are supposed to remain electrically separate do not have an unintended short.

Testing can use equipment such as:

- flying probes;
- dedicated fixtures / bed-of-nails systems;
- other qualified electrical-test methods.

The test method depends on production volume, board complexity, access, economics, and customer requirements.

Bare-board electrical testing can reveal manufacturing defects created earlier in the process, including:

- etched-open conductors;
- plating discontinuities;
- broken via/barrel connections;
- residual-copper shorts;
- unintended layer-to-layer connections.

Electrical test does **not** verify component operation because no components are mounted yet. It also does not replace dimensional, material, plating, mask, or visual inspection. A board can pass netlist continuity/isolation and still contain another nonconformance.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** profiled or panelized completed bare PCB with final conductive pattern.
- **Operation:** compare measured electrical continuity/isolation against the intended netlist or test program.
- **Physical change:** normally none; this is a verification operation rather than a material-conversion step.
- **Immediate risk:** true opens/shorts, intermittent connections, inadequate test contact, wrong test program, fixture/probe error, or false rejection.
- **Verification:** failing nets are identified, retested/confirmed as appropriate, and boards are accepted or rejected according to the qualified test process.

This operation closes the loop between the digital circuit definition and the fabricated physical interconnect network.

## 2.8.18 - Final Inspection and Quality Verification

Electrical test is necessary, but it is not the final manufacturing question.

The fabricator must also verify that the completed bare board conforms to the required **physical, dimensional, material, finish, and workmanship expectations** before shipment.

Final verification may include, depending on the product and specification:

- visual inspection of surfaces and edges;
- solder-mask and legend condition;
- surface-finish coverage;
- hole and slot condition;
- board outline and critical dimensions;
- cleanliness;
- visible laminate damage;
- warpage/bow-and-twist checks where required;
- review of electrical-test results;
- process coupons or microsections where required by the manufacturing/quality plan;
- documentation, lot identity, and traceability records.

Not every board or production lot is inspected using exactly the same methods or sampling plan. The required verification depends on the product specification, customer agreement, performance class, fabricator quality system, and applicable standards.

The important manufacturing concept is that **quality verification combines evidence from multiple operations**. A final inspector cannot recreate all previous process information simply by looking at the board. This is why process records, tests, coupons, and inspection data can matter alongside final visual condition.

### Manufacturing view

- **Input:** electrically tested finished bare boards or panelized arrays.
- **Operation:** perform final visual/dimensional/workmanship verification and review required manufacturing/test records.
- **Physical change:** normally none, except identification/packaging actions after acceptance.
- **Immediate risk:** shipping a board with cosmetic or structural damage, incorrect dimensions, poor finish/mask condition, unresolved test failure, contamination, or incomplete traceability.
- **Verification:** required acceptance checks are completed, nonconforming product is segregated, and accepted product is prepared for shipment or downstream assembly.

Detailed defect mechanisms are developed in Section 2.12, while manufacturing data, quality records, and engineering-supplier communication are developed in Section 2.13.

## 2.8.19 - Seeing the Entire Fabrication Chain as One Transformation

The individual operations are easier to remember when they are tied to what physically exists after each manufacturing phase.

| Manufacturing phase | Starting condition | Major operations | Physical result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Create inner copper layers** | Copper-clad cores | Clean, image, develop, etch, strip, inspect | Patterned and accepted inner-layer cores |
| **Build multilayer structure** | Patterned cores + prepreg + outer copper foil | Layup, registration, lamination, cure | Bonded rigid multilayer panel |
| **Create vertical interconnections** | Laminated panel | Drill, desmear, initial metallization | Conductive hole-wall starting layer connected to exposed inner-layer copper |
| **Create outer circuitry** | Metallized panel | Outer imaging, pattern plating, etching | Final outer traces/pads plus plated-hole barrels |
| **Protect and finish surfaces** | Exposed completed circuitry | Solder mask, surface finish, legend | Protected/finished functional bare-board surfaces |
| **Create final geometry and verify** | Finished manufacturing panel | Profiling, electrical test, final inspection | Verified bare PCB or assembly-ready panelized array |

Several manufacturing lessons become visible when the complete chain is considered.

### The process is cumulative

Every operation receives the result of the previous operation.

Poor inner-layer registration can later become a drilling/annular-ring problem. Poor hole preparation can later become a plating/reliability problem. Poor outer-layer registration can later become a solder-mask or assembly problem.

### Inspection should occur before information becomes difficult to access

Inner layers are inspected before lamination because they will become hidden. Outer circuitry is inspected before solder mask makes some features harder to see. Electrical testing verifies the final connectivity that visual inspection alone cannot guarantee.

### Material addition and material removal alternate

PCB fabrication repeatedly uses both:

- **material removal:** etching, drilling, routing;
- **material addition:** resin bonding, electroless deposition, electroplating, solder mask, surface finish, legend.

Understanding the physical change is more useful than memorizing equipment names.

### The board is still a bare PCB

Even after surface finish, marking, profiling, and electrical test, no packaged components have yet been assembled.

The manufacturing streams remain:

> **PCB materials -> PCB fabrication -> verified bare PCB**

and later:

> **verified bare PCB + packaged/discrete components -> PCB assembly -> PCBA**

This distinction prevents PCB fabrication from being confused with component assembly or semiconductor fabrication.

## Key Terms Introduced in This Section

**Manufacturing panel**  
A larger production panel used to process one or more future PCB images together with tooling borders, coupons, and other process features.

**Photoresist**  
A light-sensitive temporary material used to transfer circuit-image information to a physical copper-processing surface.

**Imaging**  
The operation that transfers circuit data into a patterned photoresist or plating-resist layer.

**Etching**  
Controlled removal of unwanted copper to create isolated conductors.

**Layup**  
Arrangement and registration of patterned cores, prepreg, and copper foil in the specified multilayer order before lamination.

**Lamination**  
Heat-and-pressure process that causes prepreg resin to flow and cure, bonding the multilayer stack into a rigid panel.

**Desmear**  
Hole-preparation process that removes resin smear and exposes/conditions surfaces for reliable plated interconnection.

**Electroless copper**  
Copper deposited chemically without using the workpiece as an electroplating electrode, commonly used to establish a conductive starting layer on prepared hole walls.

**Direct metallization**  
A family of processes that make nonconductive hole walls sufficiently conductive for subsequent electroplating without relying on the same conventional electroless-copper route.

**Pattern plating**  
A fabrication route in which resist defines where selected circuit features and hole walls receive additional electroplated metal.

**Etch resist**  
A temporary protective material or metal that prevents selected copper from being removed during etching.

**AOI**  
Automated optical inspection; machine-vision-based inspection used to compare manufactured PCB features with expected pattern information.

**Profiling**  
Creation of the required PCB or panel outline, slots, cutouts, and related edge/depanelization features.

**Electrical test**  
Bare-board verification of required continuity and isolation relative to the intended circuit connectivity.

## Section Takeaway

Rigid multilayer PCB fabrication is best understood as a sequence of **functional material transformations**, not as one universal list of machine steps.

The representative chain is:

> **prepare materials -> pattern and inspect inner layers -> lay up and laminate -> drill -> desmear -> establish conductive hole walls -> image and plate outer circuitry -> etch outer layers -> apply solder mask -> apply surface finish -> mark -> profile -> electrically test -> inspect**

The most important physical ideas are:

- inner layers are patterned **before** lamination;
- prepreg resin flows and cures to bond the multilayer structure;
- drilling creates holes but **not** electrical interconnections;
- desmear and initial metallization prepare those holes for copper plating;
- outer-layer pattern plating builds conductor and via-barrel copper before unwanted base copper is etched away;
- solder mask protects copper that should remain covered;
- surface finish protects intentionally exposed copper;
- profiling creates the required physical board/panel geometry;
- electrical testing and final inspection verify the finished **bare PCB** before assembly.

Section 2.9 next shifts from **how the processes work** to **what those real processes can reliably produce**. That requires understanding fabrication capability, tolerance, producibility, yield, and the difference between a physical process limit and a preferred manufacturing capability.
