# Figure 2.9.1 - Different Sources of PCB Manufacturing Limits and Requirements

## Instructional Purpose

This figure should help readers distinguish **different sources of manufacturing limits, design rules, and product requirements** before any numerical capability is discussed.

The central teaching point is:

> **Physical process limits, fabricator capabilities, EDA rules, industry requirements, and customer requirements are related, but they are not interchangeable.**

The figure should support Section 2.9.1 and reinforce the section-wide rule:

> **A manufacturing minimum is not automatically a good design target.**

The figure should not become a detailed standards hierarchy or supplier-selection chart. Its purpose is conceptual separation and reconciliation.

## Recommended Composition

Use a clean **three-zone engineering diagram**, not a poster-style infographic.

### Left zone - What manufacturing can physically and practically do

Show three vertically related items:

1. **Physical process limit**
   - smallest/tightest boundary imposed by process physics, equipment, materials, and process control;
   - visually represent this as the outermost or most aggressive capability boundary.

2. **Fabricator minimum capability**
   - the tightest feature/tolerance the selected fabricator states it can manufacture under stated conditions;
   - visually show it as a supplier-specific working boundary inside or near the process limit.

3. **Preferred / recommended capability**
   - a less aggressive region with more manufacturing margin;
   - visually show it as a safer operating region farther from the capability edge.

A simple nested-margin graphic is preferred:

`physical process limit -> fabricator minimum -> preferred region`

The diagram must not imply that these three values are universal numbers.

### Center zone - What the design tool checks

Show one separate item:

4. **EDA / design rule**
   - a rule configured in the PCB design system;
   - visually place it between the manufacturing and requirement zones;
   - include a small note:
     `must be configured from intended manufacturing requirements`.

The EDA rule should not be drawn as automatically equal to the supplier minimum.

### Right zone - What the product must satisfy

Show two separate items:

5. **Industry-standard requirement**
   - applicable design, material, test, or acceptance requirement;
   - do not imply that an industry standard defines one universal supplier capability.

6. **Customer / product requirement**
   - drawing, specification, purchase, product, or application requirement;
   - show that it may be tighter or simply different from normal supplier defaults.

### Bottom integration band

All three zones should feed into one final engineering action:

> **Reconcile before design release / fabrication review**

Optional short sublabel:

`Capability + configured design rules + applicable requirements`

The bottom integration band should visually communicate that the engineer must compare and reconcile these different inputs before release.

---

## Required Technical Relationships

The figure must preserve all of the following relationships:

- **Physical process limit** is not the same as **fabricator minimum capability**.
- **Fabricator minimum capability** is not the same as **preferred capability**.
- **Preferred capability** should visually imply greater process margin, not a tighter feature.
- **EDA / design rule** is a configured checking rule, not an independent manufacturing capability.
- **Industry-standard requirement** is not automatically a supplier capability statement.
- **Customer / product requirement** can add or tighten requirements for a specific product.
- All sources must be reconciled before design release and manufacturing review.
- The diagram must not imply one universal minimum PCB rule.

## Geometry and Visual Logic

### Manufacturing-capability zone

Use a nested or stepped visual relationship where:

- the most aggressive edge corresponds to the **physical process limit**;
- the supplier's stated **minimum capability** is shown as a more practical but still aggressive boundary;
- the **preferred / recommended capability** is shown farther from the edge with visibly greater margin.

The preferred region should be visually larger or more comfortable, not physically smaller in a way that could be misread.

A small double-headed arrow or spacing band labeled `Manufacturing margin` can show the difference between preferred design geometry and the fabricator minimum.

### EDA zone

Use a CAD/check-rule icon or simple rule box, not a computer screenshot.

The center box should receive an arrow from the intended manufacturing capability information and then feed the release/reconciliation band.

### Requirements zone

Keep industry and customer requirements as separate parallel boxes.

Do not draw the customer requirement as subordinate to the industry requirement; either one may add relevant constraints depending on the product.

## Scope Boundaries

Do **not** add:

- numerical trace/space values;
- numerical drill limits;
- annular-ring dimensions;
- solder-mask limits;
- IPC document numbers;
- fabricator names;
- supplier logos;
- acceptance-class tables;
- process capability indices such as Cp/Cpk;
- statistical distributions;
- component-placement or assembly DFM rules.

Those topics are either covered elsewhere in Section 2.9 or deferred to Chapter 3 / later sections.

This figure is about **where limits and requirements come from**, not the numerical values themselves.

## Technical Verification Basis

### Primary curriculum basis

The Chapter 2 instructional material establishes that PCB manufacturing has process-dependent constraints and that typical numerical values are examples rather than universal rules.

The approved Section 2.9 outline explicitly requires distinction among:

- physical process limit;
- fabricator minimum capability;
- preferred/recommended capability;
- EDA/design rule;
- industry-standard requirement;
- customer/product requirement.

### Secondary verification basis

PCB manufacturing references support the need to compare CAD/design-rule checks with actual supplier capability and show that violating manufacturing capability can reduce yield.

No external web source is required for this conceptual figure.
