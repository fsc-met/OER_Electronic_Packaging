## Suggested Content for Figure 2.5.5

**Figure title for authoring/reference only:**  
`Copper Balance and Thieving Concepts`

**Instructional purpose:**  
Provide a clear recognition-level comparison that distinguishes **copper balance**, **dummy copper**, and **copper thieving** without treating the terms as exact synonyms.

The figure should support the Section 2.5 teaching distinction:

> **Copper balance is an engineering goal; dummy copper is intentionally added nonfunctional copper; thieving is a manufacturing-oriented use of added copper, commonly for plating-area/process uniformity.**

The figure should also reinforce that severe copper-density differences can affect manufacturing uniformity and can contribute to stiffness/residual-stress imbalance and warpage tendency, while avoiding the false idea that a balanced PCB must have identical mirror-image copper patterns.

The figure should answer:

> **What does an obvious copper-distribution imbalance look like, how can intentionally added copper improve balance, and how can thieving be used at the manufacturing-panel level?**

**Filename:**  
`2.5.5 - Copper Balance and Thieving Concepts.png`

**Intended width:** 900 px in Markdown

**Recommended aspect ratio:** approximately 16:9

# Panel 1 - Strong Copper Imbalance

Show a simplified bare-PCB top view with a deliberately strong and obvious difference in copper density.

Recommended geometry:

- one side or region contains a large solid copper area and several copper features;
- the opposite side or region is comparatively sparse, with only a few traces/pads;
- the contrast should be clear without using numerical area percentages.

Required panel label:

- `Strong copper imbalance`

Optional concise secondary label:

- `Dense region`
- `Sparse region`

The panel should visually communicate **uneven copper distribution**, not an electrical fault.

Do not use a red X, warning triangle, failure icon, or language implying that every nonuniform copper layout is unacceptable.

Do not imply that the layout shown will always warp.

The correct teaching message is only that **large, unnecessary imbalance can increase manufacturing and deformation risk**.

# Panel 2 - Improved Copper Balance

Show a second bare-PCB top view with approximately the same functional routing concept but a more even overall copper distribution.

Recommended geometry:

- preserve recognizable functional traces/pads from Panel 1;
- retain the main functional large copper area where appropriate;
- add simple nonfunctional copper shapes in otherwise sparse unused regions;
- distribute the added shapes so the overall copper density is less severely uneven.

Required panel label:

- `Improved copper balance`

Required added-copper label:

- `Example dummy copper`

Optional concise note:

- `Nonfunctional copper`

The added copper should be visually distinct enough to identify but should still look like copper on the board.

A simple repeated pattern such as small rectangles, circles, or hatched copper islands may be used.

The figure should not imply that dummy copper must always have one particular shape or must always be electrically isolated. The shown pattern is only one conceptual example.

Do not make Panel 2 an exact mirror image of Panel 1 or of itself about the board centerline.

The key relationship is **reduced severe imbalance**, not geometric symmetry for its own sake.

# Panel 3 - Panel-Level Thieving Example

Show a simplified rectangular **manufacturing panel** containing one or more finished-board images or board outlines.

Recommended geometry:

- outer rectangle = manufacturing panel;
- one or two internal board images/outlines;
- unused panel area around or between board images;
- repeated nonfunctional copper features in the unused panel area.

Required labels:

- `Manufacturing panel`
- `PCB image`
- `Thieving`

Optional concise label:

- `Nonfunctional copper`

The thieving pattern may use repeated dots, small squares, short bars, or another simple pattern.

Required relationship:

- thieving must be clearly shown in **unused panel area** in this example;
- the thieving must not be mistaken for functional circuit routing on the finished PCB;
- the panel boundary must be visually different from the board/image boundary.

The panel-level presentation is intentionally chosen because it clearly communicates one common manufacturing use of thieving: balancing the platable area across the manufacturing panel.

This is an **example**, not a universal location rule. The surrounding Section 2.5 text explains that nonfunctional copper may also be used in unused board areas where appropriate.

# Required Technical Relationships

The final figure must correctly communicate all of the following:

- copper balance concerns the **amount and distribution of copper**, not exact visual symmetry;
- a strongly uneven copper layout can be recognized by large dense-versus-sparse differences;
- intentionally added nonfunctional copper can be used to reduce unnecessary copper-density imbalance;
- added dummy copper should not replace or obscure required functional routing;
- dummy copper is shown as an example, not as one mandatory geometry or connection state;
- thieving is nonfunctional copper used for manufacturing-process control;
- the panel-level thieving example is associated with improving platable-area/process uniformity;
- thieving is distinct from the functional copper pattern of the PCB image;
- copper balancing and thieving can support manufacturability but do not guarantee a perfectly flat board or perfectly uniform process;
- the figure does not imply that every PCB requires dummy copper or thieving.
