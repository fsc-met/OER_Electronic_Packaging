# 2.12 - Bare-Board Defects and Fabrication/Material Failure Modes

Section 2.11 treated the PCB as part of a thermal path. This section returns to the **fabricated bare board** and asks a different question:

> **What can go wrong in the PCB itself before components are assembled onto it?**

A bare PCB can contain defects or nonconformances in:

- copper patterns;
- holes and plated-through structures;
- laminate and bonded interfaces;
- pads and lands;
- solder mask;
- surface finish;
- overall board geometry.

Some defects are immediately electrical, such as an open or short circuit. Others are structural or material defects that may not fail electrically during a simple continuity test but can reduce manufacturing margin or later reliability.

The goal here is not to memorize a long defect catalog. The more useful Engineering Technology skill is to connect:

> **Observation -> Structure/Process Mechanism -> Likely Causes -> Evidence -> Corrective Action**

This section stays focused on **bare-board fabrication and material conditions**. Solder-joint defects, component cracking, assembly-process defects, and broader field-reliability mechanisms are developed later in Chapters 4, 7, and 8.

---

## 2.12.1 - Start With the Failure Location

A PCB defect becomes easier to diagnose when it is first classified by **where it occurs**.

A useful first-pass classification is:

1. **Copper-pattern defects**  
   Traces, spaces, planes, pads, and etched features.

2. **Hole and plating defects**  
   Drilled-hole position, annular ring, plated barrel, and internal layer connection.

3. **Laminate and interface defects**  
   Core/prepreg regions, copper-to-laminate interfaces, and internal separations.

4. **Surface-condition defects**  
   Solder mask, surface finish, contamination, and exposed copper.

5. **Global geometry defects**  
   Bow, twist, thickness, profile, and dimensional nonconformance.

6. **Moisture/electrochemical degradation mechanisms**  
   Conditions that may develop inside the laminate under moisture and electrical bias.

This location-first approach is useful because the **same visible symptom can have different root causes**.

For example, an electrically open net could result from:

- a broken or over-etched trace;
- a plating void in a through hole;
- a missing internal-layer connection;
- handling damage after fabrication.

The electrical symptom alone does not identify the physical defect.

## 2.12.2 - Copper-Pattern Defects: Opens, Shorts, and Etch Nonconformance

Copper features are created through imaging, development, etching, plating, and related fabrication steps introduced in Section 2.8.

Common copper-pattern defects include:

### Open conductor

An **open conductor** is an unintended break in an electrical path.

Possible physical conditions include:

- a trace etched completely through;
- a nick, scratch, or local copper loss;
- incomplete connection between a trace and pad;
- a defect at an internal-layer feature.

An open conductor is usually detected by electrical continuity testing, but optical inspection can help locate the physical break.

### Short circuit

A **short circuit** is an unintended conductive connection between features that should be electrically isolated.

Possible bare-board causes include:

- residual copper between traces;
- an imaging or etching defect;
- conductive debris;
- insufficient spacing caused by process variation;
- copper bridging between adjacent features.

### Over-etch and under-etch

**Over-etching** removes more copper than intended.

Possible results include:

- reduced conductor width;
- reduced cross-sectional area;
- locally weakened traces;
- complete opens in severe cases.

**Under-etching** leaves more copper than intended.

Possible results include:

- conductors wider than intended;
- reduced spacing between adjacent conductors;
- residual copper;
- increased short-circuit risk.

The important principle is that a trace can be **electrically continuous but still geometrically nonconforming**.

## 2.12.3 - Hole Registration, Annular-Ring, and Plating Defects

Section 2.6 introduced the structure of plated holes and vias, while Section 2.9 introduced drill position, annular ring, and aspect-ratio capability.

Those same features create several important defect modes.

### Hole registration error and annular-ring breakout

The drilled hole must align adequately with the intended copper land.

If drill position and copper-feature position shift relative to each other:

- the annular ring becomes thinner on one side;
- the hole may approach the pad edge;
- severe misregistration can create **annular-ring breakout**.

Breakout is therefore not simply a "small pad" problem. It is the result of the combined geometry of:

- pad size;
- finished-hole size;
- drill position;
- layer registration;
- fabrication variation.

### Plating void

A **plating void** is a local absence or discontinuity in the intended conductive plating on a hole wall or other plated feature.

In a plated-through structure, a void can interrupt the vertical electrical path or weaken the connection to an internal layer.

A board can therefore fail even when the drilled hole itself is present and correctly located.

### Insufficient or nonuniform hole-wall plating

If the copper barrel is thinner than intended or highly nonuniform, the hole may:

- have increased electrical resistance;
- have lower mechanical margin;
- become more vulnerable to later thermomechanical loading.

High aspect ratio, difficult hole preparation, and plating-process nonuniformity can make uniform deposition more challenging.

### Barrel cracking awareness

A plated barrel can also contain or later develop a crack.

In the bare-board context, the important recognition point is:

> **A weak or defective plated barrel may be a fabrication condition even if the final electrical failure appears later during thermal exposure or service.**

Detailed cyclic-fatigue and thermomechanical fracture analysis belongs in later chapters.

## 2.12.4 - Delamination, Blistering, Measling, Crazing, and Lifted Lands

A multilayer PCB depends on adhesion among:

- copper;
- resin;
- glass reinforcement;
- core and prepreg layers.

Defects at these interfaces can be visible at the surface or hidden inside the board.

### Delamination

**Delamination** is a separation between bonded layers or between the laminate and metal cladding.

Possible contributors include:

- weak bonding;
- contamination;
- inadequate lamination;
- thermal stress;
- moisture;
- material/process incompatibility.

The observed separation should be treated as evidence; the actual cause must still be verified.

Delamination is a structural separation, not merely a change in color.

### Blistering

**Blistering** is a localized swelling associated with separation between layers or between laminate and metal cladding.

The key distinction is geometric:

- **delamination** describes separation;
- **blistering** is a localized raised/swollen form of separation.

### Measling and crazing awareness

**Measling** and **crazing** are internal laminate conditions associated with local glass-fiber/resin separation.

They often appear as white features visible beneath the surface.

At awareness level:

- measling tends to appear as more discrete white spots or crosses;
- crazing tends to form more connected white patterns.

These conditions should be treated as evidence of laminate/interface disturbance rather than as a purely cosmetic discoloration.

### Lifted land or pad

A **lifted land/pad** occurs when a copper land separates from the supporting laminate.

Possible contributors include:

- inadequate copper-to-laminate adhesion;
- excessive thermal exposure;
- mechanical loading;
- handling or later rework.

For bare-board inspection, the important feature is loss of adhesion or separation between the copper feature and the board surface.

## 2.12.5 - Moisture Can Amplify Laminate Damage

FR-4-type materials can absorb moisture from the environment.

Moisture does not automatically mean that a board has failed. The concern is that moisture can interact with:

- temperature;
- thermal expansion;
- internal interfaces;
- ionic contamination;
- electrical bias.

During later heating, absorbed moisture can contribute to vapor pressure and can aggravate existing weak interfaces, increasing the risk of blistering or delamination.

This is one reason storage, material condition, fabrication control, and later thermal exposure are connected.

The detailed material-science and reliability treatment belongs later in the book.

## 2.12.6 - Conductive Anodic Filament (CAF) Awareness

**Conductive anodic filament (CAF)** is an electrochemical degradation mechanism that can create a conductive path through reinforced PCB laminate.

CAF is not simply a copper bridge left from etching.

At awareness level, CAF development depends on a combination of conditions such as:

- moisture;
- electrical potential difference;
- mobile ionic species;
- a susceptible path within the reinforced laminate;
- time and temperature.

CAF growth commonly follows susceptible paths associated with the glass-fiber/resin structure.

The practical concern is that two conductors that were initially isolated can gradually develop a lower-resistance path through the laminate.

Factors that can influence CAF risk include:

- conductor or via spacing;
- material system;
- drilling-related damage;
- laminate quality;
- moisture exposure;
- cleanliness/ionic contamination;
- applied voltage;
- temperature.

## 2.12.7 - Solder-Mask and Surface-Finish Defects

A bare PCB can also fail inspection because the outer protective and solderable surfaces are not produced as intended.

### Solder-mask defects

Examples include:

- misregistration;
- mask covering part of a pad;
- exposed copper where coverage was intended;
- insufficient mask web between features;
- bubbles, blisters, or local separation;
- physical damage.

A solder mask is useful for insulation, solder control, and limited environmental protection, but it should not be treated as a waterproof seal.

### Surface-finish defects

Surface-finish problems can include:

- incomplete or nonuniform coverage;
- local contamination;
- oxidation or discoloration;
- rough or damaged contact surfaces;
- solderability problems.

The exact failure mechanism depends on the selected finish.

Section 2.7 introduced finish selection. This section only emphasizes that the finished surface must be:

- present where required;
- reasonably uniform;
- clean;
- compatible with the intended assembly process.

Detailed finish chemistry and assembly solderability behavior are outside the scope of this section.

## 2.12.8 - Bow and Twist Can Also Be Bare-Board Nonconformances

Warpage is not only a later assembly problem.

A fabricated bare board can already have excessive:

- **bow**;
- **twist**;
- local flatness deviation.

Section 2.10 explained the mechanics and geometry in detail.

In the failure-inspection context, the important question is:

> **Does the board geometry remain within the controlled fabrication/product requirement for its intended use?**

Possible contributors include:

- asymmetric stack-up;
- copper imbalance;
- residual lamination stress;
- uneven cooling;
- material variability;
- handling or storage.

No additional warpage figure is needed here because Figure 2.10.3 already distinguishes bow and twist.

## 2.12.9 - A Defect Is Not the Same as Its Root Cause

A manufacturing engineer should avoid jumping directly from a visible defect to one assumed cause.

Consider a plating void.

### Observation

Electrical discontinuity through one plated hole.

### Structure/process mechanism

The intended conductive barrel is locally discontinuous.

### Likely causes to investigate

Examples may include:

- inadequate hole-wall preparation;
- contamination;
- trapped gas;
- incomplete activation;
- plating-process nonuniformity.

### Evidence

Useful evidence can include:

- electrical continuity/isolation testing;
- optical inspection;
- microsection/cross-section;
- plating-thickness measurement;
- comparison with nearby holes;
- process and lot history.

### Corrective action

Corrective action should address the verified cause, not only the symptom.

Examples could include:

- adjusting hole preparation;
- correcting plating-process control;
- changing design geometry if the feature exceeds practical capability;
- improving inspection or process monitoring.

This same logic can be applied to copper, laminate, registration, and surface defects.

## 2.12.10 - Practical Bare-Board Troubleshooting Table

The table below applies the same troubleshooting logic to a broader set of bare-board observations.

| Observation | Structure / mechanism to inspect | Example evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Open net | Broken conductor or discontinuous plated connection | Electrical test, optical inspection, microsection |
| Unintended short | Residual copper, copper bridge, conductive debris | Electrical isolation test, optical inspection |
| Narrow trace / reduced spacing | Etch or imaging nonconformance | Dimensional inspection, artwork/process comparison |
| Annular-ring breakout | Hole-to-pad misregistration | Optical inspection, dimensional measurement |
| Intermittent via | Plating void, thin plating, barrel damage | Electrical test, microsection, plating measurement |
| Raised laminate region | Blistering or local delamination | Visual inspection, cross-section if needed |
| White internal laminate features | Measling/crazing or other laminate disturbance | Visual inspection, material/process review |
| Lifted pad | Copper-to-laminate adhesion loss | Visual inspection, adhesion/process evidence |
| Solder-mask pad encroachment | Mask registration error | Optical inspection, artwork comparison |
| Poor solderable surface | Surface-finish or contamination problem | Visual inspection, finish/solderability evaluation |
| Excessive bow/twist | Global residual-stress or geometry problem | Flatness/warpage measurement |
| Reduced insulation resistance over time | Moisture/electrochemical mechanism such as CAF | Environmental/electrical testing and failure analysis |

The table provides a **starting point**, not a one-to-one cause map. Multiple mechanisms can produce similar observations, so evidence is needed before corrective action is selected.

## Key Terms Introduced in This Section

**Open conductor**  
An unintended break in a conductive path.

**Short circuit**  
An unintended conductive connection between nodes that should be electrically isolated.

**Annular-ring breakout**  
A condition in which drill/feature misregistration removes the intended continuous copper land around part of a hole.

**Plating void**  
A local absence or discontinuity in intended conductive plating.

**Delamination**  
Separation between bonded laminate layers or between laminate and metal cladding.

**Blistering**  
A localized raised or swollen separation within the laminate structure or at a laminate/metal interface.

**Measling**  
An internal laminate condition commonly visible as discrete white spots or crosses associated with local glass-resin separation.

**Crazing**  
An internal laminate condition commonly visible as more connected white patterns associated with glass-resin separation.

**Lifted land**  
A copper land or pad that has separated from the supporting laminate.

**Conductive anodic filament (CAF)**  
An electrochemical conductive path that can develop through reinforced PCB laminate under a suitable combination of moisture, electrical bias, ionic species, material condition, and time.

## Section Takeaway

Bare-board defects should be understood by **physical location and mechanism**, not only by their electrical symptom.

The most important ideas are:

- opens and shorts can originate in the copper pattern or in plated interconnections;
- over-etch and under-etch can create dimensional nonconformance before complete electrical failure occurs;
- hole registration and plating integrity are separate engineering issues;
- annular-ring breakout is a geometric registration problem;
- plating voids, thin plating, and barrel damage affect the conductive hole wall;
- delamination, blistering, measling, and crazing are laminate/interface conditions;
- lifted lands indicate loss of copper-to-laminate adhesion;
- solder-mask and surface-finish quality affect later assembly and product performance;
- bow and twist can already be present in the bare board;
- moisture can aggravate laminate damage and contributes to electrochemical reliability risk;
- CAF is an internal electrochemical mechanism, not simply a surface copper bridge;
- the most useful troubleshooting sequence is **Observation -> Mechanism -> Likely Causes -> Evidence -> Corrective Action**.
