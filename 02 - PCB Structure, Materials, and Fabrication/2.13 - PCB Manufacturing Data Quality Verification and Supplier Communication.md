# 2.13 - PCB Manufacturing Data, Quality Verification, and Supplier Communication

Section 2.12 examined defects that may already exist in a fabricated bare PCB. This section connects those physical outcomes to the information that must exist before fabrication and the evidence that must exist after fabrication.

The practical question is:

> **What does the board fabricator actually need from the engineering organization to build and verify the intended board?**

A schematic or PCB layout by itself is not enough. The fabricator needs a controlled manufacturing definition that communicates:

- board geometry;
- layer construction;
- copper features;
- holes and routed features;
- materials and finishes;
- dimensional and performance requirements;
- electrical connectivity;
- verification requirements;
- revision status.

Engineering must also resolve supplier questions and manufacturability concerns without allowing undocumented changes to the product definition.

The goal is not to memorize file extensions. The useful engineering skill is to understand how **design intent becomes controlled manufacturing information and then verified hardware**.

---

## 2.13.1 - A PCB Release Is a Controlled Manufacturing Definition

The CAD database contains the designer's working model of the board. A fabricator, however, needs a **released manufacturing definition**.

That definition may contain several coordinated files and drawings, or it may use a more integrated digital manufacturing-data exchange. In either case, the package must describe one consistent physical board.

A useful manufacturing sequence is:

> **Design intent -> controlled manufacturing data -> supplier review -> fabrication -> verification**

The released information should answer four broad questions:

1. **What geometry must be manufactured?**
2. **What materials and construction must be used?**
3. **What requirements must the finished board satisfy?**
4. **How will conformance be verified and documented?**

If one of these questions cannot be answered from the release package, the supplier may need to stop and request clarification.

---

## 2.13.2 - What Belongs in a PCB Fabrication Release Package?

A rigid multilayer PCB release commonly contains several complementary information sets.

| Information set | Primary purpose |
|---|---|
| Fabrication drawing | Defines important finished-board dimensions, tolerances, materials, finishes, notes, and controlled fabrication requirements |
| Stack-up drawing or definition | Defines the intended layer sequence, copper layers, dielectric regions, and controlled construction requirements |
| Copper/layer artwork or equivalent digital data | Defines the copper pattern and other imaged manufacturing layers |
| Drill data | Defines hole locations and drilling information for plated and non-plated features |
| Rout/profile data | Defines the board outline, slots, cutouts, and other routed geometry |
| Netlist or electrical-test reference data | Defines intended electrical connectivity for open/short verification |
| Controlled notes and callouts | Define requirements not represented completely by geometry, such as material, surface finish, or controlled impedance when required |
| Revision/release information | Identifies the authorized product revision and keeps the manufacturing information synchronized |

Not every organization packages these items in exactly the same way. The important requirement is that the **complete controlled data set defines one board without contradiction**.

This section concerns the **bare-board fabrication release**. Assembly data such as component placement, assembly bill of materials, and solder-paste/stencil information belong to the later PCBA manufacturing flow rather than the bare-board fabrication package.

## 2.13.3 - Fabrication and Stack-Up Drawings Define the Finished Construction

The **fabrication drawing** is a controlled engineering document describing important characteristics of the unpopulated PCB.

Depending on the product and organization, it may define or reference:

- board outline and finished dimensions;
- dimensional datums;
- finished board thickness;
- profile and dimensional tolerances;
- hole requirements;
- material requirements;
- surface finish;
- solder-mask requirements;
- marking requirements;
- special fabrication notes;
- applicable inspection or performance requirements;
- revision identification.

The drawing should communicate requirements that the supplier must not infer. A dimension without an applicable tolerance, an unspecified finish, or an ambiguous material requirement can force the supplier to ask what is intended.

The engineering principle is:

> **Do not make the fabricator guess a requirement that affects form, fit, function, manufacturability, reliability, or verification.**

The **stack-up definition** controls the layered construction introduced in Section 2.3. It may identify:

- layer order;
- signal and plane layers;
- core and prepreg regions;
- copper thickness or copper-weight requirements;
- dielectric thickness targets or constraints;
- finished thickness;
- material system or material requirements;
- controlled-impedance structures when applicable.

The fabricator may translate the engineering stack-up into available laminate and prepreg constructions, but controlled requirements still have to be preserved or formally resolved with engineering.

---

## 2.13.4 - Copper, Drill, and Rout Data Define Different Geometry

Several manufacturing-data sets describe different physical features.

### Copper and layer data

Copper/layer artwork defines features such as:

- traces;
- pads;
- planes;
- clearances;
- copper pours;
- other imaged conductive geometry.

Other image data may define solder-mask and legend layers where those are part of the released data set.

### Drill data

Drill data communicate hole locations and drilling information.

The manufacturing definition must distinguish, where applicable:

- plated holes;
- non-plated holes;
- through holes;
- blind or buried structures;
- slots or other machined features.

The supplier must be able to determine the intended hole location, size, and plating status from the complete release package. Where both fabrication tool size and finished-hole size matter, the controlled information must make that distinction clear rather than leaving it to assumption.

### Rout and profile data

Rout/profile data communicate geometry such as:

- outside board profile;
- internal cutouts;
- routed slots;
- other machined openings.

The profile data must agree with the dimensions and datums in the controlled drawing information.

---

## 2.13.5 - Electrical Intent and Controlled Callouts Complete the Definition

A bare PCB can look correct while containing an electrically significant open or short. The manufacturing package may therefore include a **netlist or other electrical-test reference data** representing the intended connectivity.

This allows electrical verification of two basic conditions:

- nets intended to be continuous are connected;
- nets intended to be isolated are not unintentionally shorted.

Electrical testing serves a different purpose from visual inspection. Electrical test checks the manufactured conductive network against the intended network.

Not every requirement is represented by copper geometry. Controlled notes, tables, or callouts may also define:

- laminate/material requirement;
- finished board thickness;
- copper thickness;
- surface finish;
- solder-mask requirement;
- marking requirements;
- dimensional tolerances;
- bow/twist requirement when applicable;
- inspection or test requirements;
- controlled impedance when applicable;
- special process or handling restrictions.

Section 2.9 emphasized that fabrication capability is supplier- and process-dependent. Section 2.13 adds a second principle:

> **A capable process still cannot produce the intended board reliably if the engineering requirements are missing, ambiguous, or inconsistent.**

### Controlled-impedance awareness

When controlled impedance is required, the manufacturing definition may identify:

- target impedance;
- tolerance;
- affected structures or layers;
- stack-up constraints;
- verification requirements.

Because impedance depends on both conductor geometry and dielectric construction, the supplier may need to coordinate stack-up and trace geometry with engineering. Detailed signal-integrity design belongs later in the book; the manufacturing lesson here is that the requirement must be **explicit and controlled**.

---

## 2.13.6 - Gerber-Based and Intelligent Data Exchange Are Different Packaging Approaches

**Gerber** is widely used for PCB manufacturing image data.

A Gerber-based release commonly uses a **coordinated set of layer-oriented files**, such as:

- top and bottom copper;
- inner copper layers;
- solder mask;
- legend;
- other imaged manufacturing layers.

Supporting information may be supplied through separate files or drawings for:

- drilling;
- profile;
- stack-up;
- dimensions and tolerances;
- materials and finishes;
- electrical test;
- fabrication notes.

Modern Gerber workflows can also include useful attributes. The engineering issue is therefore not whether Gerber is "good" or "bad." A Gerber-based package can be complete and reliable when its files are generated correctly, controlled together, and checked for consistency.

**Intelligent digital manufacturing-data exchange** takes a different approach.

IPC-2581 is one standardized method for communicating PCB product and manufacturing information as structured, machine-readable data. Depending on the implementation, structured information can represent relationships among:

- layer construction;
- geometry;
- materials;
- drilling;
- connectivity;
- fabrication attributes;
- test-related information.

The objective is not simply to replace several files with "one newer file." The objective is to reduce ambiguity by preserving more manufacturing intent in structured data.

Different suppliers support different workflows, so the selected exchange method must match the intended fabricator's actual capability.

## 2.13.7 - Revision Control Applies to the Whole Package

A manufacturing release is controlled only when its components belong to the same authorized revision.

Consider a package containing:

- Rev C fabrication drawing;
- Rev C copper data;
- Rev B drill data;
- an older stack-up drawing.

Each file may be valid by itself, but the combined package is not a trustworthy manufacturing definition.

Revision control should answer:

- What part number is being built?
- What revision is authorized?
- Which files belong to that revision?
- When was the package released?
- Has any information changed after release?
- Was the change communicated and authorized?
- Does the supplier acknowledge the same release?

A file-control mistake can become a physical manufacturing defect if outdated data reaches CAM or production.

---

## 2.13.8 - Supplier Capability and CAM Review Should Occur Before Fabrication

Section 2.9 introduced fabrication capability, tolerance, and manufacturing margin.

Before production begins, engineering and the fabricator should confirm that the released package is compatible with the intended process.

Typical questions include:

- Can the supplier build the specified stack-up?
- Are trace/space features within qualified capability?
- Are hole sizes and aspect ratios manufacturable?
- Can the required annular ring and registration be maintained?
- Can the specified surface finish be produced?
- Can board thickness and profile tolerances be held?
- Can controlled-impedance requirements be achieved when present?
- Are the required inspection and quality records available?
- Does the fabricator require additional manufacturing data?

The review may conclude that:

- the board is acceptable as released;
- clarification is required;
- a design change is recommended;
- a special process is required;
- a requirement cannot be met with the intended process.

A capability review identifies where engineering attention is needed. It does **not** authorize the supplier to make uncontrolled design changes.

Detailed layout-level DFM rules remain in Chapter 3.

---

## 2.13.9 - Supplier Questions, Deviations, and Design Changes Must Be Controlled

During CAM review or fabrication planning, the supplier may identify:

- ambiguous dimensions;
- conflicting notes;
- missing stack-up information;
- unsupported materials;
- unclear hole definitions;
- features near process limits;
- conflicts among artwork, drawing, and drill data;
- requirements that cannot be met as written.

The correct response is not for either side to guess.

A controlled communication loop is:

> **Supplier question -> engineering review -> documented resolution -> updated or approved manufacturing instruction**

A **clarification** explains the existing design intent without changing it.

An **authorized deviation** permits a defined departure from a released requirement under the organization's approved change-control process.

A **design change** changes the controlled product definition and requires the appropriate revision/change process.

These actions are not interchangeable.

A supplier should not silently alter a controlled feature because another construction is easier to fabricate. Engineering likewise should not approve a change informally and leave the released package inconsistent with what was actually built.

---

## 2.13.10 - Verification Must Match the Controlled Requirements

After fabrication, the finished board is checked against the applicable requirements.

Verification may include:

- visual inspection;
- dimensional inspection;
- electrical continuity/isolation test;
- finished thickness measurement;
- hole and plating checks;
- solder-mask and surface-finish inspection;
- controlled-impedance testing when required;
- marking verification;
- other product-specific inspection or testing.

Not every board requires every verification method.

The verification plan depends on:

- released product requirements;
- purchasing requirements;
- applicable standards;
- supplier control plan;
- product risk and quality requirements.

The key relationship is:

> **Requirement -> verification method -> objective evidence**

If a requirement is important enough to control, engineering should understand how conformance will be demonstrated.

---

## 2.13.11 - Test Coupons, Microsections, and Quality Records Provide Evidence

Some PCB characteristics cannot be evaluated adequately from the board surface alone.

A **test coupon** is a representative structure fabricated with the production panel so selected process or product characteristics can be evaluated.

Depending on the requirement, coupons can support verification of:

- plated-hole quality;
- layer registration;
- copper/plating condition;
- controlled impedance;
- other selected process characteristics.

A **microsection** or **cross-section** is prepared by cutting through a representative coupon or board feature, mounting and polishing the sample, and examining the internal structure.

A microsection can provide evidence about:

- plated-hole wall condition;
- plating continuity;
- internal-layer connection;
- layer alignment;
- dielectric construction;
- internal defects.

Microsectioning is destructive to the sampled feature, which is one reason representative coupons are useful.

The exact sampling plan, preparation method, measurement method, and acceptance criteria depend on the controlling specification or quality plan.

Depending on product and purchasing requirements, the supplier may also provide or retain records such as:

- certificate of conformance;
- electrical-test completion record;
- dimensional or inspection report;
- impedance test result;
- coupon or microsection result;
- material/process traceability;
- approved deviation record;
- final inspection status.

The engineering question is not whether every PCB needs every possible report. It is:

> **What objective evidence is required for this product, and where is that requirement defined?**

## 2.13.12 - Standards Awareness: Different Standards Families Serve Different Roles

PCB engineering frequently refers to standards, but no single document defines every aspect of design, materials, fabrication, inspection, and data exchange.

> **Standards Awareness:** A standards number alone is not a complete manufacturing requirement. Engineering must identify the applicable document and revision together with the product, customer, drawing, and purchasing requirements that govern the actual board.

At awareness level, the following standards families have different roles:

| Standards family | Why it becomes relevant |
|---|---|
| IPC-2221 / IPC-2222 | Generic and rigid-board design context |
| IPC-4101 | Laminate and prepreg/base-material requirements |
| IPC-6012 | Qualification and performance requirements for rigid printed boards |
| IPC-A-600 | Bare-board acceptability and inspection context |
| IPC-SM-840 | Qualification and performance requirements for permanent solder mask |
| IPC-455x family | Requirements associated with specific PCB surface finishes |
| IPC-TM-650 | Test methods for electronic materials and printed-board characteristics |
| IPC-2581 | Structured design/manufacturing-data description and transfer |

The engineering organization must determine:

- which standards apply;
- which revision is contractually required;
- what product/performance requirement applies;
- which drawing, specification, or purchasing document invokes the requirement.

This section introduces the **role** of the standards families. It does not reproduce proprietary acceptance tables, copyrighted inspection illustrations, or detailed numerical criteria.

## 2.13.13 - Applied Example: A Supplier Finds Conflicting Release Information

Assume a PCB supplier receives a release package containing:

- a fabrication drawing specifying a finished board thickness of $1.60\ \mathrm{mm}$;
- a stack-up drawing whose listed construction implies a different overall thickness;
- a controlled-impedance requirement;
- no statement explaining whether the supplier may adjust dielectric thickness or trace geometry.

The supplier should not choose an interpretation silently.

A sound engineering response is:

1. confirm the intended controlled requirements;
2. determine whether stack-up, finished thickness, and impedance can all be satisfied;
3. review the supplier's proposed construction;
4. approve the construction or revise the design as required;
5. update the controlled package if the product definition changes;
6. confirm that engineering and the supplier are using the same release.

The broader lesson is:

> **When manufacturing data conflict, the correct output is a controlled engineering resolution—not an undocumented shop-floor assumption.**

## 2.13.14 - Practical Pre-Release Review

Before sending a PCB to fabrication, engineering should be able to answer the following questions.

### Product definition

- Is the board part number and revision clear?
- Are board outline, dimensions, holes, slots, and cutouts defined?
- Does the stack-up agree with the fabrication drawing?
- Are copper/layer, drill, and profile data synchronized?

### Materials and finishes

- Is the laminate/material requirement clear?
- Is copper construction adequately defined?
- Is the surface finish specified?
- Are solder-mask and marking requirements clear?

### Electrical and performance intent

- Is the netlist/test reference available when required?
- Are controlled-impedance requirements identified when applicable?
- Are other special performance requirements explicitly controlled?

### Manufacturability and communication

- Has the intended fabricator's capability been reviewed?
- Are features near minimum capability understood?
- Have supplier questions been resolved?
- Are authorized deviations linked to the correct release?

### Verification and records

- Are required inspection/test methods identified?
- Are coupons or microsections required?
- Are required quality records identified?

A package that passes this review is not guaranteed to be defect-free, but it gives the fabricator a much stronger basis for building and verifying the intended product.

## Key Terms Introduced in This Section

**Fabrication drawing**  
A controlled drawing defining important finished-board dimensions, notes, materials, finishes, tolerances, and fabrication requirements.

**Stack-up definition**  
The controlled description of PCB layer sequence and relevant copper/dielectric construction.

**Manufacturing data**  
Digital or documented information used to convert engineering intent into fabrication tooling, process instructions, inspection, and test.

**Gerber**  
A widely used format for representing PCB image/layer data in manufacturing exchange.

**Netlist**  
A representation of intended electrical connectivity used as a reference for electrical verification.

**CAM review**  
The supplier's computer-aided-manufacturing review of released data before or during tooling preparation.

**Test coupon**  
A representative test structure manufactured with the production panel for selected process or product verification.

**Microsection / cross-section**  
A prepared cut through a PCB or coupon used to inspect internal construction and plated features.

**Authorized deviation**  
A documented and approved departure from a released requirement under defined conditions.

**Certificate of conformance**  
A supplier quality record stating conformance with specified requirements according to the applicable purchasing and quality system.

**Intelligent manufacturing-data exchange**  
A structured digital method for communicating PCB product and manufacturing information with machine-readable relationships among data elements.

## Section Takeaway

A PCB cannot be manufactured correctly from design intent that remains only inside the designer's CAD environment.

Engineering must release a controlled manufacturing definition that communicates:

- geometry;
- stack-up and materials;
- holes and profile;
- electrical connectivity;
- finishes and controlled notes;
- revision status;
- verification requirements.

The fabricator converts that information into manufacturing instructions, identifies conflicts or capability concerns, fabricates the board, and produces objective evidence that the board meets the applicable requirements.

The central engineering principle is:

> **Define the intended board clearly, control the revision, resolve supplier questions explicitly, and connect important requirements to methods of verification.**
