# 2.4 - PCB Materials and Property-to-Performance Relationships

Section 2.3 showed that a PCB stack-up is an ordered combination of conductive and dielectric layers. The next question is what those layers are made from and why their material properties matter.

For the conventional rigid multilayer boards used as the primary examples in this chapter, the main material system is typically based on:

- copper conductors;
- glass-reinforced polymer laminate;
- resin-rich bonding material that becomes part of the dielectric structure after lamination.

A common rigid-board material family is **FR-4**. However, FR-4 should not be treated as one material with one fixed set of properties.

Different FR-4 laminate systems can have different:

- resin chemistries;
- glass styles;
- resin contents;
- glass-transition temperatures;
- thermal expansion behavior;
- dielectric properties;
- moisture absorption;
- decomposition resistance;
- mechanical properties.

For engineering work, the useful question is therefore not:

> **What is the property of FR-4?**

but:

> **What are the properties of the specific laminate system, under the conditions that matter for this product?**

This section uses a practical relationship throughout:

> **Property -> physical meaning -> packaging consequence**

---

## 2.4.1 - FR-4 Is a Material Family, Not One Fixed Material

FR-4 is widely used for rigid printed circuit boards because glass-reinforced epoxy laminate systems can provide a useful combination of:

- electrical insulation;
- mechanical strength;
- dimensional stability;
- manufacturability;
- availability;
- cost effectiveness.

The glass reinforcement and polymer resin work together as a composite material.

The glass reinforcement contributes strongly to:

- in-plane stiffness;
- dimensional control;
- reduced in-plane thermal expansion.

The resin:

- binds the reinforcement;
- electrically insulates copper layers;
- fills spaces around patterned copper during lamination;
- strongly influences thermal, dielectric, moisture, and high-temperature behavior.

The finished laminate therefore behaves differently in different directions.

This directional behavior is important because a PCB is not an isotropic block of material.

> **A rigid PCB laminate is an anisotropic composite: properties measured in the board plane can differ greatly from properties measured through the board thickness.**

### Terminology caution

FR-4 should be treated as an industry laminate grade/family rather than as one universal formulation.

Do not assume that the generic label **FR-4** by itself tells you:

- the exact \(T_g\);
- the exact CTE;
- the exact thermal conductivity;
- the exact dielectric constant;
- the exact loss tangent;
- the exact moisture absorption;
- the exact flammability performance of a specific qualified product.

Those values belong to the actual laminate system and its qualification/test data.

The simplified statement sometimes encountered in introductory material that "`FR` means a specific UL rating and `4` means epoxy-glass" is not precise enough for this book.

---

## 2.4.2 - Reading Material Properties as Engineering Information

PCB material properties should not be memorized as isolated numbers.

Each property answers a practical engineering question.

| Property | Physical meaning | Packaging consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Thermal conductivity \(k\)** | How readily heat conducts through a material | Affects heat spreading and through-thickness heat flow |
| **CTE \(\alpha\)** | How much a material expands or contracts with temperature | Drives dimensional change, mismatch stress, via-barrel strain, and warpage |
| **Glass-transition temperature \(T_g\)** | Temperature region where polymer behavior changes significantly | Modulus and CTE can change; high-temperature process behavior becomes more critical |
| **Decomposition temperature \(T_d\)** | Test-defined temperature associated with chemical degradation | Indicates resistance to severe thermal exposure; not an operating-temperature rating |
| **T260 / T288** | Time to delamination at a specified elevated test temperature | Gives awareness of laminate robustness during severe thermal exposure |
| **Elastic / flexural modulus** | Resistance of a material to deformation | Influences board stiffness, local strain, and mechanical response |
| **Moisture absorption** | Tendency of the material system to take up moisture | Can affect electrical behavior, dimensional stability, and thermal-process robustness |
| **Dielectric constant \(D_k\)** | Electrical permittivity relative to free space | Influences capacitance, propagation, and controlled impedance |
| **Dissipation factor \(D_f\)** | Dielectric-loss behavior | Influences signal attenuation at higher frequencies |
| **Copper thickness / weight** | Amount of copper available in a conductive layer | Affects resistance, current carrying, heat spreading, etching, plating, and local mechanical behavior |

## 2.4.3 - Representative Property Magnitudes

The cautions above do **not** mean that students should avoid numbers.

Representative values are useful because they establish **engineering scale**. They help answer questions such as:

- Is copper only slightly more thermally conductive than FR-4, or is the difference several orders of magnitude?
- Is through-thickness expansion similar to in-plane expansion?
- Is a reported laminate \(T_g\) near \(100^\circ\mathrm{C}\), \(200^\circ\mathrm{C}\), or \(500^\circ\mathrm{C}\)?
- Is a dielectric constant near 1, 4, or 40?

The values below are provided as **representative engineering values**, not as procurement or design specifications.

> **Use these values to develop order-of-magnitude intuition. For actual design, fabrication, or material qualification, use the datasheet and test data for the specific laminate system.**

| Property | Representative value or range | Engineering perspective |
|---|---:|---|
| **Copper thermal conductivity** | \(\sim 380\text{–}400\ \mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)}\) | Copper is a very effective PCB heat-spreading and heat-conduction material |
| **Conventional FR-4 thermal conductivity** | \(\sim 0.3\text{–}0.4\ \mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)}\) | Through-laminate heat conduction is much weaker than through copper |
| **Prepreg-derived dielectric thermal conductivity** | Material/construction dependent | Use cured-construction data; dielectric spacing can form an important through-thickness thermal resistance |
| **Copper elastic modulus** | \(\sim 117\ \mathrm{GPa}\) | Copper is mechanically much stiffer than the polymer-rich laminate material |
| **FR-4 flexural modulus (representative commercial example)** | \(\sim 21\text{–}23\ \mathrm{GPa}\) | Provides useful scale for laminate material stiffness; whole-board stiffness also depends strongly on geometry |
| **Copper CTE** | \(\sim 17\ \mathrm{ppm/^\circ C}\) | Useful reference for thermomechanical mismatch |
| **FR-4 X/Y CTE** | \(\sim 11\text{–}16\ \mathrm{ppm/^\circ C}\) | Representative pre-\(T_g\) commercial values; glass reinforcement restrains in-plane expansion |
| **FR-4 Z-axis CTE** | \(\sim 60\text{–}70\ \mathrm{ppm/^\circ C}\) | Representative conventional FR-4 pre-\(T_g\) range; through-thickness expansion can be several times larger than in-plane expansion |
| **Standard FR-4 \(T_g\)** | \(\sim 130\text{–}140^\circ\mathrm{C}\) | Representative conventional glass-transition range |
| **High-\(T_g\) FR-4** | \(\sim 170\text{–}190^\circ\mathrm{C}\) | Representative higher-\(T_g\) laminate range |
| **Example standard FR-4 \(D_k\) at 1 GHz** | \(\sim 4.3\) | Shows the approximate scale of dielectric permittivity; actual value depends on construction and test method |
| **Example standard FR-4 \(D_f\) at 1 GHz** | \(\sim 0.016\) | Shows the approximate scale of dielectric loss; frequency and test method matter |

These values are intentionally limited to properties for which a simple representative number helps build intuition.

Properties such as \(T_d\), T260/T288, and moisture absorption are **not** assigned one generic FR-4 value here because their interpretation depends strongly on the specific laminate formulation, construction, and test method.

## 2.4.4 - Thermal Conductivity \(k\)

**Thermal conductivity** describes how readily a material conducts heat.

Its common SI unit is:

\[
\mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)}
\]

Copper has much higher thermal conductivity than typical glass-reinforced epoxy laminate materials.

Using representative values of

\[
k_{\mathrm{Cu}} \approx 390\ \mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)}
\]

and

\[
k_{\mathrm{FR4}} \approx 0.36\ \mathrm{W/(m\cdot K)},
\]

the conductivity ratio is approximately

\[
\frac{k_{\mathrm{Cu}}}{k_{\mathrm{FR4}}}
\approx
\frac{390}{0.36}
\approx 1100.
\]

So, as an order-of-magnitude comparison, copper conducts heat roughly **three orders of magnitude more effectively** than a conventional FR-4 laminate. The exact ratio is material-dependent. This helps explain why copper planes, pours, and vias are so important to useful PCB heat-flow paths.

This means that in a conventional PCB:

- copper is usually the most effective material for spreading heat laterally;
- dielectric layers provide much greater thermal resistance than copper;
- through-thickness heat flow can be limited by the dielectric regions between copper layers;
- copper planes, pours, and thermal vias can strongly change the board-level heat path.

The important engineering lesson is:

> **The PCB's thermal behavior depends strongly on where copper is placed and how conductive regions are connected through the stack-up.**

This does not mean that adding copper or adding layers automatically solves a thermal problem. The entire heat-flow path must be useful.

Detailed PCB thermal behavior is developed in Section 2.11 and system-level thermal design in Chapter 6.

### Representative values versus design values

The values above are useful for first-order comparison, but actual laminate thermal conductivity varies with formulation, resin content, reinforcement, fillers, temperature, direction, and test method.

For design work, use the thermal-property data for the intended laminate construction.

## 2.4.5 - Coefficient of Thermal Expansion (CTE)

The **coefficient of thermal expansion**, or **CTE**, describes how much a material changes dimension as temperature changes.

For a simple first-order estimate over a temperature range where the CTE can be treated as approximately constant:

\[
\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta T
\]

where:

- \(\Delta L\) = change in length;
- \(\alpha\) = coefficient of thermal expansion;
- \(L_0\) = original length;
- \(\Delta T\) = temperature change.

The equation is simple, but PCB behavior is not.

### X/Y versus Z direction

A glass-reinforced laminate is strongly directional.

For PCB terminology:

- **X and Y directions** lie approximately in the plane of the board;
- **Z direction** is through the board thickness.

Glass reinforcement restrains in-plane expansion relatively effectively.

Through the thickness, the resin system has a stronger influence, so Z-direction expansion is typically much greater than in-plane expansion.

This matters because plated holes and vias contain copper barrels extending through the board thickness.

During heating:

- the laminate expands in Z;
- the copper barrel expands differently;
- the mismatch creates mechanical strain in the plated structure and its interfaces.

Repeated thermal exposure can therefore contribute to:

- via/barrel cracking;
- interfacial stress;
- delamination;
- other thermomechanical reliability problems.

The key relationship is:

> **CTE mismatch + temperature change -> differential expansion -> mechanical stress/strain**

### Example: developing a sense of CTE scale

Consider a temperature increase of

\[
\Delta T = 100^\circ\mathrm{C}.
\]

For a first-order comparison that remains below the laminate's \(T_g\), use representative values of approximately \(17\ \mathrm{ppm/^\circ C}\) for copper and \(65\ \mathrm{ppm/^\circ C}\) for conventional FR-4 in the Z direction. The corresponding **free thermal strains** are approximately

\[
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{Cu}}
=
17\times10^{-6}(100)
=
0.0017
=
0.17\%,
\]

and

\[
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{FR4,Z}}
=
65\times10^{-6}(100)
=
0.0065
=
0.65\%.
\]

The difference in free thermal strain is therefore approximately

\[
\Delta \varepsilon
\approx
0.65\%-0.17\%
=
0.48\%.
\]

A plated via constrains these materials so they cannot simply expand independently. The actual stress state is more complicated than this simple comparison, but the calculation shows why Z-axis CTE mismatch can create significant strain in the plated barrel during thermal excursions.

Detailed via reliability is addressed later in Sections 2.6 and 2.12 and in Chapter 8.

### CTE is not constant over all temperatures

Polymer behavior changes with temperature.

In particular, CTE—especially through-thickness CTE—can change substantially as the material passes through its glass-transition region.

This is one reason \(T_g\) and CTE should be interpreted together rather than independently.

## 2.4.6 - Glass-Transition Temperature \(T_g\)

The **glass-transition temperature**, \(T_g\), identifies a temperature region where the polymer matrix changes significantly in mechanical behavior.

It is not the melting point of the PCB laminate.

Around and above the glass-transition region, important properties can change, including:

- modulus;
- CTE;
- dimensional response.

For a PCB, these changes matter during:

- multilayer fabrication;
- soldering and reflow;
- repair/rework;
- high-temperature service;
- thermal cycling.

A higher \(T_g\) may be useful when a product or assembly process requires improved high-temperature dimensional and mechanical stability.

However:

> **Higher \(T_g\) does not automatically mean a better laminate for every application.**

Material selection must also consider:

- decomposition resistance;
- dielectric behavior;
- moisture behavior;
- manufacturability;
- cost;
- compatibility with the fabrication and assembly process.

### Important distinction

\(T_g\) is **not**:

- a maximum operating temperature;
- a decomposition temperature;
- a guarantee of reliability;
- a complete measure of lead-free assembly compatibility.

It is one material property that must be considered with the rest of the material system.

---

## 2.4.7 - Decomposition Temperature \(T_d\) and T260/T288

### Decomposition temperature \(T_d\)

The **decomposition temperature**, \(T_d\), is associated with chemical degradation of the resin system under a defined test method.

It should not be confused with \(T_g\).

A laminate can pass through its glass-transition region without chemically decomposing.

The relationship is conceptually:

> **\(T_g\): major change in polymer mechanical behavior**

> **\(T_d\): chemical degradation under a specified thermal test**

A higher decomposition temperature can indicate improved resistance to severe thermal exposure, but \(T_d\) is still not a simple maximum-use-temperature rating.

### T260 and T288 awareness

Laminate datasheets may also report **T260** or **T288**.

These are time-to-delamination measurements performed at fixed elevated test temperatures such as:

- \(260^\circ\mathrm{C}\);
- \(288^\circ\mathrm{C}\).

The reported result is a **time**, not a temperature rating.

These tests are useful for comparing how a material/construction withstands severe thermal exposure associated with fabrication or assembly conditions.

For this course, students should mainly recognize the terms.

Detailed thermomechanical test methods are outside the scope of Section 2.4.

---

## 2.4.8 - Elastic and Flexural Modulus

**Elastic modulus** describes a material's resistance to elastic deformation.

A higher modulus generally means that, for the same stress state and geometry, the material deforms less.

However, the stiffness of an entire PCB is not determined by laminate modulus alone.

Board-level bending stiffness also depends strongly on:

- total board thickness;
- board width and length;
- stack-up;
- copper placement;
- local cutouts;
- support conditions.

This distinction is important:

> **Material modulus is a material property. Board bending stiffness is a structural property.**

Section 2.10 develops the strong influence of board thickness on bending stiffness.

### Direction matters

Because glass-reinforced laminates are composite materials, mechanical properties can differ:

- in-plane;
- through-thickness;
- with glass style and resin content.

A single modulus value from a datasheet should therefore be interpreted with its test direction and test method.

---

## 2.4.9 - Moisture Absorption

Polymer-based PCB materials can absorb moisture from the environment.

Moisture matters because it can influence:

- dielectric behavior;
- dimensional stability;
- interfacial adhesion;
- thermal-process robustness.

If moisture is present when the board experiences rapid high-temperature processing, internal vapor pressure and material-interface weakness can contribute to problems such as:

- blistering;
- delamination;
- other laminate damage.

However, moisture absorption should not be used as a single-property predictor of whether a laminate will perform well.

Material behavior depends on the combined effects of:

- resin chemistry;
- reinforcement;
- laminate construction;
- cure state;
- copper treatment;
- thermal history;
- storage and handling conditions.

The practical engineering lesson is:

> **Moisture absorption matters, but it must be interpreted together with the rest of the material and process system.**

Moisture-related bare-board defects are revisited in Section 2.12.

---

## 2.4.10 - Dielectric Constant \(D_k\) and Dissipation Factor \(D_f\)

The PCB dielectric is not electrically invisible.

Two common electrical material properties are:

- **dielectric constant \(D_k\)**;
- **dissipation factor \(D_f\)**.

### Dielectric constant \(D_k\)

\(D_k\) describes the material's relative permittivity.

At an introductory level, \(D_k\) influences:

- capacitance;
- electric-field distribution;
- signal propagation;
- controlled impedance.

### Dissipation factor \(D_f\)

\(D_f\) describes dielectric loss.

As frequency increases and signal-loss requirements become more demanding, \(D_f\) can become increasingly important.

A material with lower dielectric loss may be desirable for high-speed or high-frequency applications.

### Datasheet values require context

\(D_k\) and \(D_f\) are not universal constants for an FR-4 family.

Reported values can depend on:

- frequency;
- resin content;
- glass construction;
- material formulation;
- temperature;
- moisture;
- measurement method.

This means two datasheet values should not always be compared as though they were measured under identical conditions.

For MET406, Section 2.4 introduces the material meaning only.

Detailed transmission-line, impedance, return-path, loss, and signal-integrity behavior belongs in Chapter 5.

## 2.4.11 - Copper Thickness and Copper Weight

PCB copper is commonly described by **thickness** or by **copper weight**.

The phrase **copper weight** refers to copper mass per unit area, traditionally expressed in ounces per square foot.

A commonly used nominal conversion is:

> **1 oz/ft² copper ≈ 35 μm thickness**

Therefore, approximate nominal foil equivalents are often described as:

- 0.5 oz/ft² ≈ 17-18 μm;
- 1 oz/ft² ≈ 35 μm;
- 2 oz/ft² ≈ 70 μm.

These are useful engineering approximations, not a guarantee of final finished copper thickness everywhere on a PCB.

Outer-layer fabrication can add copper through plating, and actual finished thickness can vary with:

- foil specification;
- plating process;
- location;
- manufacturing tolerance.

Copper thickness matters because it affects:

- electrical resistance;
- current carrying;
- heat spreading;
- etching behavior;
- minimum manufacturable geometry;
- plated-hole geometry;
- local stiffness and stress.

Section 2.5 examines copper features in more detail, and Section 2.9 returns to fabrication capability and tolerances.

## 2.4.12 - Resin Content, Glass Reinforcement, and Construction

Even when two laminates are both described as FR-4, their behavior can differ because the composite construction differs.

Important factors include:

- resin chemistry;
- resin content;
- glass cloth style;
- reinforcement orientation;
- fillers;
- cured thickness.

These variables can influence:

- CTE;
- modulus;
- dielectric properties;
- moisture absorption;
- dimensional stability;
- lamination behavior.

### Why glass style matters

The glass reinforcement does not fill every location in the laminate uniformly.

Different glass styles and resin contents can create local differences in the dielectric environment and mechanical behavior.

At the level of this course, students do not need to design glass-weave constructions.

The important idea is:

> **A PCB laminate datasheet describes a composite material system, not just a resin.**

This is another reason generic "FR-4 values" should be used only as representative examples.

## 2.4.13 - Selecting Material from the Product Requirement

Material selection should begin with product requirements rather than with one preferred property.

For example:

### General-purpose rigid electronics

A conventional FR-4 laminate system may be appropriate when:

- temperature exposure is moderate;
- electrical-loss requirements are not demanding;
- standard fabrication capability is desirable;
- cost is important.

### More severe assembly or thermal exposure

The engineer may need to examine:

- higher \(T_g\);
- higher decomposition resistance;
- better time-to-delamination performance;
- lower Z-axis expansion;
- improved dimensional stability.

### High-speed / high-frequency applications

The engineer may place more emphasis on:

- \(D_k\);
- \(D_f\);
- consistency of dielectric behavior;
- controlled laminate construction.

### Thermally demanding applications

The engineer may need:

- better heat-spreading structures;
- thermally enhanced laminate systems;
- metal-core / IMS construction;
- carefully designed copper planes and thermal vias.

The correct decision is rarely based on one number.

A useful material-selection question is:

> **Which combination of thermal, mechanical, electrical, manufacturing, reliability, and cost properties best fits the product and process?**

---

## 2.4.14 - Reading a PCB Laminate Datasheet

When reviewing a laminate datasheet, an engineering technology student should ask:

1. **What exact laminate product or material family is being specified?**
2. **What test method and direction apply to each property?**
3. **What \(T_g\) is reported, and by what method?**
4. **What thermal-decomposition or time-to-delamination data are provided?**
5. **What are the in-plane and Z-direction CTE characteristics?**
6. **What mechanical-property data are available?**
7. **What moisture-absorption information is reported?**
8. **At what frequency and by what method are \(D_k\) and \(D_f\) reported?**
9. **What copper foil/thickness construction is intended?**
10. **Are the stated properties compatible with the fabricator's approved material and process?**

The goal is not to memorize a datasheet.

The goal is to understand what the numbers physically mean and which ones matter for the application.

## Key Terms Introduced in This Section

**FR-4**
A widely used family/grade of flame-retardant glass-reinforced epoxy laminate systems for rigid printed boards. Specific properties depend on the actual laminate formulation and construction.

**Thermal conductivity \(k\)**
A material property describing how readily heat conducts through a material.

**Coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE)**
A material property describing dimensional change with temperature.

**X/Y CTE**
Thermal-expansion behavior approximately in the plane of the PCB.

**Z-axis CTE**
Thermal-expansion behavior through the PCB thickness.

**Glass-transition temperature \(T_g\)**
The temperature region where the polymer matrix undergoes a significant change in mechanical behavior.

**Decomposition temperature \(T_d\)**
A test-defined temperature associated with chemical degradation of the material system.

**T260 / T288**
Time-to-delamination measurements performed at specified elevated temperatures.

**Elastic modulus**
A material property describing resistance to elastic deformation.

**Moisture absorption**
The tendency of a material system to take up moisture from its environment.

**Dielectric constant \(D_k\)**
Relative permittivity of a dielectric material.

**Dissipation factor \(D_f\)**
A measure of dielectric energy loss.

**Copper weight**
Copper mass per unit area, commonly expressed in ounces per square foot and used as a convenient way to specify approximate copper thickness.

## Section Takeaway

PCB material selection is not a matter of choosing "FR-4" and assuming one fixed property set.

A PCB laminate is a composite material system whose behavior depends on:

- resin;
- glass reinforcement;
- resin content;
- fillers;
- copper construction;
- temperature;
- direction;
- frequency;
- test method.

The most important property-to-performance relationships are:

- **thermal conductivity -> heat-flow capability;**
- **CTE -> dimensional change and thermomechanical stress;**
- **\(T_g\) -> change in polymer mechanical behavior;**
- **\(T_d\), T260, and T288 -> high-temperature robustness awareness;**
- **modulus -> material resistance to deformation;**
- **moisture absorption -> electrical, dimensional, and thermal-process concerns;**
- **\(D_k\) and \(D_f\) -> electrical propagation and loss behavior;**
- **copper thickness -> electrical, thermal, manufacturing, and mechanical consequences.**

For real engineering work, use the data for the **specific qualified laminate and copper construction**, not a generic FR-4 number taken out of context.

Section 2.5 now turns from material properties to the **copper features** created from those conductive layers: traces, pads, planes, pours, thermal reliefs, annular rings, and copper-balancing features.

- a direct standalone reliability limit.

#### Correction 7 - Moisture absorption
