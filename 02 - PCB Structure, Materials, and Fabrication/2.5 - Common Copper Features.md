# 2.5 - Common Copper Features

Section 2.4 examined the materials that make up a PCB and showed why copper thickness, laminate properties, and dielectric behavior matter. This section now turns from **what the conductive layers are made from** to **how copper is shaped and distributed within those layers**.

Copper on a PCB is not used in only one form. Depending on its geometry and location, patterned copper can serve as:

- a narrow path carrying a signal or power;
- a localized connection area for a component or plated hole;
- a broad region distributing power or providing an electrical reference;
- a heat-spreading path;
- part of the mechanical and manufacturing balance of the board.

The central idea is:

> **Copper geometry is multifunctional. The same copper pattern can affect electrical, thermal, mechanical, and manufacturing behavior at the same time.**

For that reason, common copper features should be understood as physical structures rather than only as lines and shapes in PCB layout software.

---

## 2.5.1 - From Copper Foil to Functional Copper Geometry

A PCB begins with copper associated with laminate or foil materials, but fabrication removes and adds copper selectively until the required conductive pattern remains.

The resulting copper features may include:

- traces;
- pads or lands;
- large plane regions;
- copper pours;
- annular rings around plated holes;
- thermal-relief connections;
- nonfunctional copper added for manufacturing or balance purposes.

These features differ in shape, but all are part of the same conductive material system.

A useful way to compare them is shown below.

| Copper feature | Primary purpose | Important physical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Trace** | Connect two or more electrical points | Width, thickness, and length affect resistance, current carrying, heating, and routing density |
| **Pad / land** | Provide a localized connection or attachment area | Geometry affects component attachment, soldering, inspection, and connection robustness |
| **Plane** | Provide a large, low-resistance conductive region | Supports power/reference functions and can spread heat laterally |
| **Copper pour** | Fill a defined region with copper | Can provide electrical connection, shielding/reference area, heat spreading, or copper-density control depending on design intent |
| **Annular ring** | Provide copper around a drilled/plated hole | Maintains a conductive interface between the plated barrel and the copper layer and provides tolerance to registration variation |
| **Thermal relief** | Connect a pad/hole to a large copper region through narrow spokes | Limits heat sinking during soldering while maintaining electrical connection |
| **Balancing / dummy copper** | Reduce severe copper-density or stiffness imbalance | Can improve process uniformity and reduce warpage tendency |
| **Copper thieving** | Add nonfunctional copper for manufacturing-process control | Commonly used to improve plating-area uniformity or other process consistency |

The exact function depends on the design. A large copper region, for example, may simultaneously carry current, provide an electrical reference, and spread heat.

## 2.5.2 - Traces

A **trace** is a patterned copper conductor that forms an electrical path between points on the PCB.

Traces can carry:

- digital or analog signals;
- power;
- ground or return current;
- control signals;
- other electrical connections required by the circuit.

At the simplest DC level, trace resistance can be related to conductor geometry by:

$$
R = \rho \frac{L}{A}
$$

For a rectangular trace cross-section:

$$
A = wt
$$

so:

$$
R = \rho \frac{L}{wt}
$$

where:

- $R$ is conductor resistance;
- $\rho$ is copper resistivity;
- $L$ is trace length;
- $w$ is trace width;
- $t$ is copper thickness.

</div>

This relationship gives useful first-order intuition:

- a **longer** trace has more resistance;
- a **wider** trace has less resistance;
- a **thicker** trace has less resistance.

However, resistance is only one consideration.

Trace geometry also influences:

- current carrying and temperature rise;
- local heat spreading;
- routing density;
- manufacturability;
- local copper density;
- later high-speed electrical behavior.

### Current carrying is a thermal problem as well as an electrical problem

A wider or thicker trace provides more copper cross-sectional area and usually lowers electrical resistance for a given length.

That can reduce resistive heating, but the allowable current cannot be determined from trace width alone.

Temperature rise also depends on factors such as:

- copper thickness;
- trace location on an outer or inner layer;
- nearby copper planes;
- dielectric construction;
- board thickness;
- airflow and ambient conditions;
- allowable temperature rise.

Therefore, this section does **not** assign one universal current rating to a given trace width.

Detailed electrical design and signal-integrity behavior are developed in Chapter 5. Detailed fabrication limits and DFM rules are developed later in this chapter and in Chapter 3.

## 2.5.3 - Pads and Lands

A **pad** or **land** is a localized conductive area intended to form an electrical or mechanical interface.

In practical PCB work, the terms *pad* and *land* are often used nearly interchangeably. In this book, **pad/land** refers to the local copper feature used for a connection such as:

- a surface-mount component termination;
- a through-hole component connection;
- a via connection;
- a test point;
- another designed conductive interface.

### Surface-mount pads

For a surface-mount component, the copper pad provides the surface on which the component termination is soldered.

Its geometry influences:

- the available solderable area;
- component alignment during assembly;
- solder-joint geometry;
- inspection access;
- local heat flow during soldering.

Detailed footprint and assembly-DFM rules are intentionally deferred to Chapter 3 and Chapter 4.

### Pads associated with plated holes

A plated hole commonly intersects a circular or otherwise shaped copper land on a layer.

The portion of copper remaining around the hole forms the **annular ring**, discussed next.

This creates an important distinction:

> **The pad/land is the complete local copper feature. The annular ring is the portion of that copper surrounding the hole.**

---

## 2.5.4 - Planes and Copper Pours

Large copper regions are commonly used because a PCB often needs more than narrow point-to-point traces.

### Copper plane

A **plane** is a relatively large conductive region intended to provide a broad electrical connection, commonly for:

- ground or reference;
- power distribution;
- other low-impedance conductive functions.

A plane may occupy much of a dedicated layer or only a large region of a layer.

Planes can also participate in:

- lateral heat spreading;
- current distribution;
- electromagnetic field/reference behavior;
- local and global copper balance.

The detailed electrical behavior of reference planes, return paths, power distribution, and transmission lines belongs in Chapter 5.

### Copper pour

A **copper pour** is a filled copper region created within a defined area of a PCB layer, often using a polygon or similar CAD feature.

A pour may be connected to an electrical net, such as ground, or may be used for another intentional purpose.

Depending on the design, a copper pour can contribute to:

- current distribution;
- electrical reference area;
- heat spreading;
- shielding or field control;
- copper-density balance.

### Plane and pour are related but not identical terms

In everyday PCB work, the terms can overlap.

A large copper pour connected to ground may function as a ground plane. A dedicated internal plane layer may be created using a different CAD representation.

The important engineering point is the **physical copper region and its intended function**, not the software label used to create it.

### Large copper regions are effective heat spreaders

Copper has much higher thermal conductivity than typical glass-reinforced polymer PCB dielectric materials.

As a result, broad and continuous copper regions can spread heat laterally much more effectively than sparse narrow traces.

However:

> **A copper plane does not automatically solve a thermal problem.**

Its usefulness depends on whether heat can actually enter the copper region and then continue along a useful thermal path to another part of the board, a thermal interface, a heat sink, a chassis, or the surrounding environment.

Section 2.11 develops PCB thermal behavior in more detail.

## 2.5.5 - Annular Rings

An **annular ring** is the copper surrounding a drilled or plated hole on a conductive layer.

Conceptually, if a circular pad has diameter $D_p$ and a centered finished hole has diameter $D_h$, the nominal radial annular-ring width is:

$$
a = \frac{D_p-D_h}{2}
$$

where $a$ is the nominal annular-ring width.

This simple geometric relationship is useful for visualization, but an actual manufactured annular ring may not be perfectly uniform around the hole because of:

- drill position variation;
- layer registration variation;
- plating and etching effects;
- other fabrication tolerances.

The annular ring matters because it helps provide a conductive interface between:

- the plated hole barrel;
- the copper feature on that layer.

It also provides geometric margin for normal manufacturing variation.

Detailed relationships among drill size, finished hole size, plating, registration, and annular-ring requirements are developed in Section 2.6 and Chapter 3.

## 2.5.6 - Thermal Reliefs

When a pad or plated hole connects directly to a large copper plane or pour, the surrounding copper can conduct heat away rapidly during soldering.

This can make the connection more difficult to heat to the required soldering temperature.

A **thermal relief** reduces this heat-sinking effect.

Instead of connecting the pad to the surrounding copper region with a continuous solid connection, a thermal relief commonly uses several narrow copper spokes separated by clearance gaps.

The spokes maintain electrical connection while limiting heat flow between the pad and the large copper region during soldering.

    width="900"

  >
  <p><em>Figure 2.5.4 - A solid connection provides a broad copper path from the pad into the surrounding plane, allowing heat to spread readily during soldering. A thermal-relief connection uses narrow copper spokes separated by clearance gaps to maintain electrical continuity while reducing heat flow into the plane.</em></p>
</div>

This creates a deliberate tradeoff:

- **solid connection:** lower electrical and thermal resistance to the plane;
- **thermal-relief connection:** easier localized heating during soldering but higher electrical and thermal resistance through the narrow spokes.

The correct choice depends on the function of the connection.

High-current, high-heat-flow, or special electrical connections may require a different approach from ordinary soldered connections.

> **A thermal relief is not the same as a thermal via.**

A thermal relief intentionally **limits lateral heat flow near a pad during soldering**. A thermal via is a plated vertical connection used to help conduct heat between layers or through the board. Thermal vias are introduced in Section 2.6 and developed further in Section 2.11 and Chapter 6.

## 2.5.7 - Copper Balance

Section 2.3 introduced **copper balance** as part of stack-up design.

Copper balance refers to controlling the **amount and distribution of copper** so the board does not contain unnecessary severe differences:

- between opposite sides of the stack-up;
- between nearby regions of the same layer;
- between very dense and very sparse areas where the manufacturing process is sensitive to copper distribution.

Copper balance matters for both **structural** and **manufacturing-process** reasons.

During lamination and later thermal exposure, copper-rich and dielectric-rich regions differ in stiffness and thermal-expansion behavior. Strong asymmetry can therefore contribute to residual-stress imbalance and warpage tendency.

During patterning and plating, large differences in local copper density can also make process conditions less uniform.

Large copper-density differences can contribute to:

- nonuniform etching or plating conditions;
- local stiffness differences;
- residual-stress imbalance;
- increased warpage tendency.

Copper balance does **not** mean that every layer or every side must have an identical copper pattern.

Functional routing, power distribution, component placement, and electrical requirements often make identical patterns impossible.

The engineering goal is to avoid large and unnecessary imbalance while preserving the required electrical design.

Detailed warpage mechanics are developed in Section 2.10.

## 2.5.8 - Dummy Copper and Copper Thieving

Additional nonfunctional copper may sometimes be introduced to improve manufacturing or structural uniformity.

Two related ideas are **dummy copper** and **copper thieving**.

### Dummy copper

**Dummy copper** is copper that is not required for the circuit's normal electrical function but is added intentionally to improve characteristics such as:

- copper-density balance;
- lamination uniformity;
- local stiffness balance;
- manufacturability.

Dummy copper should not be allowed to create unintended electrical, thermal, or electromagnetic effects. Its use must remain consistent with the actual product requirements.

### Copper thieving

**Copper thieving** generally refers to nonfunctional copper patterns added for manufacturing-process control, particularly to improve the distribution of platable area and reduce severe local differences during plating.

Thieving may be added:

- within unused areas where allowed;
- outside the finished board image on the manufacturing panel;
- by the design organization or by the fabricator under agreed manufacturing rules.

The terms *dummy copper*, *copper fill*, *copper balancing*, and *thieving* are sometimes used loosely in practice, but they should not be treated as exact synonyms.

A useful distinction is:

- **copper balance** describes the engineering goal of avoiding excessive copper-distribution imbalance;
- **dummy copper** describes intentionally added nonfunctional copper;
- **copper thieving** describes a manufacturing-oriented use of added copper, commonly for process uniformity such as plating-area control.

## 2.5.9 - Copper Thickness Is Part of Feature Geometry

Section 2.4 introduced copper weight and the approximate nominal relationship between copper weight and thickness.

In this section, the important point is that **thickness works together with feature shape**.

For a trace, for example, the available copper cross-sectional area is approximately:

$$
A = wt
$$

Therefore, two traces with the same width but different copper thicknesses do not have the same conductor cross-section.

Increasing copper thickness can:

- reduce DC resistance for the same trace width and length;
- improve current-carrying capability when thermal conditions permit;
- increase local heat-spreading capacity;
- make fine-feature etching more difficult;
- change finished geometry after fabrication;
- increase local copper stiffness and stress contribution.

This is why copper thickness cannot be treated independently from:

- trace width;
- pad geometry;
- plane area;
- plating;
- fabrication capability;
- the thermal and mechanical structure of the board.

Detailed outer-layer plating and fabrication sequence are developed in Section 2.8. Fabrication capability and tolerance are developed in Section 2.9.

## 2.5.10 - One Copper Pattern Can Serve Several Engineering Functions

A useful way to read a PCB is to ask what each copper feature is doing **simultaneously**.

Consider a broad ground-connected copper region.

It may:

- provide an electrical reference;
- carry return current;
- reduce resistance between connection points;
- spread heat;
- influence local stiffness;
- change copper density during fabrication;
- affect later high-frequency field behavior.

Similarly, a thermal-relief pad may:

- provide an electrical connection;
- reduce soldering heat loss;
- limit direct thermal conduction into a plane;
- create a local current-carrying bottleneck if the spokes are too restrictive for the application.

This leads to a central PCB-packaging lesson:

> **Copper features should not be evaluated from only one engineering viewpoint.**

Electrical, thermal, mechanical, manufacturing, and assembly consequences can be coupled through the same physical geometry.

## Key Terms Introduced in This Section

**Trace**  
A patterned copper conductor used to form an electrical path on a PCB.

**Pad / land**  
A localized copper feature intended to provide a conductive connection or attachment interface.

**Plane**  
A relatively large conductive region used for a broad electrical connection such as a reference or power-distribution function.

**Copper pour**  
A filled copper region created within a defined area of a PCB layer, often using a polygon or similar CAD feature.

**Annular ring**  
The copper surrounding a drilled or plated hole on a conductive layer.

**Thermal relief**  
A spoke-type copper connection between a pad or plated hole and a large copper region that maintains electrical continuity while limiting heat sinking during soldering.

**Copper balance**  
Control of copper amount and distribution to avoid unnecessary severe imbalance within the PCB construction.

**Dummy copper**  
Nonfunctional copper intentionally added for manufacturing, density, stiffness, or process-balance purposes rather than for the circuit's normal electrical function.

**Copper thieving**  
Nonfunctional copper patterns added primarily for manufacturing-process control, commonly to improve plating-area uniformity.

## Section Takeaway

PCB copper is not one uniform sheet after fabrication. It is patterned into features that perform different but often overlapping functions.

The most important relationships are:

- **trace geometry -> resistance, current carrying, heating, routing density, and manufacturability;**
- **pads / lands -> component, hole, test, and other local conductive interfaces;**
- **planes and pours -> broad current/reference paths, heat spreading, and copper distribution;**
- **annular rings -> conductive connection around plated holes and margin for manufacturing variation;**
- **thermal reliefs -> electrical connection with reduced soldering heat sinking;**
- **copper balance -> manufacturing uniformity, stiffness balance, and reduced warpage tendency;**
- **dummy copper / thieving -> intentionally added nonfunctional copper for balance or manufacturing-process control;**
- **copper thickness -> conductor cross-section and coupled electrical, thermal, manufacturing, and mechanical behavior.**

The key idea is that **the same copper geometry can affect several engineering functions at once**.

Section 2.6 now examines the features that connect copper through the board thickness: **vias, plated component holes, and mechanical holes**.

### Targeted technical corrections and qualifications made during QA

#### Correction 1 - Plane versus copper pour

The section does not treat `plane` and `copper pour` as universally identical CAD objects.

It explains that:

- a plane is a large conductive region defined primarily by function;
- a pour is a filled region created within a layer;
- a pour may function as a plane depending on the design.

#### Correction 2 - Thermal relief purpose

A thermal relief is defined as a connection that **limits heat sinking during soldering while maintaining electrical continuity**.

The section also explicitly distinguishes a thermal relief from a thermal via.

#### Correction 3 - Copper balance versus dummy copper versus thieving

The section does not use `copper balance`, `dummy copper`, and `copper thieving` as exact synonyms.

The distinction used is:

- copper balance = engineering goal of reducing excessive copper-distribution imbalance;
- dummy copper = intentionally added nonfunctional copper;
- thieving = manufacturing-oriented nonfunctional copper, commonly used to improve plating-area/process uniformity.

#### Correction 4 - Copper thickness repetition

Section 2.4 already introduced copper weight and the approximate nominal weight-to-thickness relationship.

Section 2.5 therefore uses copper thickness only to explain feature cross-sectional area and coupled performance rather than repeating the full conversion treatment.

#### Correction 5 - Current carrying

The section does not assign a universal current rating to a trace width.

It explains that trace temperature rise depends on conductor geometry together with board construction, adjacent copper, environment, and allowable temperature rise.

#### Correction 6 - Annular ring

The relationship

$$
a = \frac{D_p-D_h}{2}
$$

is presented only as nominal centered geometry.

The section explicitly notes that actual manufactured annular-ring width can vary because of drilling, registration, plating, etching, and related fabrication tolerances. Detailed acceptance criteria remain deferred.

#### Correction 7 - Copper balance and warpage

The public wording states that copper imbalance **can contribute to** residual-stress imbalance and warpage tendency rather than implying that copper balance alone controls flatness.

Detailed mechanics remain in Section 2.10.

#### Correction 8 - Copper balance manufacturing mechanism

Final QA separated two previously blended ideas:

- copper/dielectric asymmetry affects stiffness, thermal expansion, residual stress, and warpage during lamination and later thermal exposure;
- local copper-density differences can affect process uniformity during patterning and plating.

This avoids implying that etching and electroplating effects arise simply because copper and dielectric have different material properties.
