# 2.2 - Physical Anatomy of a PCB

Section 2.1 established where the bare PCB fits in the overall manufacturing pathway. The next step is to look inside the board itself.

A finished rigid PCB may look like a thin, solid sheet, but a multilayer board is actually built from **conductive and insulating layers bonded into one structure**. Its electrical paths, mechanical stiffness, heat-flow paths, manufacturing limits, and many potential defects all depend on this internal construction.

For this reason, the physical anatomy of a PCB should be understood from two complementary views:

- a **cross-sectional view**, which shows the layers through the board thickness;
- a **surface view**, which shows traces, pads, holes, solder mask, markings, and mechanical features.

The goal of this section is recognition and physical understanding. Detailed stack-up design, material properties, via structures, surface finishes, and fabrication processes are developed in later sections.

---

## 2.2.1 - A PCB Is a Layered Structure

A conventional rigid multilayer PCB contains alternating conductive and insulating regions.

The conductive regions are primarily copper. Depending on their geometry and purpose, the patterned copper may form:

- traces;
- pads or lands;
- larger copper areas;
- power or reference planes;
- plated connections through holes.

The insulating regions separate conductive layers and help form the mechanical body of the board. In common rigid multilayer construction, these insulating regions are made from reinforced polymer laminate systems such as glass-reinforced epoxy materials.

A simplified multilayer cross-section can therefore be thought of as:

> **copper + dielectric material + copper + dielectric material + copper**

with additional outer-surface features such as solder mask, surface finish, and legend markings.

The actual construction is more detailed because different dielectric layers may serve different manufacturing roles. Two important terms are **core** and **prepreg**.

## 2.2.2 - Core and Prepreg

### Core

A **core** is a cured insulating laminate that provides a stable dielectric layer within the PCB construction. In common rigid-board manufacturing, a core is often supplied with copper foil bonded to one or both sides.

After the copper is patterned, the core can provide:

- electrical insulation between copper layers;
- part of the board's mechanical structure;
- dimensional support during fabrication;
- part of the final dielectric spacing between conductive layers.

The word **core** describes a construction element within the PCB stack. It should not be interpreted as meaning that one core alone determines all board stiffness or all board thickness.

### Prepreg

**Prepreg** is a resin-impregnated reinforcement material used between other board layers during multilayer lamination. Before final lamination, its resin is not yet in the same fully cured state as the finished core material.

During lamination, prepreg helps:

- bond adjacent layers together;
- fill spaces around patterned copper;
- provide electrical insulation between conductive layers;
- contribute to the final thickness and mechanical structure.

After lamination and cure, the prepreg becomes part of the solid dielectric structure of the finished board.

A useful introductory distinction is:

| Construction element | Condition before final multilayer lamination | Main role in the finished PCB |
|---|---|---|
| **Core** | Cured laminate, commonly copper-clad before patterning | Stable dielectric and structural layer |
| **Prepreg** | Resin-impregnated bonding material that completes cure during lamination | Bonds layers and becomes dielectric between them |

The exact core/prepreg arrangement varies with the intended stack-up and fabrication process. Section 2.3 examines stack-ups in more detail.

## 2.2.3 - Copper Layers and Patterned Copper

Copper provides the main conductive structure of a conventional PCB.

Before fabrication is complete, copper foil is selectively patterned so that only the required conductive geometry remains. Depending on the layer, this geometry may include narrow conductors, large continuous areas, component-attachment features, or interlayer connection features.

At the anatomy level, four copper structures are especially important to recognize.

### Traces

A **trace** is a patterned copper conductor used to provide an electrical path between locations on the board.

Traces may carry:

- signals;
- power;
- electrical reference or return currents.

Their width, thickness, length, spacing, and relationship to other layers eventually affect electrical resistance, heat generation, manufacturability, and high-frequency behavior. Those topics are developed later.

### Pads or Lands

A **pad** or **land** is a conductive area intended to support an electrical connection at the board surface or around a hole.

Pads may be used for:

- surface-mount component terminations;
- through-hole component connections;
- connector contacts;
- test or interconnection features.

A bare PCB contains the pads, but it does not yet contain the soldered component joints that will be created during PCB assembly.

### Planes and Larger Copper Areas

Some layers or regions contain large areas of copper rather than narrow individual traces.

These areas may be used for:

- power distribution;
- reference/return paths;
- electrical shielding functions;
- heat spreading;
- copper balance or other manufacturing purposes.

A large copper area may perform several functions at once. Section 2.5 examines common copper features in more detail.

### Annular Copper Around a Hole

A plated hole normally intersects a copper pad or ring on one or more layers. The copper surrounding the hole is commonly referred to as an **annular ring**.

At this point, it is enough to recognize the geometry:

> **hole + plated wall + surrounding copper feature**

Detailed drill tolerances, annular-ring requirements, aspect ratio, and via construction are deferred to Section 2.6.

## 2.2.4 - Plated Through-Holes and Vias

A multilayer PCB needs ways to create electrical connections through the board thickness.

One common structure is the **plated through-hole (PTH)**. A PTH extends through the board, and its hole wall contains conductive plating that can electrically connect copper features on different layers.

A plated through-hole may serve different purposes.

### Plated component hole

A plated hole may receive the lead or pin of a through-hole component or connector.

In that case, the hole participates in:

- mechanical insertion of the lead or pin;
- electrical connection to selected copper layers;
- later solder-joint formation during assembly.

### Through via

A **via** is primarily an interlayer electrical connection rather than a hole intended to receive a component lead.

A simple through via may extend through the entire board and connect selected copper layers through its plated barrel.

Other via structures—including blind vias, buried vias, microvias, via-in-pad structures, and thermal vias—are introduced in Section 2.6.

> **Important distinction:** A plated through-hole describes a plated hole structure. A via describes an interlayer connection function. Some plated through-holes are component holes, while others function as vias.

Holes and vias also interrupt the otherwise continuous laminate structure, so they can influence local mechanical and thermal behavior. Detailed consequences are deferred to later sections.

## 2.2.5 - Solder Mask, Surface Finish, and Legend

The outer surfaces of a fabricated PCB include features that prepare the board for handling, identification, and later component assembly.

### Solder Mask

**Solder mask** is a permanent polymer coating applied over selected areas of the PCB surface.

It is commonly used to:

- electrically insulate covered surface conductors;
- protect covered copper surfaces from handling and surface damage;
- help control where solder is allowed to contact the board during assembly;
- reduce the risk of unintended solder bridging between nearby exposed features.

Openings in the solder mask expose the pads or other regions that must remain accessible.

Solder mask provides useful surface protection, but it should **not** be treated as an airtight, waterproof, or hermetic environmental seal. Products requiring stronger moisture, chemical, or environmental protection may require additional strategies such as conformal coating, encapsulation, sealing, or enclosure-level protection.

This distinction is important because the earlier lecture material described the protective role of solder mask more strongly than is appropriate for a general textbook statement. In this OER, solder mask is treated as a permanent board-surface coating with electrical, assembly, and limited protective functions.

### Surface Finish

Copper that must remain exposed for soldering or electrical contact is normally provided with a **surface finish**.

The finish helps:

- protect exposed copper before assembly;
- preserve a solderable or contact-compatible surface;
- provide a suitable interface for later joining or connection.

Common finishes include HASL, ENIG, and OSP, but their construction, advantages, limitations, and selection tradeoffs belong in Section 2.7.

A useful visual distinction is:

- **solder mask covers selected copper areas**;
- **surface finish is present on selected exposed copper features**.

These are different layers serving different purposes.

### Legend / Silkscreen

The **legend**, often informally called the **silkscreen**, contains printed or otherwise applied markings used for identification and manufacturing support.

Typical markings may include:

- reference designators;
- polarity/orientation marks;
- connector labels;
- board identification;
- revision or assembly information where appropriate.

The legend is primarily an information layer. It is not a major structural layer and normally should not be treated as part of the electrical interconnection.

## 2.2.6 - Board Outline and Mechanical Features

The PCB is not defined only by its copper and dielectric layers. Its physical geometry must also allow it to be handled, assembled, mounted, and integrated into the product.

Important mechanical features may include:

### Board outline

The **board outline** defines the external shape of the fabricated PCB.

It affects:

- fit within the enclosure or chassis;
- board-to-board spacing;
- connector location;
- manufacturing panel arrangement;
- later mechanical support.

### Mounting holes

Mounting holes allow the board to be attached to:

- standoffs;
- brackets;
- chassis structures;
- other mechanical supports.

A mounting hole may be plated or non-plated depending on the intended electrical and mechanical function. It should not automatically be interpreted as a via.

### Slots and cutouts

Slots and cutouts may provide:

- clearance for mechanical features;
- connector access;
- component clearance;
- isolation between regions;
- product-specific mechanical geometry.

### Tooling and fabrication features

Boards may also include holes or other features used to support fabrication, handling, alignment, or assembly processes.

Some of these features may ultimately be removed when an individual PCB is separated from a manufacturing panel.

Detailed panelization, fiducials, and assembly-tooling design are intentionally deferred to Chapter 3.

## 2.2.7 - Reading a PCB from the Outside and the Inside

A useful way to inspect the physical anatomy of a PCB is to ask two sets of questions.

### Surface-view questions

Looking at the top or bottom surface:

- Where are the copper pads?
- Which copper features are covered by solder mask?
- Which conductive areas are intentionally exposed?
- Where are plated holes and non-plated holes?
- Where are mounting holes, slots, and cutouts?
- What information does the legend provide?

### Cross-section questions

Looking through the board thickness:

- How many conductive copper layers are present?
- Which dielectric regions are cores and which were formed from prepreg during lamination?
- Which holes are plated?
- Which copper layers are connected by those plated structures?
- Where are the outer solder-mask layers?
- Where does the surface finish appear?
- Is the board construction roughly symmetric or strongly asymmetric?

The ability to move mentally between the surface view and the cross-section is important for later work.

For example:

- a pad visible on the surface may connect to an internal copper plane through a via;
- a hole that looks simple from the surface may contain a plated barrel extending through several layers;
- a large internal plane that is invisible from the outside may strongly affect electrical return paths and heat spreading;
- a mechanical cutout can interrupt copper, change local stiffness, and constrain routing.

This is why a PCB cannot be fully understood from a surface photograph alone.

## 2.2.8 - One Physical Feature Can Serve Several Functions

The same physical PCB feature often contributes to several engineering functions.

| PCB feature | Electrical role | Mechanical / manufacturing role | Thermal role |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Copper trace** | Carries signal or power | Must be manufacturable within process capability | Can conduct/spread some heat |
| **Copper plane** | Power/reference distribution | Changes copper distribution within the stack | Can spread heat laterally |
| **Plated hole / via** | Connects selected layers | Requires drilling/plating quality and interrupts the laminate | Can provide a through-thickness heat path |
| **Core / cured dielectric** | Electrically separates copper layers | Provides part of the board's structural body | Usually conducts heat much less effectively than copper |
| **Prepreg-derived dielectric** | Electrically separates bonded layers | Bonds the multilayer construction | Participates in through-thickness heat flow |
| **Solder mask** | Insulates covered surface conductors | Helps control solder exposure during assembly | Usually not a primary heat-spreading structure |
| **Mounting hole** | May be electrically isolated or intentionally connected | Provides mechanical attachment or alignment | May influence local heat flow only if intentionally integrated into a thermal structure |

The table is not meant to define universal behavior for every PCB technology. It illustrates a central packaging idea:

> **The electrical, mechanical, thermal, and manufacturing functions of a PCB are created by the same physical structure.**

Later chapters separate these functions for analysis, but the actual product does not.

## Key Terms Introduced in This Section

**Core**  
A cured insulating laminate used as part of a PCB stack, commonly supplied copper-clad before patterning.

**Prepreg**  
Resin-impregnated reinforcement used during multilayer lamination to bond layers and form part of the final dielectric structure.

**Trace**  
A patterned copper conductor that provides an electrical path on a PCB.

**Pad / land**  
A conductive feature intended to form part of an electrical connection, such as a surface-mount attachment area or the copper surrounding a plated component hole.

**Plane**  
A relatively large copper region used for functions such as power/reference distribution, shielding, heat spreading, or other design/manufacturing purposes.

**Plated through-hole (PTH)**  
A hole extending through the PCB with conductive material deposited on its wall.

**Via**  
A conductive interlayer connection used primarily to connect copper features on different PCB layers rather than to receive a component lead.

**Solder mask**  
A permanent polymer coating applied to selected PCB surface areas for insulation, solder control, and limited surface protection.

**Surface finish**  
A protective and solderable/contact-compatible finish applied to selected exposed conductive areas of a PCB.

**Legend / silkscreen**  
Markings on the PCB surface used for identification, orientation, assembly, inspection, or documentation.

## Section Takeaway

A rigid multilayer PCB is a **layered engineering structure**, not a simple sheet.

Its main physical elements include:

- core and prepreg-derived dielectric regions;
- copper traces, pads, planes, and annular features;
- plated through-holes and vias;
- solder mask;
- surface finish;
- legend markings;
- board outline, mounting holes, slots, cutouts, and tooling features.

The most important distinctions are:

- **core and prepreg have different roles before lamination but both become part of the dielectric structure of the finished board;**
- **copper features provide the electrical interconnection and also influence heat flow, manufacturability, and mechanical behavior;**
- **a plated component hole and a via may share similar plated-hole construction but serve different primary functions;**
- **solder mask and surface finish are different surface features and should not be confused;**
- **solder mask provides useful insulation, solder control, and surface protection, but it is not a hermetic environmental seal;**
- **surface appearance alone does not reveal the full multilayer construction.**

Section 2.3 builds on this anatomy by examining how these layers are organized into **different PCB types and multilayer stack-ups**.

### Technical distinctions to preserve

- The public section primarily describes conventional rigid multilayer boards; it does not claim all PCB technologies have identical construction.
- A core is a cured laminate construction element; prepreg is used as a bonding/dielectric material during multilayer lamination.
- After lamination, cured prepreg becomes part of the final dielectric structure.
- Copper foil becomes patterned copper features through fabrication; not every copper layer consists only of traces.
- A plane is a large copper region, but its exact electrical/thermal function depends on the design.
- A plated through-hole describes a physical plated-hole structure.
- A via describes an interlayer-connection function and is not normally intended to receive a component lead.
- A plated component hole may connect selected conductive layers and later receive a component lead/pin.
- Do not imply that all mounting holes are non-plated or all are electrically isolated.
- Solder mask is a permanent board-surface coating used for insulation/solder control/protection; it is not a hermetic, waterproof, or airtight seal.
- Surface finish is applied to selected exposed conductive regions; it is not the same as solder mask.
- Legend/silkscreen is primarily informational and is not a structural or primary electrical layer.
- Board outline, slots, cutouts, and mounting features are part of the manufactured PCB definition.
- Surface appearance alone cannot reveal the full internal stack-up.

Targeted corrections made during QA:

1. **Solder-mask protection**
   - The lecture includes an "airtight seal" description.
   - The public OER does not use that statement.
   - Coombs describes solder mask in terms of process compatibility, coverage/conformity, via tenting, contamination concerns, and qualification under IPC-SM-840 rather than as a hermetic environmental barrier. fileciteturn36file5
   - Current Global Electronics Association/IPC documentation identifies IPC-SM-840 as a qualification/performance specification for permanent solder mask and flexible cover materials.
   - The OER therefore uses the qualified wording "insulation, solder control, and limited surface protection" and explicitly states that solder mask is not a hermetic or waterproof seal.

2. **Core versus stiffness**
   - The lecture says core thickness "largely defines" PCB rigidity.
   - This section avoids assigning board stiffness to the core alone.
   - The board is treated as a multilayer composite whose overall thickness, layer properties, copper distribution, geometry, and stack-up affect mechanical behavior.
   - Detailed stiffness treatment is intentionally deferred to Section 2.10.

3. **Layer count and thermal performance**
   - The lecture contains broad statements that increased layer count improves thermal performance.
   - This anatomy section does not make that generalization.
   - It states only that copper geometry/planes can influence heat spreading. The actual thermal effect depends on copper amount, continuity, placement, dielectric thickness, vias, and heat-flow path and is developed in Section 2.11.

4. **Via terminology**
   - The lecture identifies vias as plated holes connecting layers.
   - The OER adds the practical distinction between a plated component hole and a via so students do not treat all plated holes as equivalent.
   - Detailed via taxonomy remains deferred to Section 2.6.
