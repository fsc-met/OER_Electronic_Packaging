# 2.7 - Surface Finishes

Section 2.6 examined holes and vias that connect through the PCB thickness. The next step is to examine the **exposed copper surfaces** that must later be soldered, contacted, tested, or otherwise used during assembly and operation.

Most patterned copper is covered by solder mask, but component pads, test points, connector contacts, and other intentionally exposed copper features cannot simply be left as bare copper.

Copper readily reacts with the surrounding environment and forms surface oxides and other contamination films. These surface changes can reduce solder wettability and make later assembly less predictable.

A **PCB surface finish** is therefore applied to exposed copper to preserve a usable surface until the board reaches assembly or its intended contact function.

The central idea is:

> **The surface finish protects exposed copper and prepares that surface for what must happen next.**

That next function may be:

- soldering;
- electrical probing;
- connector contact;
- wire bonding in specialized applications;
- storage before assembly.

No single surface finish is best for every PCB.

---

## 2.7.1 - Surface Finish Is Not the Same as Solder Mask

**Solder mask** and **surface finish** are different materials with different functions.

Solder mask normally covers most of the patterned outer-layer copper.

It helps:

- electrically insulate neighboring conductors;
- limit unintended solder spreading during assembly;
- protect covered copper from handling and environmental exposure;
- reduce mechanical damage to traces.

However, solder mask intentionally leaves openings over features that must remain accessible, such as:

- component pads;
- test points;
- connector contacts;
- selected plated holes;
- other exposed conductive features.

The **surface finish** is applied to those exposed copper areas.

A useful physical distinction is:

> **Solder mask protects copper that should remain covered. Surface finish protects copper that must remain exposed and usable.**

## 2.7.2 - Why Exposed Copper Needs a Finish

Fresh copper can be highly solderable, but bare copper does not remain chemically unchanged during storage and handling.

Exposure to:

- oxygen;
- moisture;
- airborne contaminants;
- fingerprints and handling residues;
- elevated temperature;

can change the surface condition.

The purpose of a surface finish is therefore not simply cosmetic.

A suitable finish helps provide:

### Oxidation and contamination protection

The finish separates or protects the exposed copper from environmental exposure during fabrication, storage, shipping, and handling.

### Solderability preservation

During later soldering, molten solder must wet the intended surface and form a sound metallurgical connection.

A good incoming surface condition makes that process more repeatable.

**Solderability** refers to the ability of a surface to be wetted by solder and to participate in formation of the required metallurgical bond.

### A controlled surface for assembly

The surface finish also affects:

- pad flatness;
- stencil contact during solder-paste printing;
- component placement;
- through-hole fit in some cases;
- electrical test contact;
- special contact or bonding functions.

Surface finish is therefore a **bare-board fabrication decision with downstream assembly consequences**.

---

## 2.7.3 - The Main Selection Tradeoffs

A surface finish should be selected by function rather than by appearance alone.

Important engineering questions include:

- How well does the finish preserve solderability?
- How flat is the finished pad surface?
- How sensitive is it to storage, handling, or repeated heating?
- Will the PCB be assembled using fine-pitch surface-mount components?
- Must the surface also act as an electrical contact?
- Is wire bonding required?
- Can the finish be inspected easily?
- What process controls or special failure modes must be considered?
- What is the relative bare-board cost?
- Is the finish compatible with the selected board fabricator and assembly process?

These questions create tradeoffs.

For example:

- a finish may provide excellent planarity but cost more;
- a low-cost finish may be more sensitive to handling or storage;
- a robust solderable finish may be less suitable for very fine-pitch stencil printing;
- a finish suitable for ordinary soldering may not be appropriate for long-term connector contact or wire bonding.

> **Surface-finish selection is a functional engineering choice, not a ranking from “best” to “worst.”**

---

## 2.7.4 - HASL and Lead-Free HASL

**HASL** stands for **Hot Air Solder Leveling**.

In a typical HASL process, exposed copper features are coated with molten solder and excess solder is removed using hot-air knives.

The resulting exposed pads therefore already carry a solder-based surface coating.

Traditional HASL commonly used tin-lead solder.

**Lead-free HASL** uses lead-free solder alloys and generally requires a higher processing temperature than traditional tin-lead HASL.

### Main strengths

HASL is widely recognized for:

- good solderability when properly processed;
- robust protection of exposed copper;
- broad manufacturing familiarity;
- generally low-to-moderate cost.

### Main limitation: surface planarity

Molten solder has surface tension, so the resulting solder coating is not perfectly uniform or flat.

This can be acceptable for many assemblies, but the uneven surface can become increasingly important as pad size and component pitch decrease.

A less-planar pad surface can make it harder for a solder-paste stencil to sit closely against the PCB.

That can increase sensitivity to:

- paste-printing variation;
- misprints;
- bridging;
- inconsistent deposited paste volume.

Lead-free HASL also exposes the PCB to a relatively high-temperature finishing operation, which can be a material/process consideration.

> **HASL is often a practical and economical finish, but its surface topography can limit suitability for very fine-pitch assembly.**

---

## 2.7.5 - ENIG

**ENIG** stands for **Electroless Nickel / Immersion Gold**.

Its simplified structure is:

> **copper -> electroless nickel -> thin immersion-gold layer**

The nickel provides the principal metallic barrier layer.

The thin gold layer protects the nickel surface from oxidation before assembly.

During soldering, the thin gold layer is rapidly dissolved into the molten solder, and the solder connection develops primarily with the underlying nickel surface.

### Main strengths

ENIG is commonly selected because it provides:

- a very planar pad surface;
- good solderability when properly fabricated and stored;
- useful storage robustness;
- compatibility with fine-pitch surface-mount assembly;
- useful electrical contact performance for some applications.

### Main limitations

Compared with simpler finishes, ENIG generally:

- costs more;
- requires more process steps and tighter chemical control;
- can develop finish-specific defects if the process is poorly controlled.

One well-known ENIG fabrication failure mode is commonly called **black pad**, which involves abnormal corrosion or degradation of the nickel surface during finishing.

Detailed defect mechanisms belong in Section 2.12.

### ENIG is not the same as electroplated gold

The thin immersion-gold layer used in ENIG should not be confused with thicker or specially controlled **electrolytic nickel/gold** finishes used for functions such as durable connector contacts or certain wire-bonding applications.

---

## 2.7.6 - OSP

**OSP** stands for **Organic Solderability Preservative**.

Unlike HASL and ENIG, OSP does not create a substantial metallic coating over the exposed copper.

Instead, a very thin organic coating is applied to the copper surface to preserve solderability.

During soldering, the organic coating is removed or displaced by the assembly chemistry, allowing solder to react with the underlying copper.

### Main strengths

OSP is attractive because it is generally:

- very planar;
- low cost;
- compatible with fine-pitch surface-mount assembly;
- relatively simple from a bare-board finishing perspective.

### Main limitations

OSP is more sensitive than many metallic finishes to:

- handling;
- contamination;
- storage conditions;
- time;
- repeated thermal exposure.

Its appearance also makes visual verification more difficult because the coating is extremely thin and does not produce the obvious metallic appearance of finishes such as ENIG or HASL.

OSP is also not a good general-purpose finish for long-term exposed electrical-contact functionality.

> **OSP is primarily a solderability-preservation finish rather than a durable contact finish.**

---

## 2.7.7 - Comparing HASL, ENIG, and OSP

The three primary finishes can be compared qualitatively.

| Consideration | HASL / Lead-Free HASL | ENIG | OSP |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Basic finish concept** | Solder coating on exposed copper | Nickel barrier with thin immersion gold | Very thin organic coating on copper |
| **Copper oxidation protection** | Good when finish coverage is sound | Good when finish is properly processed | Good while coating remains intact |
| **Solderability** | Good | Good | Good when properly stored/handled |
| **Surface planarity** | Generally less planar | Very planar | Very planar |
| **Fine-pitch SMT compatibility** | Can be limited by surface unevenness | Well suited | Well suited |
| **Storage / handling sensitivity** | Generally robust | Generally robust | More sensitive |
| **Repeated thermal exposure** | Finish is already solder based; board sees hot finishing process | Generally robust within proper processing/storage | Organic coating can degrade with repeated heating |
| **Visual inspection of finish coverage** | Metallic finish is visible | Metallic finish is visible | More difficult because coating is extremely thin |
| **Electrical contact use** | Possible in some cases, but not ideal for every contact application | Useful for some contact functions | Generally poor for exposed contact use |
| **Relative bare-board cost** | Low to moderate | Higher | Low |
| **Representative concern** | Pad nonplanarity / high finishing temperature | Process control / black-pad risk | Handling, storage, thermal-cycle sensitivity |

This table is intentionally qualitative.

Actual performance depends on:

- the exact finish specification;
- process control;
- board design;
- storage environment;
- number and severity of assembly thermal cycles;
- soldering materials;
- product requirements.

Do not use a comparison table like this as a replacement for the actual fabrication specification.

## 2.7.8 - Why Surface Planarity Matters During Assembly

Surface planarity becomes especially important during **solder-paste stencil printing**.

A stencil is placed against the PCB so that solder paste can be deposited through openings onto selected pads.

For good printing, the stencil should contact the PCB closely around the pad openings.

If the pad surface is uneven, the stencil may not gasket closely against the board.

A local gap can allow solder paste to spread beneath the stencil.

This can contribute to:

- excess paste;
- insufficient paste at the intended location;
- bridging between nearby pads;
- greater print-to-print variability.

Highly planar finishes such as ENIG and OSP therefore have an assembly advantage for many fine-pitch surface-mount applications.

HASL can still be used successfully in many products, but its surface unevenness becomes more significant as feature size and spacing decrease.

## 2.7.9 - Storage, Handling, and Shelf Life

A surface finish must remain usable from the end of PCB fabrication until assembly.

That interval can include:

- final inspection;
- packaging;
- shipping;
- warehouse storage;
- staging at the assembly facility;
- multiple assembly operations.

The usable storage life of a finished PCB depends on more than the finish name.

Important factors include:

- temperature;
- humidity;
- atmospheric contamination;
- packaging method;
- handling cleanliness;
- previous thermal exposure;
- time since fabrication.

Different finishes respond differently.

### HASL

HASL generally provides robust protection and is comparatively tolerant of ordinary handling and storage.

### ENIG

ENIG generally provides good storage robustness when the finish is correctly processed and protected from contamination.

### OSP

OSP is more sensitive to handling, time, contamination, and repeated heating.

Improper handling or extended storage can reduce the protection of the underlying copper and narrow the assembly process window.

> **A stated shelf life is meaningful only together with the specified packaging, storage, and handling conditions.**

For this reason, this section avoids presenting one universal shelf-life number for each finish.

---

## 2.7.10 - Inspection and Quality Verification

Surface-finish quality cannot be judged only by whether the board “looks shiny.”

Important concerns include:

- complete coverage of intended exposed copper;
- contamination;
- excessive or insufficient finish deposition;
- local corrosion;
- damage from handling;
- compatibility with solderability requirements.

Metallic finishes such as HASL and ENIG provide visible surface contrast that can help identify obvious coverage problems.

OSP is much less obvious visually because the coating is extremely thin.

That makes process control and appropriate quality verification especially important.

Detailed bare-board inspection methods, quality records, and supplier communication are developed in Section 2.13.

---

## 2.7.11 - Contact and Wire-Bond Requirements

A PCB surface may occasionally need to perform more than a soldering function.

Examples include:

- edge-connector fingers;
- switch or keypad contacts;
- test contacts;
- chassis-contact surfaces;
- wire-bond pads in specialized electronic packaging.

These functions can require a different finish from the one selected for ordinary solder pads.

For example:

- OSP is generally unsuitable as a durable exposed contact surface;
- ENIG can support some contact functions;
- durable edge contacts often use a separately specified electrolytic nickel/gold finish;
- specialized wire-bonding applications commonly require a finish specifically qualified for the wire material and bonding process.

> **A finish that solders well is not automatically the correct finish for a connector or wire-bond surface.**

Selective finishing may therefore be used when different areas of the same PCB require different surface functions.

## 2.7.12 - Other Surface Finishes for Awareness

HASL, ENIG, and OSP are the primary finishes emphasized here, but they are not the only options.

Other finishes include:

### Immersion silver

Immersion silver provides a planar metallic surface with good solderability.

Its surface can tarnish or corrode in certain sulfur- or contaminant-containing environments, so storage and environmental control can matter.

### Immersion tin

Immersion tin provides a planar solderable surface.

Its behavior changes with storage because copper-tin intermetallic compounds can grow with time and temperature.

Tin-whisker concerns may also need to be considered depending on the exact finish structure and product requirements.

### Electrolytic nickel/gold

Electrolytic nickel/gold is often selected for specialized contact or bonding functions rather than as the default solder finish across an entire PCB.

It may be used selectively on:

- edge fingers;
- wear/contact surfaces;
- selected wire-bond pads.

These alternatives reinforce the same lesson:

> **Surface finish must be selected for the required downstream function.**

## 2.7.13 - Surface Finish Connects Fabrication to Assembly

Surface finish is one of the final bare-board fabrication operations, but its consequences appear strongly during assembly.

The manufacturing chain is:

> **patterned and protected PCB -> exposed copper features -> surface finish -> bare PCB ready for downstream assembly**

A useful final point is that the surface finish does not necessarily remain as an unchanged outer layer after soldering.

At a simplified conceptual level:

- **HASL:** the solder-based finish remelts and mixes with the solder used during assembly;
- **ENIG:** the thin immersion-gold layer dissolves into the molten solder, exposing the nickel layer as the principal soldering interface;
- **OSP:** the organic coating is displaced during soldering so that solder can wet and react with the underlying copper.

The finish influences how the bare PCB behaves when it later encounters:

- solder paste;
- flux;
- reflow heating;
- wave or selective soldering;
- test probes;
- connector contacts;
- storage and handling.

This is why surface finish belongs in a PCB-fabrication chapter even though many of its most visible consequences occur during PCB assembly.

## Key Terms Introduced in This Section

**Surface finish**  
A final metallic or organic coating applied to intentionally exposed PCB copper to protect the surface and support soldering, contact, or another specified function.

**Solderability**  
The ability of a surface to be wetted by solder and participate in formation of the required metallurgical connection.

**Surface planarity**  
The degree to which a finished pad surface is flat and even.

**HASL**  
Hot Air Solder Leveling; a surface-finishing process that coats exposed copper with solder and removes excess solder using hot air.

**Lead-free HASL**  
HASL performed with a lead-free solder alloy.

**ENIG**  
Electroless Nickel / Immersion Gold; a finish using an electroless nickel layer covered by a thin immersion-gold layer.

**OSP**  
Organic Solderability Preservative; a very thin organic coating applied directly to exposed copper to preserve solderability.

**Immersion silver**  
A thin immersion-deposited silver finish applied to exposed copper.

**Immersion tin**  
A thin immersion-deposited tin finish applied to exposed copper.

**Electrolytic nickel/gold**  
An electroplated nickel/gold finish commonly used for selected contact or bonding functions and distinct from ENIG.

## Section Takeaway

A PCB surface finish protects the copper that must remain exposed and usable after bare-board fabrication.

The primary finishes emphasized in this section are:

- **HASL / lead-free HASL:** solder-based, robust, and economical, but generally less planar;
- **ENIG:** very planar and assembly-friendly, but more expensive and process-sensitive;
- **OSP:** very planar and economical, but more sensitive to handling, storage, and repeated thermal exposure.

The important engineering lesson is:

> **No surface finish is universally best. Finish selection depends on solderability, planarity, storage, assembly process, contact or bonding function, inspectability, fabricator capability, and cost.**

Section 2.8 next places surface finishing into the larger manufacturing sequence and follows a rigid multilayer PCB from starting materials through **patterning, lamination, drilling, plating, finishing, profiling, testing, and final inspection**.

The section does not rank one finish as universally superior.
