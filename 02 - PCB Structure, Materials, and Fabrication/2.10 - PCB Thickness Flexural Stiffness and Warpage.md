# 2.10 - PCB Thickness, Flexural Stiffness, and Warpage

Section 2.9 treated board thickness, thickness tolerance, bow, and twist as **manufacturing capability variables**. This section asks a different question:

> **How does the physical construction of the PCB control its resistance to bending and its tendency to warp?**

The first-order answer begins with board thickness. For a simple rectangular section, thickness has a strong cubic influence on bending resistance. A real PCB, however, is not a homogeneous beam. It is a layered structure containing glass-reinforced polymer, copper, plated holes, openings, and local geometry. Its mechanical behavior therefore depends on both **overall geometry** and **how materials are arranged through the stack-up**.

---

## 2.10.1 - Thickness Strongly Affects Bending Stiffness

For a simple first-order bending estimate, a narrow strip of PCB can be idealized as a beam with flexural rigidity

$$
EI
$$

where:

- $E$ is the elastic modulus used by the simplified model;
- $I$ is the area moment of inertia of the cross-section.

For a rectangular cross-section,

$$
I=\frac{bt^3}{12}
$$

where:

- $b$ = board-strip width;
- $t$ = board thickness in the bending direction.

If material, width, span, support, and loading are held the same, then

$$
I \propto t^3
$$

and the first-order bending rigidity also follows the same cubic thickness trend.

| Thickness ratio $t_2/t_1$ | Relative $I_2/I_1$ |
|---:|---:|
| 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 1.25 | 1.95 |
| 1.50 | 3.38 |
| 2.00 | 8.00 |

This does **not** mean that every PCB becomes exactly eight times "stiffer" when its thickness doubles. The relationship is a first-order geometry trend for otherwise comparable structures. It is useful because it shows why modest changes in thickness can produce large changes in bending response.

## 2.10.2 - Worked Example: Comparing Two Board Thicknesses

Consider two simplified rigid PCB strips with the same:

- length;
- width;
- effective material modulus;
- support condition;
- applied load.

Only thickness changes:

- Board A: $t_A=1.2\ \mathrm{mm}$;
- Board B: $t_B=1.8\ \mathrm{mm}$.

For the same width,

$$
\frac{I_B}{I_A}
=
\left(\frac{t_B}{t_A}\right)^3
=
\left(\frac{1.8}{1.2}\right)^3
=
3.375
$$

so Board B has about **3.4 times the first-order flexural rigidity** of Board A under the assumptions of this comparison.

For simple load cases where deflection is inversely proportional to $EI$, Board B would deflect only about

$$
\frac{1}{3.375}\approx0.30
$$

as much as Board A.

The engineering conclusion is more important than the exact number:

> **A 50% increase in thickness can produce a much larger change in bending response than the thickness increase alone suggests.**

This is a geometry comparison, not a complete prediction of real PCB deflection.

---

## 2.10.3 - A Real PCB Is a Layered, Anisotropic Composite

The simple $I=bt^3/12$ model is useful, but a fabricated PCB contains multiple materials and geometric features.

A real board can include:

- glass-reinforced laminate;
- resin-rich bonding regions;
- copper foils and patterned copper;
- plated holes and vias;
- solder mask and surface finish;
- cutouts, slots, and mounting holes;
- local copper planes and sparse routing regions.

The laminate itself is directional, and copper distribution can make the finished board even more direction-dependent. The board should therefore be treated as a **layered, anisotropic composite**, not as one isotropic material with one universal modulus.

Actual flexural response can depend on:

| Factor | Why it matters mechanically |
|---|---|
| Overall thickness | Strong first-order influence on bending rigidity |
| Laminate properties | Changes material resistance to deformation |
| Copper amount and layer location | Changes local stiffness and the through-thickness stiffness distribution |
| Board direction | Glass reinforcement and copper routing can make X- and Y-direction response different |
| Cutouts and slots | Remove load-carrying material and can create local flexibility |
| Support and mounting locations | Change effective span and load path |
| Local components or stiffeners | Can restrain or redistribute board deformation after assembly |

This is why **layer count alone does not determine stiffness**. A thinner multilayer board can be less resistant to bending than a thicker board with fewer copper layers.

---

## 2.10.4 - Bending and Warpage Are Different

**Bending** and **warpage** both produce out-of-plane deformation, but they should not be treated as the same phenomenon.

### Bending from an external load

A board can bend because of:

- handling force;
- connector insertion;
- fixture contact;
- screw or clamp loading;
- vibration or shock;
- component or cable loads.

When the load is removed, much of the elastic deformation may disappear.

### Warpage from internal imbalance

A board can also curve or twist even when no external load is applied.

This type of deformation can result from:

- residual stress from lamination and cure;
- asymmetric stack-up construction;
- unbalanced copper distribution;
- different thermal-expansion behavior among materials;
- nonuniform temperature history;
- repeated fabrication or assembly thermal cycles.

> **Bending describes deformation under load. Warpage describes a loss of flatness that can remain because internal stresses and material imbalance create a net deformation tendency.**

A board can also experience both at the same time.

---

## 2.10.5 - Internal Mismatch Can Become a Net Bending Moment

Section 2.4 introduced coefficient of thermal expansion (CTE) and showed that different PCB materials do not expand by the same amount when temperature changes.

Inside a laminated PCB, layers are bonded together. They cannot all expand and contract freely as separate pieces.

During heating and cooling:

1. each material tends to change dimension according to its own properties;
2. bonding constrains the layers to remain together;
3. constraint creates internal stress;
4. if the stress and stiffness distribution is balanced through the thickness, opposing bending effects can partly cancel;
5. if the construction is strongly unbalanced, the remaining moment can produce curvature or twist.

The critical concept is therefore not simply "different CTE values." It is:

> **Differential thermal strain + mechanical constraint + through-thickness imbalance -> residual stress and possible warpage.**

Through-thickness expansion mismatch remains especially important for plated holes, vias, and interfaces. Global board warpage, however, depends on the combined distribution of thermal strain, stiffness, copper, laminate, and processing history through the board thickness.

---

## 2.10.6 - Stack-Up Symmetry and Copper Balance Reduce Warpage Tendency

A symmetric stack-up does not require every top-side copper feature to be a mirror image of the bottom side.

The practical goal is to avoid large unnecessary imbalance about the board mid-plane.

Useful balance considerations include:

- similar dielectric construction above and below the mid-plane;
- reasonably balanced copper thickness and coverage;
- avoidance of large copper concentration on only one side where it is not functionally required;
- layer placement that does not create a strongly one-sided stiffness distribution.

A balanced construction can still contain internal thermal stress. The important difference is that the resulting bending moments are more likely to oppose one another rather than add in the same direction.

## 2.10.7 - Thermal Exposure Can Change Warpage

PCB warpage is not necessarily fixed at one value for the life of the board.

The board experiences important thermal histories during:

- multilayer lamination and cool-down;
- solder-mask and surface-finish processing where applicable;
- assembly soldering and reflow;
- repair or rework;
- thermal cycling in service.

Temperature changes can alter:

- thermal strain;
- polymer modulus;
- stress distribution;
- local curvature.

This means a board that appears acceptably flat at room temperature can behave differently during a high-temperature assembly cycle.

The detailed material behavior near $T_g$ was introduced in Section 2.4. Detailed assembly thermal profiles belong in Chapter 4. Here, the mechanical lesson is simply that **thermal history and mechanical flatness are coupled**.

---

## 2.10.8 - Bow and Twist Are Different Deformation Shapes

**Warpage** is a broad description of unwanted out-of-plane deformation. Two common geometric forms are **bow** and **twist**.

### Bow

Bow is a smooth curvature of the board. For a rectangular board, the corners can remain approximately in one plane while the center or edges deviate from that plane.

### Twist

Twist is a torsional or diagonal form of deformation. One corner can move out of the plane defined by the other three corners.

These terms describe the **shape of the deformation**, not the root cause. Either form can result from material imbalance, copper imbalance, residual stress, thermal history, or other processing and structural factors.

## 2.10.9 - Why Warpage Matters

Warpage is not only an appearance problem.

It can affect later manufacturing and product integration by changing the physical relationship between the PCB and the surfaces that are supposed to contact or support it.

Important consequences include:

### Assembly

A warped board may not sit uniformly on process tooling or supports. Local height differences can make component placement and solder-joint formation less predictable.

### Connector and enclosure alignment

A warped PCB can make it harder to:

- enter card guides;
- align edge connectors;
- match mounting-hole locations to hardware;
- fit into controlled-clearance enclosure features.

### Solder-joint loading

If a warped board is forced flat during assembly or installation, reaction loads can be transferred into:

- solder joints;
- component terminations;
- connectors;
- mounting points.

### Inspection and test

Flatness affects how consistently a board can contact:

- inspection fixtures;
- support surfaces;
- test probes;
- dimensional-measurement setups.

### Reliability

Warpage can contribute to repeated or residual mechanical loading at interfaces. The detailed fatigue, creep, and structural-reliability analysis is developed later in Chapters 7 and 8.

## 2.10.10 - Engineering Levers for Reducing Warpage Tendency

Warpage control usually begins by reducing the source of imbalance rather than simply trying to make the board harder to bend.

Useful engineering levers can include:

1. **Improve stack-up symmetry.** Balance dielectric construction and layer placement about the mid-plane where practical.
2. **Improve copper balance.** Avoid severe unnecessary differences in copper amount and distribution between opposite sides or regions.
3. **Use nonfunctional copper carefully.** Dummy copper or manufacturing thieving can help balance copper density in some designs, but electrical, thermal, EMI, and fabrication requirements must also be considered.
4. **Adjust core and prepreg thickness distribution.** Dielectric construction can sometimes be changed to improve symmetry without changing every routing layer.
5. **Select suitable laminate systems.** Material modulus, CTE, $T_g$, and process robustness affect thermomechanical behavior.
6. **Control lamination and thermal processing.** Fabrication and assembly process conditions influence residual stress and temperature gradients.
7. **Increase overall thickness when mechanically justified.** A thicker board can reduce curvature and external-load deflection, but it does not remove the underlying material or copper imbalance.
8. **Use process support where appropriate.** Fixtures, carriers, or controlled clamping can reduce temporary handling or assembly deformation, but they do not eliminate residual stress in the board.

The best correction depends on the root cause.

---

## 2.10.11 - Thickness Is a Lever, Not a Universal Fix

Increasing thickness is attractive because the $t^3$ relationship is powerful.

However, a thicker board can also affect:

- via aspect ratio for the same drilled diameter;
- material usage and cost;
- connector and card-guide fit;
- mass;
- stack-up construction;
- local stress transfer after the board is assembled into a constrained system.

A good engineering review therefore asks two separate questions:

> **Is the board stiff enough for the expected mechanical loading?**

and

> **Is the stack-up balanced enough that internal stress does not create unacceptable warpage?**

Those are related questions, but they are not the same question.

---

## Key Terms Introduced in This Section

**Flexural rigidity**  
Resistance of a simplified beam-like structure to bending, commonly represented by $EI$.

**Area moment of inertia $I$**  
A geometric property describing how cross-sectional material is distributed relative to a bending axis. For a rectangular section, $I=bt^3/12$.

**Anisotropic composite**  
A material or structure whose properties depend on direction and on the arrangement of more than one constituent material.

**Residual stress**  
Internal stress that remains in a structure after a manufacturing, thermal, or mechanical process even when no external load is applied.

**Warpage**  
Out-of-plane deformation caused by internal stress, material/structural imbalance, processing history, or a combination of these effects.

**Bow**  
A smooth curvature form of PCB warpage.

**Twist**  
A diagonal or torsional form of PCB warpage in which the corners do not remain coplanar.

**Stack-up symmetry**  
Reasonable balance of the board construction about its mid-plane, including dielectric and copper-layer arrangement.

**Copper balance**  
Management of copper amount and distribution so that the board is not strongly unbalanced across layers, sides, or regions.

---

## Section Takeaway

PCB thickness is a strong first-order mechanical design variable because the area moment of inertia of a rectangular section varies with $t^3$.

The most important ideas are:

- for otherwise comparable geometry and material, increasing thickness can greatly increase bending rigidity;
- layer count alone does not determine stiffness;
- a real PCB is a layered, anisotropic composite, so actual response also depends on laminate properties, copper placement, direction, cutouts, supports, and local geometry;
- external-load bending and internally driven warpage are different phenomena;
- residual thermal and process stresses become more likely to create curvature when the through-thickness construction is strongly unbalanced;
- stack-up symmetry and copper balance reduce warpage tendency by reducing the net bending moment produced by internal mismatch;
- bow and twist are different geometric forms of warpage;
- warpage can interfere with assembly, connector alignment, inspection, system integration, solder-joint loading, and reliability;
- increasing thickness can reduce deformation, but it does not remove the root cause of an unbalanced stack-up.

The same stack-up, material properties, copper distribution, and board thickness that influence mechanical behavior also influence **heat flow**. Section 2.11 therefore shifts from stiffness and warpage to **PCB thermal behavior**.
