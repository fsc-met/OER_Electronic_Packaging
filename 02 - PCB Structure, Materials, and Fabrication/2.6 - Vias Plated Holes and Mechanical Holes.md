# 2.6 - Vias, Plated Holes, and Mechanical Holes

Section 2.5 examined how copper is patterned into traces, pads, planes, annular rings, thermal reliefs, and other conductive features. Many of those features must also connect **through the thickness of the PCB** or coexist with holes needed for components, fasteners, alignment, and mechanical clearance.

This section focuses on those through-thickness features.

The most important distinction is:

> **A hole describes physical geometry. A via describes an interlayer electrical-connection function.**

A plated hole may receive a component lead, act as a via, or serve another purpose. A mechanical hole may be intentionally non-plated, but some mounting or chassis-contact holes may be plated by design.

Understanding these distinctions is practical because hole geometry affects:

- electrical interconnection;
- component and connector fit;
- manufacturing capability;
- plating quality;
- thermal paths;
- mechanical attachment;
- inspection and troubleshooting;
- long-term reliability.

---

## 2.6.1 - Not Every Hole Is a Via

PCB holes can look similar from the outside while serving very different functions.

A useful first classification is shown below.

| Feature | Main purpose | Plated? | Intended to receive a component lead or hardware? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Plated component hole** | Electrical connection and later soldered component/connector attachment | Yes | Usually a component lead or connector pin |
| **Through via** | Electrical interconnection between selected copper layers | Yes | No component lead is normally intended |
| **Blind / buried / microvia** | Interlayer electrical connection without using a full board-thickness through hole | Yes or otherwise metallized as required by the process | No component lead is normally intended |
| **Thermal via** | Promote heat conduction between layers or through the board | Usually metallized | No component lead is normally intended |
| **Mechanical / mounting hole** | Fastener, standoff, clearance, or structural attachment | Often non-plated, but may be plated by design | Hardware rather than an electronic component lead |
| **Tooling / alignment hole** | Fabrication, assembly, or fixture alignment | Commonly non-plated | Tooling or locating pin |
| **Slot / cutout** | Mechanical clearance, keying, connector geometry, or other board-shape requirement | May be plated or non-plated | Depends on function |

The terms therefore should not be collapsed into one category.

For example:

- a plated through-hole used for a resistor lead is a **plated component hole**;
- a small plated hole used only to connect Layer 1 to Layer 4 may be a **via**;
- a hole for a mounting screw may be a **mechanical hole**;
- a routed slot may have no electrical function at all.

## 2.6.2 - Plated Component Holes

A **plated component hole** is a plated through-hole intended to receive a lead or pin during later PCB assembly.

Before assembly, the bare PCB contains:

- the finished hole opening;
- conductive plating on the hole wall;
- annular rings or pads on selected layers;
- electrical connections from the plated barrel to selected copper features.

During assembly, a component lead or connector pin is inserted into the finished hole and may then be soldered or otherwise attached.

### Finished hole size matters

For a component hole, the important dimensional requirement is usually the **finished hole size**, because that is the opening available for the actual lead or pin.

The finished hole must be compatible with:

- the maximum expected lead or pin size;
- lead-shape variation;
- hole-size tolerance;
- plating and surface-finish buildup;
- the intended assembly process.

Too little clearance can make insertion difficult or impossible.

Too much clearance can reduce control of component position or change the intended solder-joint geometry.

Detailed land-pattern and assembly-fit rules belong in Chapters 3 and 4, but the physical relationship is important here:

> **Component fit depends on the finished hole, not simply on the drill tool diameter used earlier in fabrication.**

---

## 2.6.3 - Drill Size, Plating, Finished Hole, and Annular Ring

A plated through-hole does not normally retain the same internal diameter that existed immediately after drilling.

After the hole is drilled, its wall is prepared and metallized. Copper plating on the wall reduces the open diameter.

For a simplified circular hole with approximately uniform metal buildup:

$$
D_f \approx D_d - 2t_p
$$

where:

- $D_f$ is the finished hole diameter;
- $D_d$ is the drilled-hole diameter before wall metallization;
- $t_p$ is the effective metal buildup on one side of the hole wall.

This relationship is an **idealized geometric explanation**, not a fabrication rule.

Actual finished hole size can also be affected by:

- drill-tool tolerance and wear;
- material behavior during drilling;
- plating-thickness variation;
- surface finish or other coating buildup;
- inspection and acceptance requirements.

The fabricator therefore normally selects or compensates the drilling process to achieve the required finished hole.

### Connection to the annular ring

Section 2.5 introduced the **annular ring** as the copper surrounding the hole on a conductive layer.

The complete local structure now consists of:

> **finished hole + plated barrel + layer connection + annular ring**

If the drilled hole is displaced relative to the copper pad because of drill or layer-registration variation, the annular ring can become smaller on one side.

The annular ring therefore provides both:

- conductive area for connection to the plated barrel;
- geometric margin for normal manufacturing variation.

## 2.6.4 - Through Vias

A **through via** is a plated interlayer connection that extends through the full PCB thickness.

A through via may physically resemble a plated component hole, but its function is different:

- it is not normally intended to receive a component lead;
- its primary job is to connect selected copper layers.

The plated barrel may pass through many layers, but it electrically connects only to the layers whose copper features intentionally meet the barrel.

Other layers may include clearance around the via so that no electrical connection is made there.

### Why through vias are common

Through vias are widely used because they can be fabricated using the same general drilling and through-hole metallization concepts used elsewhere in rigid-board fabrication.

However, a through via occupies a hole through the entire board, even when only two layers need to be electrically connected.

For dense designs, that unused passage through other layers can consume routing area. This motivates more localized structures such as blind, buried, and microvias.

---

## 2.6.5 - Blind Vias, Buried Vias, and Microvias

Not every via extends through the entire board.

### Blind via

A **blind via** starts at an outer surface and terminates on one or more internal layers without passing completely through to the opposite surface.

This can preserve routing area on layers that do not need the connection.

### Buried via

A **buried via** connects only internal layers and is not visible from either outside surface of the finished PCB.

Its construction normally requires the via to be created within an internal substructure before the complete multilayer board is laminated.

### Microvia

A **microvia** is a small, shallow interconnection feature associated with high-density interconnect construction.

Microvias are commonly formed by laser-based or other specialized processes rather than conventional full-thickness mechanical drilling.

At this level, the important point is not a specific dimensional definition. It is the manufacturing concept:

> **Microvias provide short, localized layer-to-layer connections that enable higher interconnection density.**

Blind vias, buried vias, and microvias can reduce routing obstruction compared with a full through via, but they usually require more specialized fabrication planning and can increase cost and process complexity.

## 2.6.6 - Via-in-Pad and Filled or Capped Vias

A **via-in-pad** places a via directly within a component pad rather than beside it.

This approach can:

- save routing area;
- shorten the local electrical connection;
- help escape dense component patterns.

However, a via opening located in a solderable pad can also affect solder behavior during assembly.

For that reason, via-in-pad structures may use additional fabrication steps such as filling, capping, or plating over the via opening.

### Filled via

A **filled via** has some or most of the internal void intentionally filled with a selected material.

Depending on the application and fabrication process, the fill may be:

- nonconductive;
- conductive;
- part of a larger planarization process.

### Capped or plated-over via

A **capped** or **plated-over** via includes additional metal over the opening to create a more continuous surface.

The exact process sequence depends on board technology and fabricator capability.

> **Via-in-pad, filled via, and capped/plated-over via are related concepts, but they are not exact synonyms.**

A via can be filled without serving as via-in-pad, and a via-in-pad implementation may require a specific fill/cap process depending on the solderable-surface requirement.

## 2.6.7 - Thermal Vias

A **thermal via** is a via used primarily to improve heat conduction between copper regions on different layers or between opposite sides of the board.

Thermal vias are commonly used in groups or arrays beneath or near heat-generating components.

They can help move heat:

- from a surface pad into internal copper;
- from one side of the PCB to the other;
- toward a larger copper region, thermal interface, heat spreader, or heat sink.

A thermal via is primarily a **functional designation**, not a completely different hole-manufacturing category.

A thermal via may physically be:

- a conventional plated through via;
- a blind or microvia structure in some designs;
- electrically connected to a copper net as well as serving a thermal function.

> **Thermal via and thermal relief are different concepts.**

A thermal relief intentionally restricts lateral heat flow near a soldered pad during assembly. A thermal via intentionally provides a through-thickness heat-conduction path.

Detailed thermal-via sizing, spacing, heat-flow modeling, and system thermal design are developed in Section 2.11 and Chapter 6.

## 2.6.8 - Mechanical Holes, Tooling Holes, Slots, and Cutouts

Not every opening in a PCB has an electrical purpose.

### Mechanical or mounting holes

Mechanical holes may be used for:

- screws;
- standoffs;
- locating features;
- structural attachment;
- enclosure or chassis integration.

Many mounting holes are **non-plated through-holes (NPTHs)**, but this is not universal.

A mounting hole may be intentionally plated when the design requires:

- electrical grounding;
- chassis contact;
- metal reinforcement;
- another product-specific function.

The function therefore determines whether plating is appropriate.

### Tooling and alignment holes

Tooling holes support manufacturing or assembly operations such as:

- panel registration;
- fixture location;
- assembly alignment;
- inspection setup.

Some tooling features exist only on the manufacturing panel and are removed when individual boards are separated.

### Slots and cutouts

A PCB may also include routed slots or cutouts for:

- connector geometry;
- mechanical clearance;
- board keying;
- enclosure features;
- isolation or structural requirements.

Slots and cutouts may be plated or non-plated depending on their intended function and fabrication definition.

Section 2.13 later examines how these mechanical features must be communicated clearly in fabrication data.

## 2.6.9 - Via Aspect Ratio

A plated hole is more difficult to process uniformly as it becomes deeper relative to its diameter.

This relationship is commonly described by the **aspect ratio**.

Conceptually:

$$
\text{Aspect ratio} = \frac{T_b}{D_h}
$$

where:

- $T_b$ is the board or plated-hole depth;
- $D_h$ is the reference hole diameter used for the calculation.

A larger aspect ratio means a relatively deeper and narrower hole.

This can make it more difficult to achieve uniform:

- hole-wall preparation;
- chemical access and exchange;
- metallization;
- copper plating.

As aspect ratio increases, the middle of a deep hole can become more difficult to process consistently than regions near the outer surfaces.

This is one reason via geometry is a **manufacturing-capability issue**, not only a CAD-layout choice.

### A terminology caution

Different fabrication documents or suppliers may use slightly different diameter bases when discussing aspect ratio, such as drill diameter or finished hole diameter.

Therefore:

> **Do not apply one aspect-ratio number without first confirming how the fabricator defines the calculation.**

Section 2.9 develops fabrication capability and tolerance in more detail and discusses why manufacturer-specific capability data are more useful than memorizing one universal number.

## 2.6.10 - Hole Preparation Before Plating

Mechanical drilling through a laminated PCB generates heat and physically cuts through resin, glass reinforcement, copper, and other materials.

This process can leave:

- resin smear on exposed inner-layer copper;
- rough hole walls;
- glass-fiber remnants;
- burrs or debris.

Before reliable metallization can be created, the hole wall must be appropriately cleaned and prepared.

A common manufacturing function is **desmear**, which removes resin smear and conditions the exposed hole wall so the later conductive metallization can contact the intended inner-layer copper.

At the conceptual level, the sequence is:

> **drill -> clean / desmear -> create an initial conductive surface -> build copper plating**

Exact chemistry, equipment, and process sequence vary with material system and board technology.

Those fabrication details belong in Section 2.8.

The important engineering point here is:

> **Good plating begins with a well-formed and well-prepared hole wall.**

## 2.6.11 - Tolerances and Registration

The final geometry of a hole is affected by several independent variations.

Important examples include:

- drilled-hole diameter variation;
- drill positional variation;
- layer-to-layer registration variation;
- plating-thickness variation;
- finished-hole diameter tolerance;
- component-lead or hardware dimensional tolerance.

These variations interact.

For example, a hole can meet its diameter requirement but still be poorly registered to the surrounding annular ring.

Likewise, a component lead can meet its dimensional specification while the combination of lead maximum size and finished-hole minimum size leaves insufficient insertion clearance.

This leads to an important manufacturing principle:

> **Hole design is a tolerance-stack problem, not a single nominal-dimension problem.**

This section introduces that relationship without assigning universal numerical tolerances.

Section 2.9 develops PCB fabrication capability and tolerance in more detail, while Chapter 3 uses those capabilities in DFM decisions.

## 2.6.12 - Hole and Via Quality Affect Reliability

A plated hole or via is a three-dimensional metal structure embedded in a laminated composite.

Its reliability depends on more than whether continuity exists when the bare board is first tested.

Potential quality problems include:

- incomplete or nonuniform copper plating;
- plating voids;
- weak connection between barrel copper and an inner-layer land;
- excessive drill smear;
- rough or damaged hole walls;
- drill or layer misregistration;
- annular-ring breakout;
- cracks in the plated barrel.

Thermal expansion is particularly important.

The dielectric structure generally expands more strongly in the through-thickness direction than the copper barrel. During repeated thermal cycles, this mismatch can strain the plated hole or via.

A poorly formed or insufficiently robust barrel may therefore crack even if the board initially passes electrical continuity testing.

The physical cause-and-effect chain is:

> **hole geometry and preparation -> plating quality -> thermomechanical strain capability -> long-term interconnection reliability**

Detailed defect classification and failure mechanisms are developed in Section 2.12 and Chapter 8.

## Key Terms Introduced in This Section

**Plated through-hole (PTH)**  
A through-hole whose wall contains conductive metal so that electrical connection can be made between conductive features on different PCB layers.

**Plated component hole**  
A plated through-hole intended to receive a component lead or connector pin during later assembly.

**Via**  
An interlayer electrical connection formed by a metallized hole or related conductive structure and normally not intended to receive a component lead.

**Through via**  
A via that extends through the full PCB thickness.

**Blind via**  
A via that begins at an outside surface and terminates on one or more internal layers without reaching the opposite outer surface.

**Buried via**  
A via that connects only internal layers and is not visible from either outside surface of the finished board.

**Microvia**  
A small, shallow interconnection feature associated with high-density interconnect construction and commonly formed by specialized processes such as laser drilling.

**Via-in-pad**  
A via placed directly within a component pad.

**Filled via**  
A via whose internal void is intentionally filled with a selected material.

**Capped / plated-over via**  
A via with additional metal covering the opening to create a more continuous surface.

**Thermal via**  
A via used primarily to improve through-thickness heat conduction between copper regions.

**Non-plated through-hole (NPTH)**  
A through-hole whose wall does not contain conductive plating.

**Aspect ratio**  
The ratio of plated-hole depth or board thickness to a specified hole diameter.

**Desmear**  
A hole-preparation process used to remove resin smear created during drilling and expose/condition surfaces for later metallization.

## Section Takeaway

PCB holes and vias must be interpreted by both **structure and function**.

The most important distinctions are:

- **plated component hole -> plated opening intended for later lead or pin attachment;**
- **through via -> full-thickness interlayer electrical connection;**
- **blind / buried / microvia -> more localized electrical connections used to preserve routing area and increase interconnection density;**
- **via-in-pad / filled / capped via -> specialized constructions used when geometry or surface requirements demand additional processing;**
- **thermal via -> interlayer connection used primarily to promote heat conduction;**
- **mechanical / tooling hole -> primarily structural or manufacturing function rather than circuit interconnection;**
- **drill size, plating, finished hole, annular ring, aspect ratio, and registration -> coupled manufacturing variables rather than independent dimensions;**
- **hole preparation and plating quality -> major contributors to plated-hole and via reliability.**

The key idea is that a via is not simply a small hole. It is a **manufactured three-dimensional interconnection whose geometry, plating, and relationship to the PCB stack-up determine how reliably it performs**.

Section 2.7 next examines the surfaces left exposed after the copper and hole structures are fabricated: **PCB surface finishes and the tradeoffs among common finish types**.
